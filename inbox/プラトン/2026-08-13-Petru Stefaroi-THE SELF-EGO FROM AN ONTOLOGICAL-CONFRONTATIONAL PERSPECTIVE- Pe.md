Abstract: The article "THE SELF FROM AN ONTOLOGICAL-
CONFRONTATIONAL PERSPECTIVE" proposes a radical re-evaluation of
the Self, rejecting the traditional view (from Plato to Descartes) that
defines it as a static structure destined for harmony. Instead, it argues that
the Self is, fundamentally, an agonistic structure and the central onto-
formation of the Personal Ontos. The core thesis asserts that tension
and confrontation are not a pathology, but the active ontological
principle of being, the necessary engine for development and
ontological actualization (Ontification). The Self functions as a dynamic
stabilizer that does not seek peace, but rather the agonistic
equilibrium—that is, the highest level of productive tensiune it can
manage—thereby avoiding stagnation and psychic entropy. This process

is described by five essential properties (Emergence, Transmergence,
Sinmergence, Convergence, Telegence) which define how the Self is
born and operates. The being of the Self is achieved through continuous
"agonistic persistence," engaging in an essential four-way struggle with
the other onto-formations: the Organism (the struggle for mastera over
facticity), the Mind (the struggle for decisional autonomy), the
Soul/Affectivity (the struggle for the modulation of passions), and the
Moral Conscience (the struggle for ethical authenticity against norms).
Consequently, Personality is redefined as a dynamic synthesis, a
repertoire of acquired tensional-resilient strategies; Personhood becomes
the socio-axiological subject whose moral identity is earned through
confrontation and responsible assumption; and the Person's Conduct is
the operational and visible expression of this internal tensional
equilibrium, reflecting the Self's ability to transform opposition and
confrontation into effective action.

1. Introduction

The Self (Zu-J has profiled itself for millennia as a central enigma, an
inexhaustible subject that transcends the boundaries of philosophical,

psychological, and anthropological reflection, being, in its essence, the
measure and the mystery of being. For centuries, the major philosophical
tradition exhibited a clear predilection for stability, unity, and certainty.
The quest was for a stable, static, and essentialist Self—whether it was
defined as a unique and indubitable thinking substance (Descartes), or as
a universal and legislative moral consciousness (Kant). In this dominant
paradigm, which postulated a finality of interior harmony and rational
tranquility, confrontation and tension were inevitably viewed as a defect,
a pathology, or a deviation that had to be either subordinated to reason
or eliminated through asceticism. This classical vision of the Self,
however, ultimately failed to explain the radical and often contradictory
dynamism of subjective experience.

However, existential experience and new philosophical orientations
impose the radical necessity of a rupture from this unilateral and
idealistic vision. The present work addresses the scientific and
philosophical community with the proposal of a new analytical
framework, one that is radically dynamic and tensional. The central
question guiding this endeavor is: What if the Self is not destined to be
peaceful or unified by definition, but is, structurally, a field of permanent
tension and an agonistic nucleus—a fundamentally conflictual
structure? The essence of the Self, we demonstrate, is not static, but
dynamic and strained, operating as the central onto-formation of the
Personal Ontos—that primordial and fundamental space where
consciousness not only resides but also clashes head-on with interior and
exterior reality. This thesis shifts the center of gravity from what the Self
is, to how the Self constructs itself in real-time through resistance and
dynamic processes.

Here, in this internal space of maximum density, where being unfolds,
lived experiences (following phenomenology), complex thoughts within
the cognitive structure, contradictory emotions in the affective sphere,
and the pressures of the socio-cultural environment do not merely coexist
passively. They intersect violently, entering into a constant and
fundamental tension that defines the existential condition itself. This
tension (Kierkegaard's Anxiety) is not a dysfunction or a simple
psychological failure. On the contrary, it is, as we will argue in detail, the
driving force that generates essential confrontations necessary for
dynamic existence and for the ontification of the subject in the face of
its inherent limitations. Without this internal tensional energy,
development would cease, giving way to entropy and psychic stagnation.

The Ontological-Confrontational Perspective, which we propose in
this work, surpasses frameworks that treat confrontation as a mere
psychological or sociological consequence. Our thesis asserts that the
Self is, structurally and functionally, a structure of resistance, and that
internal and relational confrontations (with the Organism, Mind, Soul,
and Conscience) constitute the active ontological principle of identity.

The essential tension between the Self and alterity is the necessary basis
for its formation, functioning, and full becoming.

Starting from this radical premise, the article explores and expands upon
the implications of this unique vision, answering the fundamental
question: How exactly does this ontological-confrontational perspective
influence the formation of Personality, Personhood, and Conduct,
compelling us to view them not as results of pre-existing harmony, but as
dynamic, ever-provisional syntheses resulting directly from
confrontation? This analysis leads us to the NECESSITY of accepting a
new interpretation, establishing an imperative for the re-evaluation, from
the perspective of the Personal Ontos, of the entire set of concepts
studied, under the title: THE SELF FROM AN ONTOLOGICAL-
CONFRONTATIONAL PERSPECTIVE: Personality, Personhood, and
the Person's Conduct - Towards a New Interpretation? The present
work aims to launch and substantiate this new thesis into the space of
philosophical and scientific debate. To fully grasp the nature of this
rupture and establish our starting point, we must first briefly review how
the Self was conceived and defined in the traditional view.

2. Historical Markers of the Concept of the Self.
The Traditional, Ontological-Static, and
Structural Perspective on the Self

The history of the Self is a long narrative spanning thousands of years, a
perpetual oscillation between sought stability and conflictual dynamism, a
journey that begins with Greek metaphysics. In Antiquity, Plato
configured the soul as a site of ideal balance between the three parts
(rational, spirited, and appetitive), where reason was supposed to
dominate the passions, establishing a normative model of internal
harmony that heavily influenced Western thought. Subsequently,
Aristotle defined the soul as the form of the body (entelechia), an
actualization of potential, yet maintaining a teleological vision of the
human being, whose final realization implied a certain virtuous stability.

In the Hellenistic period, this search for tranquility intensified as a
response to social instability. Stoics, such as Epictetus and Marcus
Aurelius, emphasized reason as the supreme tool for controlling emotions
(apatheia). Epicurus sought inner peace (ataraxia) by eliminating fear and
pain, both visions aiming at the pacification of internal tensions as the
supreme goal of a successful life.

This tendency toward stability culminated in the Modern Era, becoming
the backdrop for the traditional ontological perspective. René Descartes
radicalized subjectivism, reducing the Self to the Cogito, the unique
"thinking substance." John Locke, although shifting the focus from

substance to consciousness given by memory and experience, maintained
the necessity of a smooth continuity of the self. David Hume, though a
critic of the idea of a self-substance, remained within the idea that the
Self is a bundle of perceptions, still seeking a coherence among them.

Subsequently, Immanuel Kant structured the Self as a transcendental
instance (the J-think), a necessary but purely formal condition of all
possible knowledge, denying it substantial empirical content but
affirming its functional, unitary necessity. German Idealism, with Fichte
and Schelling, took this autonomy to the extreme, viewing the Absolute
Self as the source of all reality, a vision that absorbs any conflict into its
totalizing system.

The true turning point, however, came with the destructive and creative
force of thinkers who recognized conflict as the engine of existence.
Arthur Schopenhauer, through his concept of blind and irrational Will
manifesting in every being, anticipated humanity's constant conflict with
an uncontrollable inner force, the primordial source of suffering. S@ren
Kierkegaard, the father of Existentialism, described the Self as a
tensional synthesis between finite and infinite, temporal and eternal,
defined by anxiety and desperate choice, a permanent inner struggle.

Friedrich Nietzsche, celebrating Dionysian forces, identified in the Will
to Power a force for overcoming and growth through resistance and self-
sacrifice, glorifying tension as the source of value and the basis of his
Ubermensch concept. Freudian psychoanalysis demolished the
Cartesian unity, revealing an Ego (Fu) trapped in a constant struggle
between the drives of the Id (instincts) and the censorship of the
Superego (morality), a structure conflictual by definition. Carl Jung
added the tension between the conscious and the collective unconscious,
defining the Self through the unification of opposing archetypes. These
currents, from psychoanalysis to existentialism, elevated conflict from a
mere pathology to an essential dimension, but one still predominantly
psychological or part of the traditional, non-confrontational ontology,
which this article challenges.

The Ontological-Confrontational Perspective, which constitutes the
central thesis of this work, affirms, in a radical and unequivocal manner,
that the Self is not a subject meant to be harmonious, seeking tranquility.
It is, structurally, a fundamental agonistic structure and an entity of
Emergence. Here, internal and external confrontations are not fleeting
events, the result of unfavorable circumstances, but constitutive and
inherent elements of its identity structure. In this sense, Conflict,
confrontation is not just a reality, but the active ontological principle
of being, not a simple secondary effect or a manufacturing defect. The
Self functions as a dynamic and intense field of forces, a permanent
chessboard, in which different instances (impulses, reason, norms)
simultaneously attract and repel one another. This game of attractions
and repulsions generates, paradoxically, cohesion and the vital energy

necessary for action and survival. This vision resonates strongly with
Hegelian Dialectics, where progress and becoming are impossible
without the Thesis-Antithesis tension, a tension that our Self continuously
experiences and synthesizes through an uninterrupted dynamic
movement, although we do not accept a final and static synthesis, like the
Absolute Spirit. The connection is direct also with post-Hegelian
Existentialism: Jean-Paul Sartre sees the relationship with Alterity (The
Other) as a permanent struggle for recognition and objectification—"hell
is other people." Maurice Merleau-Ponty described the subject as a
tension between the Lived Body (Leib) and consciousness, a struggle that
we subsume to internal confrontations, emphasizing that there is no
consciousness without its tensional body. The contemporary philosopher
Paul Ricoeur emphasized the self that defines itself through commitment
in the face of alterity, an eminently tensional and narrative relationship,
defined by ipse (self-identity) versus idem (sameness identity).

To fully ground this thesis in an original framework, we must now move
from the critiques of the traditional view to the concrete definition of the
Self's nature, establishing it as the core entity of the human being.
Therefore, the next section will detail our proposed Ontological-
Confrontational and Dynamic Perspective on the Self and define the
Self as the central onto-formation of the Personal Ontos.

3. An Ontological-Confrontational and Dynamic
Perspective on the Self. The Self - Central Onto-
Formation of the Personal Ontos

In striking contrast to the traditional vision of major philosophy, which
has postulated for millennia a stable, essentialist, and predestined
harmonious Self—following the thread of thinking from the Cartesian
substance to the Kantian legislative reason—this work launches and
substantiates a radically new framework of analysis: the Ontological-
Confrontational Perspective. The fundamental premise guiding the
entire endeavor is that the Self is not conceived to be peaceful or unified.
On the contrary, it is, in its very internal structure, a field of permanent
tension and an agonistic nucleus. Therefore, the essence of the Self is
not static, but intrinsically dynamic and strained, imposing itself as the
central onto-formation of a broader and deeper framework: the
Personal Ontos.

The Personal Ontos is not a mere psychological instance, but represents
the fundamental ontological framework, the primordial space of being
where consciousness manifests as an active presence. It is an
inexhaustible reservoir of ceaseless tension and potential. In this internal
space of maximum density, where being unfolds, the Self does not just
passively reside, but clashes head-on with internal and external reality.

Lived experiences (following phenomenology), complex thoughts within
the cognitive structure, often contradictory emotions in the affective
sphere, and the relentless pressures of the socio-cultural environment do
nothing but violently intersect, generating a constant tension that
defines the existential condition itself.

This tension, similar to the Anxiety described by Kierkegaard, must not
be interpreted as a dysfunction, a pathology, or a simple psychological
failure. On the contrary, it is, in our view, the driving force generating
essential confrontations, absolutely necessary for maintaining a
dynamic existence and for the realization of the ontification of the
subject in the face of its inherent limitations. Without this kinetic energy,
generated by opposing forces, development would abruptly cease, giving
way to inevitable entropy and psychic stagnation. Thus, this perspective
surpasses those frameworks that treat conflict as a mere consequence,
elevating it to the rank of a principle.

Our thesis asserts, in a radical and unequivocal manner, that the Self is
structurally and functionally a structure of resistance, and that internal
and relational confrontations are not simple passing events, but
constitute the active ontological principle of identity. The essential
tension between the Self and alterity is the necessary, unconditioned
basis for its full formation and becoming. Conflict is not a defect, but a
sine qua non condition of existence. The Self functions as an intense
and dynamic field of forces, similar to Hegelian Dialectics (Thesis-
Antithesis), where progress is impossible without tension. The Self
continuously experiences and synthesizes this uninterrupted dynamic
movement, although, essentially, we reject the idea of a final and static
synthesis. Ultimately, through this Ontological-Confrontational
framework, the Self assumes the role of the subject that self-creates
through friction and opposition, transforming the raw energy of struggle
into action and meaning, thereby ensuring a positive role for conflict and
becoming the source of its own freedom.

To understand exactly how this central onto-formation manages and
transforms these opposing forces into functional cohesion and the engine
of development, it is essential to define the constitutive mechanisms that
enable it to operate this way. Thus, in the section immediately following,
we will explore in detail those Properties and Laws fundamental to
governing how the Self forms, subsists, and functions within the Personal
Ontos.

4. Properties and Laws of the Self in the Process
of Formation, Being, and Functioning from an
Ontological-Confrontational Perspective

The Self, understood as a dynamic structure generated by conflict,
exhibits five fundamental properties that define its mode of constitution
and functioning within the Personal Ontos—properties that reveal its
complex and non-linear nature. These properties function as constitutive
laws, not just as accidental descriptions.

The first of these is Emergence, through which the Self appears as a
new, irreducible quality, generated directly from the multiple tensions
(biologic, social, psychic) acting upon the subject. This concept finds
strong analogies in Edgar Morin's Theory of Complex Systems, where
superior structures are born from the chaotic interaction of inferior
components. The Self is more than the simple sum of its conflicting parts;
it is a new level of organization.

Next is Transmergence, its unique capacity to be a functional mediator
that traverses and integrates the diverse levels of being, from corporality
(somatic) to moral consciousness (normative), functioning as a tensional
liaison between the strata of being. This property ensures functional unity
and integrity despite the structural diversity and opposing forces it hosts.

The third is Sinmergence (Co-mergence), which represents its inherent
co-emergence with the environment and Alterity, emphasizing that the
Self is not an isolated monolith (anti-Cartesianism), but is always a
situated and embodied, interconnected subject. The Self cannot develop
in isolation, but only through its conflictual and necessary interaction
with the world and with others, confirming the theses of symbolic
interactionism and pragmatism.

The fourth is Convergence (Conmergenta), which describes the network
of internal tensional-functional interconnections, showing that the Self is
not coherent through a lack of conflict, but through the active
management and orchestration of these tensions. It maintains an
agonistic and dynamic equilibrium, a concept that resonates with the
ideas of Pragmatism, especially those of John Dewey, about experience as
a process of active adjustment to the environment based on problem-
solving, but also with the Theory of Complex Thinking.

Finally, Telegence (Telegenta) is the property of proactive and purpose-
driven orientation towards the future (telos), in which the Self utilizes
tension (the gap between what is currently and what ought to be in the
future) as a vector that propels it toward possibility. This property is
essential for freedom and is similar to the concept of the project in
Existentialism.

These properties impose a clear Ontological Law, which is the operating
principle of the Ontos: Conflict and Confrontation are not a
pathology or a defect, but a sine qua non condition of being and
development. This view is supported by Developmental Psychology
(see Erik Erikson), which views identity crises as nodal points necessary
for maturation. Attempting to annul conflict means, in fact, annulling the
possibility of growth and becoming, condemning the subject to stagnation
and symbolic death. A fundamental understanding of these properties is
indispensable for grasping the subsequent stages of the Self's
development. Therefore, the next section will explore how the Self, driven
by these constitutive laws, forms its structure through a process marked
by layered and necessary crises—the Formation of the Self from an
Ontological-Confrontational Perspective.

5. The Formation of the Self from an Ontological-
Confrontational Perspective

The formation of the Self is a layered and agonistic process that
transforms initial interactions and primary tensions into a complex
internal structure, a veritable Bildung marked by crises and temporary
resolutions of tension. This process is one of continuous metamorphosis,
not governed by linear and definitive stages.

The process begins with the Contact Stage, where the incipient Self
perceives the primary tension between its inherent biological needs
(hunger, safety) and the limits imposed by the external environment,
realizing that the world is not immediately satisfying. This first necessary
and inevitable frustration, as Donald Winnicott might have argued with
his concept of the "good enough environment"—which must partially fail
in the immediate satisfaction of desire—is the initial condition for the
development of the Self.

Next is Acquisition, where the selective internalization of norms,
language, and cultural values occurs. This process immediately
intensifies, through Accumulation, internal contradictions (e.g., the
opposition between personal desire and internalized parental norm or
social imperative), amplifying the tensional field. This accumulation of
contradictions is not an accident, but the fuel source for subsequent
development, creating an internal zone of proximal development.

Structuring and Configuring mark the moment when the Self
organizes and gives form to these accumulated forces. Structuring
creates a functional framework for tension management, defining defense
mechanisms and adaptation strategies. Configuring defines the
individual's confrontational style—that is, their preferred ways of
approaching the struggle, whether to avoid or engage—a concept close to
Alfred Adler's idea of the style of life, but also to Pierre Bourdieu's

concept of habitus, as a generative structure of social and psychic
practices.

Subsequently, Institution takes place, a process by which the agonistic
structure is consolidated, transforming conflict from a series of events
isolated into a stable organizational principle of personality, an active and
accepted principle of functioning. The Self becomes expert in managing
its own tensions. The final stage of becoming is Ontification, when the
Self attains ontological maturity. It becomes an active and self-regulated
presence, capable not only of enduring confrontation, but also of
deliberately generating it (by taking on limit-exceeding projects), in order
to permanently reaffirm itself and validate the meaning of its struggle—
an act of auto-poiesis at the ontological level, similar to the ideas of
Humberto Maturana and Francisco Varela, a continuous process of
system self-creation through self-referentiality.

Once the process of formation is understood, it is necessary to analyze
the environment in which the Self exists and validates itself. This
environment is not passive, but a constant battleground. The next section
will detail the nature of these fundamental conflicts, establishing the Self
as the Field of Internal and Relational Ontological Confrontations.

6. The Self - Field of Internal and Relational
Ontological Confrontations

The Self acts as a total and complex agonistic field, being engaged in
permanent conflict on three inter-connected levels: internal,
intersubjective, and social. Each tension is vital for maintaining its
functional energy and for its authentic manifestation in the world.

On the internal level, the Self is caught in the middle of its own system.
It confronts itself in a continuous struggle with the Organism—a
confrontation with the facticity of the body (illness, fatigue, instincts,
death) in which the Self fights for autonomy and self-mastery,
attempting to transcend the mere somatic state and impose rational will.
This is a dichotomy intensely explored by the Philosophy of the Body
(Drew Leder) and phenomenology, which emphasizes the opposition
between the body-object and the lived body.

There is then the tension between the Self and the Mind (Cognition), a
fine struggle between the rational plan of the Self, which coldly analyzes,
plans, and calculates risks and logic, on the one hand, and the urgency of
action, impulse, or immediate intuition, on the other. This is where the
stake of self-control and decisional freedom is played, a classic
problem in the psychology of the will.

The Soul (Affectivity) represents another internal and often the
strongest adversary, being the source of overwhelming emotions
(passion, fear, anger) that threaten the decisional autonomy of the Self.
This is a central theme in Spinoza and Hume, but also in modern
psychology of emotions, which sees them as tendencies to action that
conflict with the conscious plans of the Self. Here, the Self negotiates
between submission to feeling and the imposition of will, fighting to
transform raw affects into organized sentiments and directed motivation.

Last but not least, the Moral Conscience (Superego) imposes the
struggle with internalized norms (the Freudian Superego, or Lawrence
Kohlberg's "inner voice"), generating feelings of guilt, obligation, or
shame. The Self must negotiate between these instances, and
Observable Behavior thus becomes the dynamic and visible result of all
these opposing forces that temporarily balance each other—an ever-
provisional tensional synthesis.

On the external level, confrontations extend to the intersubjective
level. The Self constantly negotiates with Internalized Alterity—the
voices of authority, parental expectations, and cultural models (e.g.,
George Herbert Mead spoke of the Significant Other and the Generalized
Other)—an essential confrontation with the "other within us" for defining
authenticity and avoiding Heideggerian inauthenticity. Finally, the Self
directly confronts Social Alterity—the World, society, the economic
environment, institutions, and dominant culture, which are major sources
of constraints and normative expectations. The Self must adapt, fight for
resources, power, or recognition (as in Hegel's Master-Slave dialectic, or
Jurgen Habermas's social critique regarding the system and the
lifeworld). The relationship is one of permanent negotiation and
reaffirmation of limits. This perspective aligns with Erving Goffman's
sociology, which views social life as a series of tensional-negotiated
performances on the public stage.

Having established the nature of these constitutive struggles, the
following section will address how the Self actively subsists and
maintains itself within this environment of permanent conflict. We will
define the Being and Functioning of the Self as Part of the Personal
Ontos, focusing on the concept of agonistic persistence.

7. The Being and Functioning of the Self as Part
of the Personal Ontos

The being of the Self is not a state of passive rest, but manifests as an
active and continuous process of agonistic persistence—the
fundamental act of existing through conflict, which is its source of
functional energy. In this theoretical framework, the Self does not pursue
absolute peace or static tranquility, an ideal rejected by our thesis, but,

on the contrary, seeks the highest level of productive tension it can
manage. Only in this tensional zone, situated on the border between
equilibrium and disorganization (similar to the concept of the edge of
chaos in complexity theory), can the Self self-actualize and develop—a
concept that recalls Abraham Maslow's ideas about the fulfillment of
potential, but viewed through the lens of struggle and sustained effort as
a method to overcome the resistance of the world and its own inertia.

Within the Personal Ontos, the Self is, by definition, the center
generator of tension. It does not function merely as a passive mediator
between drives, but initiates necessary confrontations, questioning its
own beliefs to prevent stagnation and avoid entering psychic entropy—a
state of disorganization lacking purpose or meaning (a problem analyzed
by Viktor Frankl). Its essential role is that of a dynamic stabilizer that
maintains the functional cohesion of the Ontos, integrating the other
instances (Organism, Mind, Soul, Moral Conscience) through the
continuous flow of confrontation and temporary resolution. This dynamic
of tensions maintains the system of being in an always unstable, yet
functional and vibrant equilibrium.

The Self thus ensures, through its confrontational function, that the
Personal Ontos does not become blocked in any definitive synthesis,
remaining open to the future (Telegence). By deliberately taking on new
conflicts (e.g., assuming a new difficult social role), the Self reconfirms its
agency and its role as an active subject. Without this ontological
struggle, the Personal Ontos would become a disorganized collection of
impulses and norms, losing its capacity to project a unitary meaning and
give direction. Thus, the functioning of the Self within the Ontos is
governed by a law of self-maintenance through conflict, in which
every overcome confrontation solidifies and extends the boundaries of the
Personal Ontos, conferring upon it a deep structural resilience and a
richer narrative identity.

Having established the functioning mechanisms of the Self, the final
analytical step is to explore how this agonistic structure manifests
outwardly. The next section will synthesize the entire argument, showing
the direct consequences of the Ontological-Confrontational perspective
on the definition of Personality, Personhood, and the Person's
Conduct.

8. The Self from an Ontological-Confrontational
Perspective, Personality, Personhood, and the
Person's Conduct

Our narrative culminates in the definition of the Self not as a static or
harmoniously conceived structure, as the philosophical tradition from

Plato to Descartes suggested, but as a fundamental agonistic structure.
The Self is, in the Ontological-Confrontational view, a central onto-
formation and the pulsating nucleus of the Personal Ontos, that
essential space where internal and external confrontations are not
accidents, but constitutive principles and the driving force of being.
Tension is not a pathology, but a sine qua non condition of dynamic
existence. The Self functions as a dynamic field of forces, similar to
Hegelian dialectics, where the opposition between it and the other onto-
formations of the Personal Ontos generates vital energy and cohesion.
The being of the Self is a continuous agonistic persistence, seeking not
peace, but the highest level of manageable productive tension, an always
unstable equilibrium necessary for ontification.

This approach is validated by the analysis of how the Self tensionally
interacts with the other onto-formations of the Personal Ontos. The Self
engages, first, in a struggle with the Organism, the confrontation with
its biological facticity: instincts, physical limitations, illness, and
fragility. Through this effort to transcend the factual, to impose will over
the simple somatic state (e.g., disciplining the body), the Self obtains
self-mastery, and success in this struggle decisively contributes to the
Personality's character, establishing how resilient or disciplined the
individual will be. Another internal struggle unfolds with the Mind
(Cognition), where tension arises between the rational plan of the Self—
the one that coldly analyzes, plans, and calculates risks—and the
automatism of thought, cognitive routines, or the urgency of logical
impulses. The Self fights here for self-control and decisional
autonomy, and the result of this confrontation directly shapes the
Personality traits related to conscientiousness and the capacity for
reflection.

Furthermore, the confrontation with the Soul (Affectivity) is often the
most dramatic, involving the force of overwhelming passions and
emotions (fear, anger, love) that threaten the Self's autonomy. Here, the
Self negotiates between submission to feeling and the imposition of will,
fighting to transform raw affects into organized sentiments and directed
motivation. The way the Self manages this tension determines the
Personality's temperament and its level of emotional stability, directly
influencing Personhood by defining its ethical sensitivity. Finally, the
