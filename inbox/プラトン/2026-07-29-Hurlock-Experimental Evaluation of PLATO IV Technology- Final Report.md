SUMMARY
Probl a Pu

This subproject was conducted as part of Education and Training
Development, ZPNO7 (formerly 43-03X) and was designed (1) to test the
feasibility of new advances in computer-based training (CBT) systems,
and (2) to apply new CBT methodologies to skills training. The sub-
project was jointly sponsored by the Defense Advanced Research Projects
Agency. Objectives of the research were (1) to evaluate the capabilities
and special features of the PLATO IV CBT System (2) to develop and
evaluate CBT strategies and techniques for training on-the-job skills,
and (3) to determine the applicability of PLATO IV to such skills train-
ing.

Approach

Experimental studies designed to evaluate PLATO features and CRT tech-
noiogy were conducted in a variety of Navy training applications. Training
tasks for this evaluation included multimeter operation, oscilloscope
operation, operation of S-3A copilot Integrated Control System (INCOS)
panel, remedial mathematics, and recipe conversion. Computer-based train-
ing (CBT) techn‘ques investigated included use of still, animated and
interactive graphics, simulation, drill and practice, feedback in the
context of games, guidance of offline instruction, learner control of
instruction, and use of preprogrammed instructional strategies ("“driver"™
less« ., for efficient course development. Data were obtained which pro-
vided estimates of training effectiveness and efficiency and cost effec-
tiveness for both course materials development and course delivery.

Findings and Conclusions

l. The PLATO IV System is one of the most advanced and flexible CBI
systems available operationally today for storing, controlling, and pre-
senting training materials. Its instructional effectiveness for delivering
all types and kinds of training is restricted only by the imagination and
skill of instructional designers using the system. Its great capability
may be a disadvantage because of the expensive CPU required: a large
number of terminels must be utilized to decrease cost. If terminals are
remotely located, communication and maintenance costs increase signifi-
cantly.

2. The most useful terminal features are the plasma screen touch
panel, and keyboard; the audio device and slide selector were judged the
least useful.

3. PLATO ‘V is capable of providing instruction across a wide varicty
of Navy trainiwm. Seven different training applications were demonstrated
in the experimental studies. It was found to be highly effective for
training conceptual and cognitive skills and on-the-job skills involving

Recomm endations

1. Purchase and operational vlementation of a large PLATO-type
system should not be done at this time, because: (1) maximum cost bene-
fits require operation at full capacity, (2) data on potential cost savings
in specific applications are not available, and (3) the capabilities
and sts of PLATO should be compared to those of competitive CBT systems

before making a purchase decision (pp 52, 57, 58, 65).

2. Lease of time from the University of Illinois PLATO System to run
small units of terminals should not be done without a careful analysis
of mputing requirements for the particular applications under considera-

tion. This is because limits on the use of extended core storage (ECS)
festrict the capability for individualized training and prohibit complex
part-task simulation applications. Leasing time from other private PLATO
venders may avoid ECS problems but may be accompanied by higher delivery
osts. Maximum benefits would again depend upon full utilization of
terninals and avoidance of excessive communication costs. When only a
few terminals are required or needed, small scale systems or stand-alone
terminals may be more cost effective (pp. 42, 43, 58).

3. wo approaches are suggested in order to assess the proper role

i systems in Navy training. First, an analysis should be made of
the potential for Navy CBT applications and their respective cost et ffec-
tiveness. It was outside the scope of this project to do the necessary

needs analysis that would make it possible to identify specific Navy
training needs and their current and/or anticipated costs. This information
is required before it is possible to make recommendations on the opera-
tional utility and potential cost and training benefits that may be achieved
using a large CBT system.

4. Second, additional R&D is needed to determine the kinds of skills
that can best be taught using this methodology, especially potential cost
effective part-task training applications using interactive graphic simula-
tions, and to isolate necessary instructional elements that yield effective
CBT.

INTRODUCTION
Problem

The Navy's support to the development of this subproject was in response
to the requirements given in Advanced Development Objective (ADO) P4303
which states, "Required levels of personnel readiness and capability are
highly dependent upon Navy education and training programs. Significant
improvements in these programs must be achieved to meet increasing demands
of future naval operations." An objective of the ADO is to test the
feasibility of advances in training technology as a means of providing
increased personnei readiness. To meet the ADO requirements, the objectives
of this subproject were: (1) to test the feasibility of new advances in
computer-based training (CBT) systems and techniques for providing part
or all of modularized, individualized training programs, and (2) to apply
new computer-based methodologies to skills training, including operator
and maintenance training. The subproject is part of Advanced Develop-
ment Project, Education and Training Development, ZPNO7 (formerly P4303X).

Purpose

The Experimental Evaluation of PLATO IV Technology effort at the
Navy Personnel Research and Development Center (NAVPERSRANDCEN) combined
the evaluation of special capabilities of the PLATC IY system with the
development and evaluation of CBT strategies and techniques for on-the-job
skills. The purpoec of the final report is to bring together the experi-
ences and findirgs of the work efforts (eight studies) conducted on the
project. Attention is given to summarizing the research studies and to
drawing conclusions and making recommendations about instructional effec-
tiveness, range of training applications, usefulness of special features
and characteristics, implementation problems, and instructional development
and delivery cost of the PLATO IV system for military training.

Background

This subproject on the Experimental Evaluation of PLATO IV Technology
was conducted by NAVPERSRANDCEN, San Diego, and was jointly sponsored
by the Defense Advanced Research Projects Agency (ARPA). A principal
focus of the ARPA/Joint Services Training Technology Program is the de-
velopment of computer-based training technology for DoD-wide application.
The four objectives of the ARPA program are:

1. To provide advances in technology required to permit development
and use of advanced training and educational delivery systems in the
Armed Forces.

2. To provide research in learning behavior needed to set requirements
for delivery systems, course development, and the production of course
materials.

3. To facilitate the transfer of advanced training technology and
research results to potential users and research agencies in the several
Services.

4. To encourage and facilitate rvoperation between the Services in
research, development, and operationul use of advanced training and
educational delivery systems.

Research on PLATO IV conducted at the Service sites was intended:

1. To assess the training efficiency of the PLATO IV system on a variety .
of DoD curriculum pacxages.

2. To provid» measures of PLATO reliability and operating costs.

3. To determine costs, problems and optimal methods for programming
curricuium materials in PLATO IV format.

4. To assess the relative effectiveness of the TUTOR programming
languege.

5. To provide a basis of comparison with minicomputer CAI systems.

6. To acquaint operational training personnel with the capabilities and
limitations of this advanced technology.

7. To provide Service R&D personnel with an advanced state-of-the-art
system for CAI research.

8. To evaluate the general capability of the PLATO IV systen.

ARPA support to NAVPERSRANDCEN covered installation, hook-up, com-
munications, and maintenance of 12 PLATO terminals.! Through the coopera-
tion of the Service School Command, Naval Training Center, San Diego,
space was provided for the terminals in the Technical Training Building
(Building #94, Room 344) which gave convenient access to trainees in a
variety of Navy Schools.

1other Navy organizations which were part of the ARPA joint-supported
PLATO IV program included the Office of Naval Research and the Naval
Training Equipment Center.

It is important to point out that the project was a research and
development effcrt:

1. Studies were planned, developed, and performed as experiments.
Although internal comparisons of computer-based training materials were
often made against conventional instruction, the purpuse was not to prove
that CBT using PLATO IV was superior or better. The relative superiority
of CBT over class instruction has been amply demonstrated (e.g., Ford
and Slough, 1970; Hurlock, 1972; Ford, Slough and Hurlock, 1972). CBT
has also been shewn to produce faster learning than self-paced instruction
(Army Final Report, 1975). Comparisons were made to determine where
and how particular PLATO IV applications could be successfully used in
operational Navy training.

2. The tryout and revision cycles necessary to develop CBT materials
which are instructionally more effective (improve performance significantly)
and efficient (yield significant trainiig time savings) than conventional
meth-ls (Tord, Slough, and Hurlock, 19° 2) were not a procedural step
4acluded in .he PLATO experiments.

3. The effort was not intended is a prototype test bed to evaluate
operational trainii.c capability or cost for a large integrated course
of instruction. Sucl, 2 test bei evaluation was conducted by the Army
(Evaluation of the PLATO LV System, 1975). The present project evaluated
effectiveness, problems, and optimal methods in using PLATO in a variety
of operational settings.

SUMMARIES OF RESEARCH STUDIES

This section presents a brief summary of each of the eight research
studies performed at NAVPERSRANDCEN, San Diego. The purpose of these
summaries is to enable the reader to quickly learn the rationale, research
design, results, findings, and conclusions of each study. References
provided in each summary indicate source documents which contain more
detailed information; for example, a separate NAVPERSRANDCEN Technical
Report has been or will be published on each study.

The reader is cautioned that each study was designed and executed
as a research and development (R&D) effort to answer specific questions
concerning PLATO applications and not as an attempt to develop instruc-
tional course material solely to evaluate operational effectiveness.
Research findings reported in each summary are, therefore, specific only
to that particular study. Generalizations across studies have been made
in later sections, especially in the final section on conclusions and
recommendations.

Later sections cover special information obtained and collected on
each study. Topics include: (1) descriptions of method used and effort
involved in course material development, (2) evaluation of PLATO features
and capabilities, and (3) analysis of cost of developing and delivering
instruction.

Study One: Remedial Math

Reference: McCann, P. H. Training mathematics skills with games.
San Diego: Navy Personnel Research and Development Center, Technical
Report 75-28, April 1975.

t. Purpose/Rationale: The goal of this study was to test the efficiency
of the games preseated by the PLATO IV System in remedial mathematics
training for Basic Electricity/Electronics (BE/E) School trainees. The
idea was to create an intrinsic motivation in remedial students during
practice to improve their mathematics skills by making moves of a game

a part of their feedback.

It. Research Design

A. Course Description

1. Subject Matter Area: Basic algebra formula manipulation,
powers-of-ten scieatific notation and computational skills.

2. Major Training lasks: (a) Correctly add, subtract, multiply,
divide, square, and extract square roots of numbers expressed as powers
of ten, and (b) transpose formulas with correct placement of decimal point;
identify and use correct metric prefixes.

3. Number of Student Contact Hours Developed: 3 hours.

B. Experimental Design

1. Design: Each student took two CAI lessons presenting practice
on different kinds of math and received a different kind of feedback (con-
ventional, convent*onal plus game #1 play move, or conventional plus game
#2 play move) during each lesson.

2. Independent Variables: Three kinds of feedback.

3. Dependent Variables: Lesson time, test score, and attitude
questionnaire responses.

4. Subjects: 48 BE/E School students requiring remediation in
mathematics.

Discussion of PLATO Features and Capabilities

Stimulus Presentations

The plasma screen is the outstanding feature of the PLATO terminal.
Its capabilities for displays of all kinds, including modifiable graphics,
were extensively used in the materials developed in this project. The
ease with which displays may be coordinated with touch panel inputs makes
the screen very useful for simulations of many kinds of interactive tasks.
The speed cf writing is too slow for animation involving large or complex
graphics, although methods for greatly speeding up display presentations
are under development. Displays are highly readable, without any tiring
flicker. The principal advantage of che plasma screen from an economic
point of view is that displays do not need to be refreshed, making dispersal
of terminals feasible.

The microfiche projection system is unsatisfactory in its present
form. Its chief advantage over an external projector would seem to be
the coordination of the back projected images with plasma screen writing:
This was attempted in the oscilloscope studies with poor results. Align-
ment of images and screen displays was very difficult and varied from
copy to copy of the microfiche. In addition, screen writing overlaid
on images was extremely difficult to read. Attempts to use rear projected
images of job aid cards containing interpolation scales--lines, marks,
and numbers--were unsuccessful in the Recipe Conversion study. Graphics
and hardcopy handouts had to be used instead. Quality of images was often
poor, even with very good original photographs, because of the several
stages required in production of microfiche. Time required to produce
and revise microfiche was much longer than for comparable screen material.
Use of the image projector requires a compressed air supply.

The random-access audio unit was not experimentally evaluated in
this project. It also requires an air supply.

Interactive Capabilities

The keyboard was judged good by most users. It was easy to use
and had very few maintenance problems. The special function keys ("help,"
“data,” etc.) were liked by some authors but were not used by all. They
are not essential and require the coder to have a knowledge of the special
branching logic iavolved in their use.

The touch panel was quite popular. It considerably augmented
PLATO's simulation capabilities, as described previously, and was often
used in place of or as an alternative to alphanumerics and function keys
for multiple choice responding and branching. The best example of its
use was in the Part Task Trainer study where sets of push button switches
of the S-3A copilot INCOS panel were graphically simulated. Use of th
touch panel to interact with the display closely approximated the perceptual-
motor response required with actual gear.

Course Management

PLATO facilities for this are very good. The router lesson and
other features provide control and monitoring of student progress. Standard
student performance data are readily kept, and the system has ample cap-
abilities for collection and analysis of more extensive data required for
formative evaluation and revision of new courses. The system is also easily
capable of management of off-line instruction, although special input/output
equipment would be required for large-scale applications.

Course Preparation

TUTOR is a very powerful language and was found to be quite useful
for the varied and often complex applications studied in this project.
Coding of simpler types of instruction is straightforward. TUTOR's chief
drawback is that it contains too many unnecessary complexities (variable
formatting, etc.) and requires extensive experience for a coder to become
expert. For this reason the most cost effective approach to course prepara-
tion is to separate the authoring and coding functions, with separate
personnel assigned to each task.

Although comparisons with other systems are difficult because each
system has special advantages, the PLATO editing system was judged to be
excellent in comparison to most current (line-editing) systems. PLATO
has many special features, such as functions to build up graphic displays,
material location functions, and error detection capabilities.

Driver lessons, using a preplanned strategy for development and
presentation of instruction, were shown to be readily implementable on
PLATO. Such driver lessons are an important way of reducing course develop-
ment costs.

The hardcopy device is just about essential for developing and
administering a course. It was heavily used in this project for materials
development and debugging, documentation of course content, and for student
records and performance data.

System Capacity

The PLATO system is designed to handle a very large number of
students. The current system at the University of Illinois has two million
words of extended core storage and about 950 terminals. System "overhead"
requires 400,000 words of ECS, so there is an average of abowt 1700 words
of ECS available per terminal. Since the average hour of on-line instruc-
tion requires about 4000 to 10,000 words, it is clear that efficient use
of terminals requires that several students be on a lesson simultaneously.
A problem with a large system is that there may be little courseware avail-
able initially, so that it cannot be expected to be cost effective unless
used over a period of several years.

The use of highly adaptive, self-paced CBI imposes severe limita-
tions ~ the number of terminals that the system can handle. It was pre-
vious. shown (Table 4) that the simulation lessons required about twice
as much ECS as nonsimulation lessons. An extreme example of the limitation
on terminal usage can be illustrated for the Navy's Site 24 on the PLATO
IV System consisting of 16 terminals; a total base ECS of 24,600 words
(approximately 1530 words per terminal in student mode) is allocated.

The INCOS lessons for part-task training on the S-3A copilot panel require
that six lesson files be available to each student in a student-controlled,
self-paced learning condition. Each lesson was originally developed with

an average size of 8000 words. Professional programmer/coders on the

INCOS project have recently judged that the lessons cannot be reduced below
6000 words to function; two new lesson files would have to be added.

This would still mean that when only four students are each receiving train-
ing on different INCOS lessons, the other 12 (75%) terminals in the site
are rendered functionless for use by other students.

The driver lesson, "learner," designed by NAVPERSRANDCEN researchers
to facilitate lesson development of CBI, offers another example of this
problem. The driver requires approximately 1500 words of ECS. This is
the base ECS allocation before adding instructional content. At best the
minimum size lesson would be at least 3000 or 4000 words in length. This
means that use of the driver reduces terminal utilization by 50% when
students are scattered on different lessons.

Reliability

The amount of system downtime during prime time was very small,
despite the fact that the system was undergoing experimental development.
System crashes while running students were usually short, the worst problem
being the loss of student data.

Terminal reliability was satisfactory. Much of the downtime was
due to design/production faults which have since been corrected.

Transmission line errors, due to the long distance between the
University of Illinois and San Diego, were a continual source of annoyance
for authors and students, although they normally did not seriously interfere
with use of the terminals. Occasionally, however, transmission was so
bad that some of the terminals were unusable.

PLATO COST ANALYSIS

Two cost questions are of major importance in the evaluation of any
instructional delivery system: (1) How much does it cost to develop instruc-
tional materials for the system? and (2) How much does it cost to present
the materials on the system to students?

Instructional Materials Development Cost

The studies conducted in this project previded information on the
relative importance of factors determining course development costs.
The project was not designed to provide direct estimates of costs to the
Navy of developing operational materials for a "PLATO-like system."
To evaluate the cost of developing instructional materials for operational
training applications in the Navy, certain requirements must be met:

1. Materials must be designed for real training, i.e., either as a
supplement or as a replacement for an actual existing or proposed curri-
culum.

2. Materials must be developed under the same type of conditions and
with the same personnel that would be expected to be used in an operational
situation.

3. Sufficient quantities or amounts of materials should be developed
across a wide variety of training application areas to provide for generali-
zations of findings.
 openliiianas
4. Accurate records must be available for the various types of man-
hours of efforts required to develop and prepare the instructional materials.

Materials developed for the project were experimental and designed
only for the purpose of testing specific research questions. Instruc-
tional materials which were developed were not designed as a prototype
or test-bed for cperational evaluation. Research personnel developed
the materials. Although the range of subject matter areas, Navy training
applications, was broad (see Table 12), the quantities of CBI materials

An example which illustrates the wide differences in effort expended
across different studies, subject matter areas, instructional strategies,
and performance requirements developed by research personnel in the
NAVPERSRANDCEN PLATO Evaluation Project is tne Recipe Conversion Study.

Here CBI materials were developed with an effort of only 77 man-hours per
student contact hour. With only 4 hours of CBI, experimental students
scored as well as classroom controls who had received 4 days (28 hours)

of recipe conversion instruction. Another example is the Part-Task Training
Study where 3 student contact hours of instruction averaged 480 man-hours

of effort per hour to develop. Copilots using PLATO only 3 hours performed
faster and more effectively than controls who studied a workbook 8 hours

and spent 1 hour practicing in a $250/hour Position Trainer. Both studies
demonstrate possibilities for cost-effective CBI training in: (1) recipe
conversion with Mess Management Specialist enlisted personnel because

of the relatively small man effort required to develop the CBI and the

large savings accured in training time (4 hours versus 23 hours); and

(2) part-task training on the S-3A Copilot INCOS panel because CBI, though
developed at a high cost, was less expensive than conventional copilot .
training, saved valuable training time (3 hours versus 8 hours), and offered
a means of freeing an expensive high-fidelity simulator for more complex

and advanced training use.

An accepted method of expressing one-time cost for hardware which
has a useful life of more than 1 year is to figure cost per year based
on its estimated life: Cost per Year = one-time cost/years of life. This
method is also referred to as amortization of cost.

Hardware costs are also frequently expressed as cost per hour or
cost per student contact hour. This method of determining and reporting
cost is one of the most important in the analysis of true cost effective-
ness of an instructional delivery system. It is a cost estimate which
must always be carefully determined and analyzed when making implementation
decisions. The actual number of hours that students are expected to use
the terminals each year for training is the base of the analysis for cost
per student contact hour. In other words, whenever an hourly cost estimate
is given, it implies that the system can deliver instruction at a certain
cost per hour. The estimate is not meaningful, however, unless information
is also provided which tells how many hours per year students must use
the system in order to achieve that cost. A common base used for multi-
media systems, such as computer-based instructional systems, that have
the capability and capacity for large-scale utilization is 2000 student
contact hours per year per terminal. This base assumes that each terminal
is used 8 hours per dzy, 5 days per week, 50 weeks per year (8 x 5 x 50
= 2000 student contact hours per year per terminal).

CONCLUSIONS

Experimental Findings

1. Remedial Math Study: PLATO i: ‘eally suited for contingency
management of learning and practice during training using the wide variety
of games available on the system as reinforcers. The use of games as
part of feedback confirmation to math practice problems positively in-
fluenced student attitudes toward training.

2. Oscilloscope Simulation Study: PLATO successfully taught cognitive
skills of voltage and frequency measurement during training on oscilloscope
operation using simulation. Training on perceptual-motor skills, e.g.,
ones requiring skiiled manual manipulation, were the least effectively
taught. Training using computer-based simulation can greatly reduce the
amount of hands-on training needed.

3. Sine-Ratio Study: Graphics were not found to enhance learning
of verbally-loaded concepts.

4. Multimeter Simulation Study: PLATO graphics and TUTOR language
were highly successful for programming dynamic and interactive simulations
of the multimeter. Simulation may offer an instructional technique for
reducing cost of training and training equipment.

5. Learner Control Study: The use of a preprogrammed, "universal"
driver to control lesson strategy can eliminate a major part of the task
of CBI lesson preparation and offers a significant method of saving both
author training and lesson preparacion time.

6. Recipe Conversion Study: Student-controlled individualized CBI
in nontechnical Navy training can be used to markedly decrease training
time without degrading the performance of trainees with below average
academic skills. Low aptitude students do not appear to have difficulty
in using the PLATO terminal.

7. Oscilloscope Guidance Study: Computer guidance of hands-on
training is an effective instructional procedure and can reduce or elimin-
ate instructor based tutoring. Part-task procedural training seems to
be superior to whoie-task training, if the part task is an actual com-
ponent of the criterion task.

8. Part Task Trainer Study: Part-task training of complex procedural
skills via computer-based simulation, i.e., interactive graphics and touch
panel response mode of PLATO IV, is technically, operationally, and eco-
nomically feasible. It can be used to (a) reduce instructor time, (b)
reduce student training time, (c) increase performance, and (d) substitute
for expensive one-of-a-kind simulators in application areas where initial
training is expensive, e.g., flight training.

Instructional Fffectiveness

Previous research has already decisively demonstrated the instructional
effectiveness of CBI over conventional training methods, especially class-
room instruction. It has shown that CBI can produce significant increases
in performance and decreases in training time, when the instructional
materials have been carefully planned, developed, trial tested, and re-
vised. The reason for the success of CBI is because this automated instruc-
tional method is capable of incorporating the necessary laws and principles
of learning into the instructiona! environment for the student.

The PLATO IV System is a CBI system for storing, controlling, and
presenting training materials. It appears to be one of the most advanced
and fiexible systems in the state-of-the-art. Experience during this
project leads to the conclusion that the instructional effectiveness of
PLATO IV is restricted only by the imagination and skill of the instruc-
tional designers using the system. This conclusion is not intended to
imply that other CBI systems are not or cannot be instructionally effective
for certain types of training. -It merely points out the great flexibility
and capability of PLATO IV for effectively delivering training and instruc-
tion.

A final conclusion on the instructional effectiveness of PLATO IV
as a delivery system for CBI is difficult because, during the evaluation
of the system, decisions regarding its operations have been under the
control of the University of Illinois. The system, as currently configured
with 950 terminals and a base ECS allocation of 1500 words per terminal
in student mode, may not be operationally useful for self-paced training.
There is considerable question as to whether lessons which are indivi-
dualized, adaptive, self-paced, and which may or may not use interactive
graphic routines for simulation can be developed with 1500 words or less.
If they cannot, then the ECS allocation must be increased and corresponding
adjustments must be made in the system configuration, i.e., the number
of terminals must be decreased. A configuration of 500 terminals may
be more operationally feasible.

Training Applications

PLATO IV is capable of providing instruction across a wide spectrum
of Navy training. Over seven unique application areas are represented
in this effort; subject matter ranged from mathematics, recipe conversion,
simulation of electronic test equipment and the S-3A copilot INCOS panel,
to social training. Students used in the studies included recruits with
below and above average aptitudes, recruit company commanders, and officers.

PLATO IV is highly effective for training conceptual and cognitive
skills. Because of its interactive capability, students can practice
on-the-job skills involving sequential and procedural learning tasks.
Perceptual-motor responses are less effectively taught using simulation

because of the lack of response fidelity, e.g., learning to manipulate
an oscilloscope probe by looking at graphics and reading text is not as
effective as learning a complex switch pressing sequence by touching
graphic displays of the switches.

One of the most innovative PLATO IV training applications is the use
of its programmable interactive graphic displays for simulation. S<veral
examples are: (1) simulation of operational equipment, gear, and hardware
for training operator and maintenance skills; (2) simulation of weapon
systems and tactical situations for individual or team training; and (3)
simulation of interpersonal situations for training social interactions
and behavior. In each case, PLATO can integrate a training function and
provide interactive responding, feedback, guidance, management, and per-
formance measurement and evaluation.

PLATO Features

The most useful features of the terminal were found to be the plasma
screen, the touch panel, and the keyboard. These three features provided
an ample capability for stimulus displays, student responding, and course
sequencing. The image projector was of very limited usefulness because
of problems in alignment, readability, and preparation of microfiches.
The audio capability mzy only be needed for highly specialized training
requirements.

The system is especially well suited for training tasks involving
extensive student interactions, such as test equipment operation and part~
task simulation triining. Valuabie features include the graphics cap-
ability, the touch panel, and the broad usefulness of the TUTOR language.

User acceptance was very good. Authors particularly mentioned the
plasma screen, the touch panel, TUTOR, and user services and aids.
Students gave high ratings to all features of instruction except the
quality of the photographs shown on the image projector.

Editing and coding capabilities were judged to be very good. TUTOR
is a very powerful CAI language with extensive uses for displays, response
processing, and course control. Since complex applications require an
experienced coder, it is recommended that authoring and coding functions
be done by separate personnel. The hardcopy device is practically es-
sential for efficient editing, course documentation, and student data
analysis.

PLATO is a very large system. This means it has excellent capabilities
for data management, course management, and collection of data for course
evaluation and modification. It also means that a lot of course material
is required if the system is to be used efficiently, i.e., enough to keep
250 to 1000 terminals busy. Finally, if most applications utilize highly
interactive, adaptive training, there will be a restriction on the number
of terminals imposed by the high ECS requirements. A system configured
for 2,000,000 words of ECS could probably support a maximum of 500 terminals.

The system was quite reliable during the project, with very few
“crashes.” Terminals were also judged to have satisfactory reliability,
particularly later versions. Line errors, due to long-distance trans-
mission, were a continual annoyance and were most troublesome when dis-
playing graphics. Reliability of the hardcopy device was very poor.

CBI Material Development Method and Effort

The use of a team may be the most efficient and effective method for
developing CBI lesson materials. A minimum team divides the authoring
and programming/coding activities; this allows each team member to spe-
clalize. Extensive experience is required for a coder to become an expert
in the use of TUTOR. An author must (1) be familiar with the subject
matter, and (2) be able to determine training objectives, design instruc-
tional strategies, and apply principles of learning; and know the cap-
abilities of the CBI system being employed.

Driver lessons, using preprogrammed logics for development and pre-
sentation of instruction, are readily implementable on PLATO IV. Drivers
offer a method for reducing lesson development effort.

The most important factors influencing man-hours of effort in the
development of lesson courseware are: (1) the experience and expertise
of personnel employed, (2) the degree of individualization and student
interaction involved, (3) the level and complexity of the subject matter
content, and (4) the kinds of text and graphic presentation and types
of response modes required.

Man-hours of effort required to develop a student hour of instruction
varied by a factor of seven between the experimentally developed lessons
of the eight research studies in this project. With a range of from 80
to over 550, a mean of 315 man-hours were expended per lesson hour. This
figure closely approximated the 284 man-hours reported by the Army during
their PLATO project. A conservative estimate of the time required to
develop one student hour of instruction on the PLATO IV System is, there-
fore, probably somewhere between 200 and 490 man-hours.

CBI Development Cost

The PLATO IV System compares well with other CBI systems and multimedia
methods with regard to the cost of development of instructional materials.

Development costs decrease as the number of hours of instruction in-
creases: (1) It usually takes longer to develop the first or early hours
of a course, and (2) Cost effectiveness, resulting from training time
savings over conventional instruction, may not occur until several hours
of adaptive, self-paced CBI are used in a course.

If a large number of courses and hours of instruction were going to
be implemented on a PLATO IV type delivery system, cost could be minimized
by organizing and maintaining an experienced and specialized team of

authors, programmer/coders, and instructional technologists to develop
and/or assist in the development of instructional materials. ;

Assuming that an average of 200 to 400 man-hours of effort is required
for the development of each hour of instruction and that each man-hour
cost $8.00, the development cost would be between $1600 and $3200. If
the course were to be used by 500 students each year for 10 years, the
development cost per student contact hour would range from 32 to 64 cents.

RECOMMENDATIONS

There are two possibilities for implementation of training using
a PLATO system. One is to purchase a system; the other is to lease time
from the University of Illiancis or from another PLATO installation.

1. The purchase of a PLATO system cannot be recommended at this time.
It is not known if there is sufficient need for the type of training
provided by an interactive CBT system to justify the use of a large PLATO-
type system. Such a system must be operated at full capacity in order
to provide maximum cost benefits. Also the relative cost effectiveness
of CBT and other methods of instruction will vary widely from applica-
tion to application, and data on these costs are not readily available.
Finally, many of the capabilities needed for effective CBT development,
delivery, and evaluation are available on other CBT systems. The capa-
bilities and costs of PLATO should be compared to those of competitive
CBT systems before making a purchase decision.

2. Leasing of time on a PLATO system has drawbacks. The University
of Illinois system has so many terminals that it is difficult to provide
enough ECS to allow adequate individualization of instruction. This is
particularly true for complex part-task simulations with high ECS require-
ments. Leasing of time from a private vendor may cost more. One reason
for this is that the University of Illinois project has been partly sub-
sidized by research funds. In any case, potential users should keep in
mind that maximum cost benefits can only be realized if terminals are
fully utilized and are located close enough to the computer system to

avoid excessive communication costs. Where only a few terminals are needed,
small systems and/or stand alone terminals may be more cost effective.

3. In order to assess the proper role of CBT systems in Navy training,
two approaches are suggested. First, an analysis should be made of the
potential for Navy CET applications and their cost effectiveness in com-
parison to other trairzing options. It was outside the scope of this
project to do the necessary needs analysis that would make it possible
to identify specific Navy training needs and their current and/or anti-
cipated costs. This information is required before it is possible to
make recommendations on the operational utility and potential cost and
training benefits that may be achieved usiiug a large CBT system. If there
is a high requirement or demand for CBT, then a large system may be more
cost effective than the use of many smaller systems (Kribs, 1976). A
large CBT system would be most useful in an area such as San Diego where
a large variety of users could access the system and communication costs
could be minimized. A needs analysis should be made within the next few
years. Otherwise there is a risk of the proliferation of many small
systems by individual training groups, without an overall Navy plan for
introducing CBT on the most cost effective basis.

4, Second, because of the unusually successful applications of
interactive computer graphics to simulate operational equipment and to
provide part-task training, it is recommended that this methodology be
considered as a cost effective training supplement or substitute for

expensive high fidelity simulators or trainers. Additional R&D is es-
sential to determine the kinds of skills that can be best trained using
this methodology and to isolate the necessary instructional elements which
must be present to yield effective training using CBT simulations.
