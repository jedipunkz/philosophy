I descended to the banks of Mother Shyok. The path down was rugged and hard-going, with little to
recommend it. As I went further, I passed the dismal jacals of the indigenes, which were themselves eclipsed
by the loft eyries of the limitanei.

It had been the time for fasting and abstinence, with only unseasoned porridge for sustenance.
Practice had been observed strictly by all, excepting the usual burghers. Now, however, had begun the holy
season of rejoining. The high tensions, almost strictly inevitable after such austerity, were displaced by high
spirits — excepting, of course, the usual burghers, whose sour mood was fixed by their ambitions and
superiors.

I decided to rest when I reached the valley. Shyok was energetic, and her cool waters stripped the
mineral dross from my feet. A voice called out for me not to leave; I had made no motion to do so. It was
Judoc, the former cancellarius of Ladakh. He handed me a warm cup of tea, whose bitterness had been
tempered by rich butter. I told him how very glad I was to see him, and how fortunate it was that his was the
first voice I had recognized today. He returned my courtesy, with some addition.

“Sten, you absolutely must come with us to tonight’s opera. The archon spared no expense for its
preparation. I know that he brought in the foremost troupe from the Valley of Flowers; the women are
devilishly beautiful and the men as lithe as panthers.” My senses had been starved of much color and noise,
and I told him so.

“Besides, it’s my last season in town. [ll be leaving as soon as there’s ice in Shyok.” I asked him why
he planned to leave so shortly after his retirement, but he brushed my question aside and badgered me for my
assent, which I had never withheld.

We made for a yogurt stall. No payment was required. As the sun fell, the unmistakable toll of brass
clashing against brass was heard from the plaza, calling us to our seats. I sat next to a grim youth, whose
brilliant habit was overtaken by russet stains. Judoc thrust his arm over my chest and shook the boy’s hand
eagerly. I caught nothing of what he said, though I correctly assumed it was an invitation. The youth nodded
slowly without lifting his hood. Whispering to me, Judoc confessed that the man was our carnifex, and that
he was out of work during the jubilee. I had no objections to his company, I replied truthfully. Judoc flashed
a nervous smile nonetheless.

The show was rich and vibrant, as promised. The masked women were gowned in bright silk of
changing shapes, and the acrobats spirited. I stepped away during the morality play, searching for our local
delicacy of fried cheese and sugar. The message of the play was Manichean, a doctrine that I understood well
but outgrew in youth. As I returned, the opera neared its end. Judoc and I walked to his Awstz, the carnifex
following a few paces behind. It was almost compline when we reached the house.

The cataphract Philotimos was pacing under the pergola, dimly lit by torches. I had last seen him on
campaign in Siachen decades ago, joining battles with thin air and disabling incandescence. His cropped hair
was now quicksilver, and his damask skin had fractured further. He was one of the chbinarii, and we both
remembered well how his heavy armor — so useful against the mountain cold — effectively seared him by
the snow’s reflection. Repeated encounters with the violet array had not been kind to him.

I took fast strides to say hello. His posture straightened upon greeting us. He had perfected the art of
bearing martial airs, despite how much retirement atrophied the practice. I nearly believed his self-
assuredness.

We three were led into the perfumed atrium, which carried an ornate cupola above. The carnifex and
I both paused to stare upwards at the illustration, lit by some unknown source. The scene was pastoral,
scatred by an act of divine violence. It imparted a chill unease. I turned away. The young man looked up

rapturously, unblinking. Philotimos either did not see it, owing to pains in his neck, or else had seen it before.
He and Judoc were speaking of mundane affairs as I rejoined them. The house eunuchs ushered us guests
into the andron and served a strong barleywine. Judoc returned shortly in a fine samite gown. The four of us
drank slowly in relative calm for a watch, although the carnifex drank with noted urgency. He had again
pulled his hood down after it fell in the atrium.

“Well, my friends, it wouldn’t do for us to revel like mute symposiasts. As I am your host, I order
you to set a theme for tonight’s conversation!” It was plain that Judoc spoke out of kinship, but plainer still
that he spoke as one accustomed to having his orders followed. Perhaps it was this natural authority that
compelled Philotimos’ reply. I myself had grown unused to wakefulness at this hour; the young man had
fixed his gaze on some new ftesco-secco.

“You aged lech! We all” — he paused to appraise the carnifex — “know well what satisfies you.
Catamites!”’ Philotimos issued a low chuckle after his own comment. Judoc looked down and tugged at his
