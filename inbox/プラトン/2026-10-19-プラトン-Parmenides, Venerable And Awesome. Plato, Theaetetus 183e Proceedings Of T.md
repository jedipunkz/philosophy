Foreword

Although Parmenides probably wrote as early as the beginning
of the 5th century Bce, his work was still in circulation some
eleven centuries later, even if it was considered “a rarity” (omévwv)
by then according to Simplicius (6th century AD).' Since then,
the text of Parmenides, like most of the treatises written in
ancient times, has been on the list of works irredeemably lost.
For several centuries, the only access to Parmenides' philosophy
lay in the rare references to his work preserved in those of other
philosophers or ancient authors, references that were sometimes
accompanied by textual quotations (which lets us assume that
these authors had a copy of the original text of Parmenides’
Poem at hand).

The practice was eventually abandoned when, by the end
of the 16th century, philologists like Henri Estienne and Julius
Caesar Scaliger began to reconstruct the lost text through a
meticulous search and compilation of the quotations spread
throughout classical treatises. T'his process arguably culmi-
nated in 1835, when Simon Karsten succeeded in gathering
nineteen passages (one of them in Latin) from the lost original
to publish the most complete reconstruction. We now call this
set of 152 verses "Parmenides Poem.” Thanks to the efforts
of these scholars, researchers now have an approximate (given
its fragmentary nature) but faithful (given its literal character
backed-up by the work of philologists and codicologists) version
of the original poem.

Since then, we've been able to confirm that the adjectives
"venerable and awesome,” used by Plato (Theaetetus, 183e) to

Thousands of works have been dedicated to the study of the

been made in "cleansing" the text of impurities and erroneous
readings by the original sources, errors which were passed down

which has been considered “orthodox” ever since. However, even
Diels' cleverness did not prevent him from introducing new
anomalies to the misreadings transmitted by tradition.?

may have occupied in the original. The present arrangement,

text continues, studies about the ideas conveyed by Parmenides

Part I: On Parmenides

Existence and Essence in Parmenides
Scott Austin

Summary

Parmenides’ absolute monism puts existence and essence into an
absolutely monistic Being as it joins levels in an ontological hierarchy that
other philosophers, from the Neoplatonists through Hegel, were later
to separate. The result is a fusion of presentation and representation, a
fusion not teased apart until the twentieth century.

In this offering, I would like to suggest that Parmenides’ phi-
losophy and methodology already contain, in a focused way,
many crucial elements which later philosophers merely set out
and distinguish from one another. I shall concentrate on the
question of existence and essence; the questions of negation
and necessity; and on features of Eleatic methodology which
become features of later ontology. First, a comparison between
Parmenides and Aquinas on the question of necessity.

In Aquinas, the distinction between contingent and neces-
sary beings can be phrased in terms of the distinction between
existence and essence. In a contingent being, we have existence
separate from essence, a kind of boundary. In a necessary being,
we have no separation between existence and essence, together
with unboundedness. And Aquinas reads Exodus, “I Am Who
Am,” as meaning that God is the being which is What Is, i.e.,
the being in Whom existence and essence are one: I am the One
Who Is. Parmenidean Being is also a being which is its essence.
Its essence, as logical subject, is simply what is: it is the é6v: for it

to be is for it to be ungenerable, unperishing, whole, and so on.
This is why the subject is a participialized version of the copula.

Being is; it has no other essence than to be in certain ways,
ways expressive of complete being outside of time, in uniformity
and completeness. Being as essence is simply what it is to be
what is. This is why we do not find the copula directly negated
in a description of Being in fragment 8 of Parmenides’ poem,
although we do find negated predicates; to negate the copula
would be to destroy the essence, to make it impossible to intro-
duce a transcendental predicate, to bring it about that what is is
not, either globally or in some particular respect.

Another way to approach Parmenidean Being is to notice
that, in it, source and archetype coincide. In a contingent hier-
archical series, the cause of the series, the reason for its being, is
not necessarily the same as the archetypal member of the series,
the embodiment of its essence. The prototype is not necessarily
its own cause. But Aquinas’ God is both prototype, the essence
in which all beings participate, and cause, the only being which
is unlimited being. Similarly with Parmenides: there is no dif-
ference between Being, as a proper name, and What-Is, since
for it to be is for it to be what it is.

Finally—and this is a difference between Parmenides and
Aquinas—Parmenidean Being is also the only member of the
series, the only thing that participates in being, whereas, for
Aquinas, the whole order of created natures consists in beings
which, deriving their being from God, are nevertheless distinct
from God. Thus Parmenidean Being is the only embodiment,
the only particularization, of the essence which it is.

Parmenidean Being is, then, what Aquinas’ God would be
if it were the only being in existence. It collapses metaphysical
distinctions within itself just as Aquinas’ God does, and also
collapses the distinction between God and creation. The result
is a being which, as logical subject, simply is a participialized
version of the copula, and whose predicates all express totality
in Being's possession of its own essence. And all of this follows
from Being’s necessity—in Aquinas, expressed by Being’s infin-
ity, in Parmenides by its finitude or boundedness.

Existence and Essence in Parmenides

Parmenidean Being is also a collapsed version of the three
hypostases of Neoplatonism. For, I take it, the One, the first
hypostasis, is the source in the series of realities, Mind, the second,
is the archetype which has all the intelligibles, all the Platonic
Forms, within it; and Soul, expressing the relationship between
Mind and the One as Mind returns to the One, is the participant
in the archetype as caused by the source, the hypostasis which puts
the many intelligibles into time one by one. Collapse these three
principles into an ontological monism, and you have Parmenidean
Being as I have just described it. It is the reason why it is, the
source; it is the ultimate essence, the archetype; and it is the only
being which has being, the only being which particularizes the
archetype. This, again, is simply a consequence of monism, for
when I say, for example, “What-is is whole,” I name a subject
which is the participialization of the copula; I connect it, using
the copula, with a predicate; and I also express its essence using
that predicate—and yet all three of these roles are one.

There are also other distinctions that are collapsed in the
absolute monism of fragment 8. The universe of the goddess,
besides being a universe with only one member, is also a universe
without contingency: Being is necessary, but it is also the only
being which is possible, so, in a sense, whatever is possible is
also necessary, if one leaves out of consideration for a moment
the haphazard universe of mortal opinion. Thus there are only
four possible modal propositions, propositions which I take to be
expressed by the modal metaphors in the poem, and also by the
pervasive metaphors of fetters, chains, and boundedness. First,
it is not possible for Being to alter in time: Justice does not allow
it to come to be or to perish. Second, it is necessary that it not
alter in time: True Trust drove off coming-to-be and perishing.
Third, it is necessary that it occupy space uniformly: Necessity
held it in bonds. Finally, it is not right for it to be incomplete,
a double-negation. The four faces of necessity, the necessity in
the modal relationship of Being to the road-markers expressive
of its essence, all coincide at the bound, which does not allow
itself to be transgressed either from inside or from outside,
and which does not weaken its grip or relax its hold on Being.

Scott Austin

Negative and positive modal propositions coincide in these four
faces of necessity.

There is also a coincidence of negativity and positivity
in the essence itself. Positive predicates are asserted (“whole,”
“perfect”) and denied (“bigger,” “divisible”), and this is also true
of privative predicates (“incomplete” is denied while “unperish-
ing” is asserted). But no predicate is introduced with a negated
copula. ‘The point seems to be that Being can tolerate denials,
especially where contraries are concerned, as well as affirma-
tions, especially where uniformity is concerned, either where
positive predicates are concerned or where privative predicates
are concerned. Instead of denying something of Being with a
negated copula, Parmenides seems to prefer modal paraphrase:
Justice does not allow Being to fail to be at any time, either by
way of coming-to-be or by way of perishing, and Doom does not
allow anything besides Being to come to be, either now or in the
future, which would make it fail to be whole and immovable. The
spectrum of possible types of predications is thus represented
at every point. Thus the essence of Being, as expressed by these
affirmations and denials, is such that it necessarily is globally and
in every way, and cannot be otherwise—i.e., existence and essence
coincide: to be identical with what completely is, essentially, is
to be What Is, denominatively. All four faces of modality and
of predication are, then, represented in fragment 8.

There is also a group of variations on the theme of unity and
plurality. Being in lines 6-15 is defined negatively with regard to

5. Conclusion / Appendix

Every language introduces its own form and manner of
equivocating and/or of disambiguating, on both the syntactic
and the semantic planes.

To the extent that Parmenides’ poem plays on multiple
senses of esfi in order to function, the disambiguation neces-
sary for translation risks being one interpretative step too many,
interfering with the very functioning of the poem. I call this the
scheming of language. Doing the same thing as Gorgias had
already done with Parmenides, it projects a categorical perspec-
tive of an Aristotelian type onto another state of language, a
historically and deliberately different or ambiguous state.

Summary

I have collected all the data (¢estimonia and fragmenta), which dem-
onstrate that in Parmenides poem On Nature there was a long section
concerning astronomy, where he described the heavens and also illustrated
recent, astonishing discoveries accomplished by astronomical research of
his time. Such a section, which is very important in the history of ancient
science, could not be a mere digression, not related to his general theory
of nature. Therefore, every modern interpretation of his philosophical
thought based on the removal of this aspect should certainly be considered
inadequate to explain the whole doctrine in its very essence.

In the first half of the third century BcE, Colotes of Lampsacus,
himself a pupil of Epicurus, wrote a polemical book with the
purpose of demonstrating that all philosophical teachings other
than those of Epicurus denied legitimacy to real life, this on
account of the fact that all of them, though in different ways, did
not rely upon the testimony of the senses. This work was lost in
late antiquity, but we know its contents well, thanks to Plutarch's
scrutiny in his opuscule Against Colotes. Among philosophers
criticized by Colotes there was also Parmenides. This is Plutarch’s
summary of the critiques leveled against him by the Epicurean:
Parmenides, “by saying that ‘the universe is one, has—I do not
