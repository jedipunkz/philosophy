Introduction

Among the many words and numbers which circulate in our contempo-
rary world, descriptors of quality are particularly numerous. Ratings,
rankings, metrics, indicators, auditing, accreditation, benchmarking,
smileys, user reviews, dashboards, international comparisons, and various
forms of quality reports are used to capture quality.

Quality is one of the most widely used terms in our time. Three obser-
vations testify to its contemporary relevance as a social phenomenon
which deserves to be studied (Dahler-Larsen 2008).

The first observation is that discourses of quality have proliferated to a
broad range of social domains. Quality is expected to occur not only in
art and music but also in consumer goods and service provision. We
expect quality in schools, hospitals, universities, and housing. We expect
quality in the air we breathe and in our personal relations. More recently,
it has even become legitimate to talk about “quality of life” both as some-
thing personally and existentially relevant and as something that institu-
tions and polities must take into account. The discourse on quality thus
claims to encompass our life, as such. It has become a meta-discourse
describing how we live; a mirror in which we should see our existence.
This is no small accomplishment.

The second observation is that organizations play important roles in
relation to quality. Organizations are providers of goods and services
characterized by more or less quality. Organizations have recipes, stan-
dards, and prescriptions for how to measure and document quality. Some
organizations make a living from the construction and implementation
of such quality regimes. Organizations make sure that particular inscrip-
tions of quality travel through time and space. They do so in the form of
pre-arranged quality statements (reviews, rankings, websites, and reports)
that are filtered through some organizational machineries and which hold
other organizational machineries accountable. Organizations not only
provide quality, they also hold each other accountable for the provision of
quality, thereby also defining quality in practice in terms of dimensions
and standards pertaining to goods and services.

Organizations are linked in chains and networks and organizational
fields (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). In some views, the smallest unit of
analysis that works as an organizational field independent of other orga-
nizations is the world (Meyer 2008). Many of the organizations that help
define regimes of quality relevant for others do in fact operate on a global
scale. On the micro level, however, organizations are also the sites for
practical negotiations about what quality might mean faced with the dis-
sonance possibly occurring in the friction between universal standards
and the contingencies of the local situation at hand (Stake 2004; Stark
2009).

It is not up to each individual to invent quality from scratch; the orga-
nizational world is already defining quality for us and making it relevant
in many ways.

The third observation is that quality today relates to public matters.
Quality has invaded the public space. We demand quality from hospitals,
schools, foundations, and governments in their capacity as public institu-
tions. Quality has become an important way of dealing with that which
ancient Rome discovered as res publica: the public thing. The res publica
(the etymological source of political units such as the republic) is basically
all of the matters connecting us in a society, as they represent our common
destiny. The public thing stands between us and connects us, because what
you do with it influences my life. Take the quality of the air we breathe:
Once the issue of air quality is raised, we have to talk about pollution from

industry and cars. We also have to talk about the regulation of smoking. As
much as we may disagree on how we balance the different and opposing
interests of drivers, smokers, industry, and so forth—and correspondingly,
what constitutes various dimensions of the notion of air quality—the mat-
ter is a public one simply because people affect each other’s lives through
the air, like it or not. Addressing an issue of quality is often a particular way
of mobilizing others or regulating the behavior of others around a matter
of public relevance. Or rather, it has become that way.

There is a particular socio-historical particularity to the contemporary
status of the concept of quality. Society has changed how it talks about its
res publica. Schools were formerly supposed to bring education, equality,
progress, dannelse, or Bildung’ (Dahler-Larsen et al. 2017). Today, their
primary obligation may be quality. We deal with the management of
public services in terms of quality management. We now see public issues
in the light of how they are framed and presented as issues of quality.
Perhaps we are even at a turning point where we see them as quality issues
rather than as public issues.

The constitution of something as a quality issue creates a fundamental
hermeneutical contradiction undergirding social life. On the one hand, qual-
ity can be understood as collection of subjective viewpoints. You like some
things—I like others. De gustibus not est disputandum, as the Latin maxim
goes. Personal taste simply cannot be discussed. With such a fragmentation
of viewpoints, there can be no collective reasoning about res publica.

On the other hand, the definition of quality can be delegated to an
institutional arrangement, so that common criteria, goals, and instru-
ments are made possible. The institutionalization of quality, however, is
afflicted with uncertainty (Boltanski 2011: 275). Which definition of
quality is at play? Who speaks on behalf of which institutional arrange-
ment? With which consequences?

So if we want to understand the contemporary form and shape of pub-
lic matters, we should attend to quality as a concept as well as its social
and institutional embodiment.

There exists other concepts in society, which also help structure com-
mon social realities, which are also more or less institutionalized, and
which are more or less contested. In this perspective, academics have ana-
lyzed security (Buzan, Wever, and de Wilde 1998), sustainability (Gorz
1980; Latour 2004), equality (Rasmussen 1981), and risk (Beck 1992;
Power 2016). Some of these discursive constructions have overlapping or
competing relations with quality.

However, we are still lacking an academic analysis of the politics of
quality itself. Such analysis should be sensitive to institutionalizations of
the concept through metrics and other documentation practices, as well
as the socio-political consequences hereof. At the same time, an analysis
should pay attention to the uniqueness of quality as a concept. How does
quality in particular help structure reality?

The Many Meanings of Quality and One Thing
That Keeps Them Together

Given the broad applicability of the concept of quality, it is hardly surpris-
ing that quality is one of the richest terms in our civilization, perhaps super-
seded only by “culture.” The term is used in a variety of meanings, such as:
Quality referring to properties of things and human beings. It can refer to
a wide range of phenomena, such as physical attributes, artistry of perfor-
mance, taste, freshness, conformity with norms, durability, user satisfac-
tion, and others; each of which, in turn, embodies multiple dimensions.

Quality can be used in the singular and in the plural (“qualities”).
Quality also occurs as a quantified variable, since there can be more or
less quality. Quality may refer to both absolute scores on some scale and
relative rankings. It may be based on standards or a responsive apprecia-
tion of the specificities of the situation that takes into account that qual-
ity is relative to human experience (Stake 2004).

Quality can refer to phenomena in a category in itself, parallel to
excellence.

Quality can be used in the context of globalization or in the most specific
contexts in time and space. It can stand alone or be combined with a number
of other terms (quality management, quality indicators, quality assurance).

Introduction 5

It may be futile to seek any core meaning inherent in the term. It may
be more realistic to think of a number of meanings that may be mobilized
with flexibility and in different combinations. The concept would be
threatened by fragmentation, however, unless it was used with at least
one common denominator. There is one: Quality is positively loaded.
Higher quality or more quality is always better.* Even without further
qualification, if your friend describes a new restaurant simply as “quality,”
then you know it is good. When negative instances occur, a negative
qualifier is mandatory: The other restaurant is “bad quality.”

In the absence of further qualification, quality itself tilts toward the
positive. As a consequence, even if quality is technically speaking a noun,
it can appear as a positive adjective, as in “she is a quality musician.” At
the same time, a similar combination of terms occurs in, say, “quality
management.” More often than not, what is meant is presumably “man-
agement of quality” (two nouns), although, if you look at the structure of
words, it could also be “good management” (with quality as a positively
loaded adjective).

Quality may take on many meanings, not despite the one principle
uniting them all but rather because this single, unifying principle—its
positive loading—is exactly what allows it flexibility in almost all other
respects, each time making people see it as something irrefutably good.
An appeal to quality itself allows one to add positive stardust to a variety
of projects and initiatives with a simple rhetorical sleight of hand.

It is very difficult to speak against quality. Quality is a concept without
opposition. A Google search for “the national association against quality”
gives you the National Association for Healthcare Quality and the
National Association for Clean Air, and so forth.

Whatever there is to analyze in the term, quality may lie not in any
hidden, essential meaning inherent in the concept but instead in its social
use. There is a link between the conceptual structure of the term “quality”
and its socio-political usefulness. A key part of that use is that quality
always appears under a flag of positivity. This is useful for those who want
to create alliances, gain support, and act with legitimacy.

Very different projects that aim at the improvement of quality may
understand and operationalize quality in very different, perhaps contra-
dictory ways. Yet it is not enough merely to say, “I want to create an alli-
ance and act with legitimacy.” A specific appeal to quality hooks a
particular quality initiative up with a particular quality object understood
in a particular way and, most importantly, provides a positive flag under
which operations can take place. These operations are interesting because
they have a bearing on how we deal with the res publica.

From Common Misunderstandings
to Analytical Principles

Before proceeding with analytical steps, it is important to remove obsta-
cles and misunderstandings. Since quality is already entangled in our
world in so many ways, and since it is positively loaded, we are standing
too close to be able to see it clearly—the proverbial forest for the trees.
Berger et al. (1973) talk about different types of relevance structures.
Relevance structures are ways we see and talk about the world depending
on the type of engagement we have in it. Some relevance structures are
very practical in our daily lives. Distinct from these are theoretical rele-
vance structures, which are guided more by our intentions to analyze
things. In a practical relevance structure, for example, money is a good
thing. In a theoretical relevance structure, however, we can analyze vari-
ous economic systems in a cool way apart from our personal fascination
with money. In a similar vein, we need to overcome some common mis-
understandings and dead ends in our everyday understanding of quality
if we want to proceed with analytical thinking. The following analytical
principles are recommended.

1. Do not let the goodness of quality trap you

We have seen that positivity might be the only principle holding the
various notions of quality together. Yet, positive (or negative) connota-
tions of objects of study are not good guides. Positivity is an impediment
to cool analysis. Cool evaluations may point out that prematurely label-

Introduction 7

ing things “good” can end badly, and positive as well as negative effects
may wander in interesting ways across individuals, social units, and levels
of analysis. Quality finds itself in the midst of a variety of social and
political values. A particular notion of quality is always a choice between
particular values, albeit usually guised in a general veil of goodness.

2. Study quality as a social, not a transcendent phenomenon

An encounter with genuine quality fills anyone with awe. We find
quality in food, music, literature, and many other domains of life. Quality
does not occur as frequently as we would like, and it is hard to define; like
happiness, we simply know it when we see it. Quality fascinates. Quality
comes to us as a gift. It appears to us as sent from above, something tran-
scendent. Quality lets us forget ourselves.

As fascinating as it is, quality is therefore a dangerous object of
study. The most recent good book about quality (not about a particu-
lar quality regime, not about quality assurance, not about quality in
this or that sector, but about quality as such), and an often-cited one,
is Robert Pirsig’s (1974) Zen and the Art of Motorcycle Maintenance.
While the author maintains that quality itself remains undefinable,
the narrator alter ego becomes insane as a result of his inquiry into
metaphysical quality.

Let us not forget ourselves. Let us stay on this side of the moon. Quality
should be studied as social phenomenon through the lens of social sci-
ence, including the history of socio-political concepts, and not mixed
with a preoccupation with motorcycles, drugs, nor Buddhism. In this
book, quality is understood in entirely earthly terms. We have a res
publica to deal with.

3. Beware of synecdoche

A synecdoche is a figure of speech where a part is taken for a whole or
a whole is taken for a part. Its literal meaning in Greek is “simultaneous
understanding.” While the notion of quality in its entirety is very rich, in
the practical world quality usually comes to us in a particular, enunciated
form as some aspect of something in a particular situation.

8 P. Dahler-Larsen

In many instances of quality assurance, there is a preoccupation with
minute details that need to be documented. Yet, the umbrella term given
to all of these matters is “quality” even if not all aspects of quality are
taken into account. That would be impossible given the richness of qual-
ity in its totality. The specific version of synecdoche used here is totum pro
parte. A grand and positive term is mobilized to describe a range of more
specific phenomena. Totum pro parte provides a creative lack of clarity
about exactly which parts of the larger unspecified set of meanings are
actually referred to in the specific case at hand. Through this figure of
speech, all of the positive connotations of quality may be taken onboard
a particular project, even if this particular project is fairly pedestrian. The
totum pro parte may help create support for a project, because if you are
not supportive of the project, the rhetorical figure will position you as an
adversary of quality, as such. Therefore, beware of synecdoche. Without
this awareness, you will fall victim to anyone who uses the term for any
purpose and thereby claims all of the positive connotations of quality in
toto, regardless of what is specifically done practically and rhetorically.

4. Do not conflate a situated experience and a quality standard

Let us consider a solution to a problem in a particular situation in
the form of a quality initiative. For example, nursing homes for the
elderly have been subjected to cutbacks, and there is now concern for
the level of personal care and hygienic standards. A political party
suggests a legally mandated quality standard at two showers per week
for all elderly citizens under care. Once this criterion is applied to a
number of different situations, new problems occur. Some of the
elderly have skin diseases that make showers uncomfortable. Some
prefer bathtubs instead of showers due to the risk of falling. Some
have simply never taken two showers a week. Evidently, the elderly
individual must have the right to refuse the showers. But does that
mean that those who cannot speak, the mentally ill, and the senile
should have two showers, whether they speak up or not? Perhaps
wisely, the proposal was dropped. ‘The elderly and the local staff sim-
ply had to figure out how they would handle the problem from day to
day, from situation to situation, as they usually did.

There is a long, rich, and respectable tradition in the literature on
practice to conceive of quality as sensitive to the specifics of the situa-
tion (Schwandt 2002). The tricky part, as the example shows, is that
once a criterion of quality becomes incorporated into a quality stan-
dard, the concept of quality travels when the standard travels. Standards
do not always specify their own limitations. They constantly lend
themselves to new situations. If you buy into a quality standard that is
relevant in one situation (S1), you often also accept it being applied to
other situations even if there is no way in the world in which you can
think all of the contingencies through that characterize the next situa-
tions (S2, S3, etc.). In a political or organizational perspective, how-
ever, it is not enough to think of either $1, S2, or S3. Policies,
standards, management instruments, and recipes by definition operate
across situations.

An important thing to watch then becomes how and when specific
experiences from specific contexts translate into quality criteria that travel
across time and space as parts of a concept of quality that are then talked
about as quality, as such. It is difficult to dissect a particular notion of
quality in order to determine the boundaries of the settings to which it
applies, because all of these possible variations in settings are not known.
However, opponents of any given general notion can be positioned as an
“enemy of quality” even in Situation 1, where the application of that
notion may be very reasonable. In order to reduce opposition, it is there-
fore rhetorically tempting to operate with general notions of quality
without situation specificity.

In phenomenology, an interesting concept is the indexicality of typifi-
cations (Schutz 1978; Schwandt 1994). It means that a given situation
lends itself to various ways of seeing the “typical” features of that situa-
tion. Interpretation mediates so to speak between the typification and its
situation-specific application. Because of the indexicality of typifications,
we cannot be sure that a particular typification will actually be put to use
in that situation—until it actually happens. Once you buy into a given
quality standard as relevant for a set of situations, however, you have also
typified those situations as “fitting” the type of quality you are about to
deliver (consistent with the standard), even if you have yet to encounter
these new situations.

People sometimes buy into a notion of quality that has very little con-
tact with even one practical, situated experience. Broad discourses about
quality without contact to practical situations are problematic. For exam-
ple, students occasionally interview childcare workers about how they see
quality as part of the practices of childcare. The childcare workers then
talk broadly about respect for children as human beings and the wonder-
ful experience of seeing them growing up. Students believe that these
concerns permeate pedagogical practices, but the interview perhaps never
makes a connection between the wider quality discourse and practical
situations.

Thus far, we can conclude that quality within a specific situation and
quality as a concept (potentially relevant outside that situation) have a
precarious relationship. While they can mutually inform each other, it is
also (too) tempting to think that once one of them is known, the other is
also. Sometimes they have little to do with each other. What is interesting
is what brings a particular notion of quality and a particular understand-
ing of a situation together. Defining a situation is problematic, defining
quality is problematic, and connecting the two is problematic.

An analytical key is to decompose notions of quality depending on the
relevance structures that enunciate them. In plain language, this means:
Who is asking? In what situation(s)? What is his/her/their project? If we
understand the relevance structure in which a notion of quality is
anchored, then we may understand better (analytically) which exact ver-
sion of quality we are talking about and what it might afford. Each qual-
ity notion should be taken seriously as representative of a particular
relevance structure, but not literally as evidence of quality in any univer-
sal sense. To understand how the “same” concept can operate so differ-
ently, what is necessary for an analysis is a controlled shift in our grasp of
quality in a theoretical relevance structure. It does not help to just insist
what quality is.

5. Do not believe that descriptors are only descriptors

It is common to talk about quality indicators as if they “indicate” an
underlying objective reality. We gauge our descriptors based on whether
they provide us with valid and reliable accounts of the aspect of reality we

Introduction 11

wish to describe. This notion is particularly problematic in the case of
quality. Quality descriptors do not relate to quality in the same way as
thermometers relate to temperature. Even if we accept what has to be
found in the sociology of science—that our understanding of physical
phenomena depend to a large extent on paradigms and conventions—it
is obvious that there is a high degree of consensus about temperature,
whereas the notion of quality remains fragmented, contested, value-
laden, and situation-dependent.

A claim to measure quality cannot be understood as referring to an
already-existing reality, but as an attempt to define reality in a particular
way. It is performative (Butler 2010). It seeks to enact what it claims to
describe. When people react to the measurement, it may happen that
they take the definition of quality inherent in the quality measurement
for granted and base their actions on that understanding in which case
the measurement has constitutive effects (Dahler-Larsen 2014). The
measurement interferes with reality, sometimes colonizing minds and
practices (Erkkila and Piironen 2014; Shore and Wright 2015), some-
times leading to new uncertainty, risk, paradox, or controversy (Barry
2012; Dambrin and Robson 2011; Jensen 2011; Pollock et al. 2018;
Roberts 2018).

There is a close link between the conceptual structure of quality and
the capacity of quality measurements to interfere with reality. It is because
of the openness and flexibility inherent in the concept of quality that
metrics and other forms of documentation have such a wide space for not
only measuring quality, but in fact also defining what it means. The con-
ceptual structure of quality helps pave the way for the socio-political
influence of people who operationalize it. In addition, the positivity bias
inherent in quality provides this endeavor with a sugarcoating, at least on
the rhetorical level.

Purpose of the Book

It is an interesting challenge to write against something which appears to
have transcendent origins, which usually involves confusing relations
between the concept and the situations to which it is applied, and which

12 P. Dahler-Larsen

turns around the relation between an object and its measuring.
Furthermore, what makes the concept of quality particularly “unhin-
tergehbar” as the Germans would say, meaning “something you cannot
go behind,” is the ever-present positivity of quality. It is tempting to criti-
cize one enunciation of quality for not being “real quality” (as if the
author knows what that is). But the ambition here is to go behind even
the idea that there is an underlying real quality apart from the socio-
historical practices in which it is dealt with.

I apologize if my writing might appear speculative in some passages
and my terminology unconventional. The intention is to achieve a kind
of Verfremdungseffekt (an “alienation effect”). This effect helps create dis-
tance from everyday thinking, which is necessary for analysis.

The first and primary purpose of this book is to take the obviousness
out of any statement that claims to be based on evident knowledge about
quality. My intention not to prescribe any particular specification of
quality does not mean I am uninterested in quality. I just have different
kinds of interests in it. These are analytical interests.

The first of these interests relates to my engagement in the field of
evaluation. I am a researcher, teacher, and sometimes consultant. Since
quality takes up much discursive space, I want to understand what kind
of evaluative statements and practices are produced with the help of the
notion of quality. Without going too deep into evaluation theory (which
has been done elsewhere; see Dahler-Larsen 2012; Schwandt 2002, 2015;
Vedung 1997), let me briefly say that some of the key concerns are how
objects of evaluation are conceived, how values matter in evaluation, and
what difference evaluations make for policy and practice. I am particu-
larly interested in how evaluative statements work as arguments to help
shape practices, minds, priorities, and the broader social order (Dahler-
Larsen 2012). Obviously, quality relates to all of this.

As mentioned earlier in this introduction, quality is so prominent in
contemporary discourse that we have reason to believe it plays an impor-
tant socio-political role. Other books have studied evaluations and audits
as socio-political phenomena (Dahler-Larsen 2012; Power 1997; Taylor
and Balloch 2005). This is the first book to explore the socio-political
implications of evaluative statements with a specific focus on the contri-
bution of the concept of quality to these processes.

Introduction 13

More specifically, I want to know which understandings of quality are
in play and what might be accomplished by appealing to quality. I explore
the dynamics of the relation between the conceptual and social aspects of
quality. What are the conditions of possibility for producing statements
about quality that reach across time and space, sometimes even in a global
perspective?

What is the role of quality, and more specifically quality inscriptions,
such as measurement? And finally, if the concept of quality reigns under
present socio-historical conditions, what happens to the collective rea-
soning about public issues?

The concept of quality is so rich that the only place where all of its mean-
ings meet is in a dictionary. In practice, quality enunciates itself as some-
thing more specific. Quality perspectives are ways of talking about quality
that conceptually only highlight a particular conceptual aspect. A quality
perspective is logically linked to an understanding of a quality problem in
a given quality situation. I will map and discuss some prominent quality
perspectives and show their relativity and, sometimes, instability. On that
basis, I will argue that quality measurement plays a critical role in keeping
a notion of quality fixed. In other words, I turn a common argument on its
head. Conceptual unclarity does not make measurement difficult. Instead,
measurement controls quality in a way that the concept itself is too con-
fused to do. In fact, the social function of measurement expands exactly
because measurement plays a key role in regulating the meanings and social
implications of an otherwise elusive phenomenon.

The centrality of measurement and quality inscriptions (forms of qual-
ity documentation) raise important questions for public issues related to
democracy, particularly when they occur without deliberation about
quality. To shed light on quality as a socio-political problem, it is neces-
sary to draw on a variety of theoretical sources.

Theoretical Approaches and Intellectual Debts

Neo-institutional theory in the so-called sociological version has deliv-
ered much-needed criticism of functionalistic, rationalistic, and individ-
ualistic models of organizational life. Instead, neo-institutionalists argue

that most of what takes place in organizations are not rational solutions
to well-defined problems but rather reflections of norms, values, scripts,
and recipes that circulate in the social environments of organizations;
hence the “sociological” label. For the key contributors to this school of
thought, such as DiMaggio and Powell (1983) and John Meyer (2008),
conformity with modern organizational environments first and foremost
implies the institutionalization of bureaucratic rationality (Stark 2009:
168). The critical edge here is that, in this understanding, rationality has
less to do with technical problem-solving than with achieving a particular
kind of legitimacy. In this theoretical light, regimes of quality are regimes
for creating legitimacy. Organizations in the modern world are surpris-
ingly similar in terms of how they conform to the same rationalized
myths about quality. Quality may appear as a positive word that can be
used to improve the world in a “warm” way in contradistinction to “cold”
bureaucracies. Bureaucracies may engage with quality in order to renew
the moral framework they are otherwise lacking (Boltanski 2011: 204).
Tragically, however, bureaucracies do not stop being bureaucracies just
because they engage in quality management.

In one of the key texts that inaugurated the neo-institutional era,
Meyer and Rowan (1977) offer hypotheses about how organizations han-
dle evaluations. They predict that organizations will protect their opera-
tive core from evaluations of quality. Evaluations of quality will primarily
be used as “window dressing.”

Meyer and Rowan possibly underestimated how invasive evaluations
of quality ultimately proved to be. Today, external inspectors have ways
to enforce quality assurance. Digital networks help diffuse massive
amounts of data in and between organizations. Quality measurement is
now connected with legal and financial sanctions in a manner that Meyer
and Rowan did not predict. Quality has become more invasive, and there
are specific mechanisms which ensure that managers must respond to
ratings and rankings (Espeland and Sauder 2007).

There is a camp of scholars studying quality indicators, whose critical
view is almost antithetical to the neo-institutional paradigm of window
dressing and decoupling. Instead, quality indicators, metrics, and other
forms of documentation are neoliberal instruments which colonize prac-
tices and undermine professional values (Shore and Wright 2015; Bruno

Introduction 15

et al. 2014). Sometimes these critics assume, however, that this form of
governing-at-a-distance works in a linear way. It is as if when the instru-
ments used to govern people have been identified, we also know that the
steering is successful.

Instead, paradox, tension, and the active, but ambiguous involvement
of people under measurement could be attended to and understood as a
part of how quality inscriptions operate (Dambrin and Robson 2011;
Kauko et al. 2018; Kaltenbrunner and de Rijcke 2017).

Vocabularies are needed that connects the concept of quality with
practical situations and practical consequences. Theoretically, we find
inspiration in measurement theory, which describes the social and practi-
cal tensions regarding metrics (Desrosiéres 2009, 2014; Porter 1994);
and in aspects of Actor—Network Theory (Latour 2004), which explains
how actors and mechanisms that produce inscriptions are connected in
complex ways. My purpose is not to contribute specifically to each of
these fields of theory but to distill only what is helpful for the under-
standing of quality and its journey through the world.

Stark uses practice theory to describe how actors find their ways
through conflicting orders of worth (Stark 2009). He focuses not on iso-
morphism as an outcome of rationalization (as neo-institutionalists do)
but on dissonance in organizational life that results from the meeting of
rationalizing forces with concrete situated practices. Dissonance—the
simultaneous presence of multiple values and concerns—increases the
need for a theoretical focus on distributed intelligence, diversity, recom-
bination, adaptability, multivocality, and heterarchical organization (Stark
2009: 172, 187). Stark brings action in organizations back into alignment
with notions of situated action (Hay 2012) and the long-standing tradi-
tion of theories of practice (Schwandt 2002). In the light of this tradition,
practice is complex and often embedded in situations that call for atten-
tion to multiple (if not conflicting) evaluative principles.

While Stark’s work resonates with classical tenets in practice theory, he
adds special attention to the dynamics and complexities of technologies and
quickly changing markets. Consequently, actors cannot merely take recipes
for action “for granted” (Stark 2009: 189). Clever practitioners engage in
“trading zones” where multiple orders of worth are negotiated. Stark argues
that heterarchical organizations help us curb the total dominance of one

order of worth and one single metric: market value. He also argues that
heterarchical organizations have a high capacity for learning, adaptation,
and discovery. They actively develop tools that allow them to construct their

individual and the evaporation of accountability? Which, to be fair, Stark

heterarchical organizational model, including its contribution to learn-

appropriate for the evaluation of orders of worth?

My favorite philosopher, Gianni Vattimo (2005), would respond that,
in the present era, which he describes as postmodern in the sense of “post-
metaphysical,” there can be no ultimate principle guaranteeing the pri-
oritization of the rest of the principles in this world.

While I hope to have made my intellectual debt to several of the above-
mentioned literatures clear, my intention is not to contribute to these
fields. I am merely interested in understanding how the present
construction and use of quality as a concept helps structure social order
in particular ways. My analytical entry point is the concept of quality. This
project is embedded in a problem-oriented research frontier, of which
there are many in social science but which do not necessarily converge
into “high” theoretical knowledge (Mjoset 2009: 64).

In tying my research strategy to the social life of a concept, I have
found the kind of conceptual analysis suggested by Reinhard Koselleck
particularly helpful. In Koselleck’s terminology, a word becomes a con-
cept when loaded with a set of socio-political meanings, experiences, and
expectations (Koselleck 2007: 71).

While any concept is a result of particular history and social order, it is

Given the centrality of the concept of quality and the complexities related
to it, which I described in the introduction, it is surprising how little the
history of the concept of quality is discussed—how often it is completely
neglected, in fact.

Neither Blond’s Encyclopedia of Education (Blishen 1969), Webster’
Dictionary of Word Origins (Merriam-Webster 1995) written “for the

provide any entries for the term quality.

In many other situations, a definition is given as in the following
example: “Quality: A parameter of action describing those properties of
the product which will be critical for the potential consumer’s perception
of the product and preference for the product” (Management Leksikon
2001: 219). Notice how much is already taken for granted (e.g. products
and consumers and preferences and the whole economic regime in which
these terms make sense).

Whether no definition is given or whether a definition is provided
without further reflection, taken-for-grantedness reigns. Even among
researchers who deserve respect for the fine intellectual analyses of qual-

The Ancient World: Qualia and the World
of Ideas

Quality was first coined in Ancient Rome by Cicero (106-43 BC) in the
form of the term qualis. The term can best be translated into “how-ness,”
“of what sort,” or “of what kind.” A quale (in plural, qualia) is a singular
sense of a property of things, usually materials. It is also a property of a
mental state that determines what it is like (Cambridge Dictionary of
Philosophy 1995: 762).

Cicero coined the term qualis after the Greek poiotes, another artifi-
cially constructed word. This was used by Plato (app. 428-348 BC) in his
report on the dialogue between Socrates (470/469-399 BC) and
Theaetetus (Klein 1977: 114). The two discuss deep questions in what we
would later call epistemology, particularly the role of the sense perception
for the construction of knowledge. If knowledge is built on sense percep-
tion, a key question then becomes what can be done with the fact that
when the same wind blows, one person perceives it to be cold and another
does not? Things do not speak directly to us. Human perception must
play a role.

The dialogue introduced an important distinction between a thing and
the sensations of its properties (the qualia). A precondition for sensing
qualia is the human capacity to recognize or recollect the corresponding
properties. So, for example, the notion of whiteness is logically different
from, but a precondition for, the sensation of something actually being
white (Klein 1977: 114). Whiteness exists as an idea distinct from its
being embodied in particular phenomena. The sensation of white related
to the thing is therefore not totally a property of the thing itself. The
quality of a thing depends, at least partly, on an idea that is not inside the
thing.

One question that follows is also whether a thing has a separate exis-
tence apart from the sum of the qualia describing it, or, in other words,
whether the essence of the thing is fully exhausted through a number of
qualia used as descriptors. Plato would say no. He would argue that it is
possible to recognize all of the letters in Theaetetus’ name and still not
grasp the idea of the name, as such.!

Plato continues to insist on the role of ideas. There are ideas about
objects that are not grasped simply by adding up a number of qua-
lia that characterize the object. Furthermore, qualia themselves only
make sense because they are supported by ideas, such as the idea of color,
weight, or extension. A “world of ideas” explains how it is possible
for human beings to “know” something about “things” through “sensa-
tions.” The world of ideas is a bit of a deus ex machina. In the interpreta-
tion of later philosophers, this is an immensely metaphysical solution
(Vattimo 2005).

According to Plato, human insight remains linked to a basis of human
virtue; or in modern terminology: Epistemological success depends on
the virtue of human beings (plato.stanford.edu/entries/plato-Theaetetus).
The ultimate harmony of these domains is secured by the metaphysical
canopy overarching Plato’s thinking.

The dialogue ends inconclusively, which has stimulated curiosity
among philosophers. Is Plato consistent in his account of the dialogue
and in his position (Klein 1977: 4)? Is Plato’s account of the dialogue a
smokescreen hiding the fact that he himself has no firm understanding of
knowledge (Bostock 1988: 270)? Or is his mission merely to get away
with a few common misunderstandings, really just playing with a num-
ber of philosophical training exercises (Klein 1977: 4)?

In practice, Socrates’ dialogue with Theaetetus ends as Socrates leaves
the scene to go to the courthouse to attend the trial against him, which
ended in his death sentence. So much for epistemology.

Primary and Secondary Qualia

The meaning of quality as a set of properties which characterize objects
has been long-lived. In 1425, medicine was known to produce the quali-
ties of hot/cold and moisture/dryness (Barnhart and Steinmetz 1988).
Mention of the qualities of water occurs in 1533. In 1725, the qualities
of ideas were described as “clear” or “obscure” (Oxford English Dictionary
1989). Qualia also referred to the qualities of sound and color.

Philosophers continue to struggle with the question of how much of
qualia are due to objects and how much to human beings. With the
advent of empiricism, this question was answered by a simple move,
namely splitting qualia into two categories: primary and secondary quali-
ties. No issue in the history of the concept of quality is more extensively
dealt with among philosophers than this distinction.

According to Locke (1632-1704), the primary qualities reside in
things themselves. The secondary ones are human perceptions somehow
triggered by our perceptions of the things. This distinction is important
because it paves the way for a split between objective and subjective
dimensions of quality. The primary (or objective) qualities can only be
measured and objectively gauged if technical difficulties in measurement
are resolved and proper instruments developed. All of the paraphernalia
of modern science can help us here, guided by Galilei’s dictum: Measure
everything, and make measurable everything that is not yet measurable.

This dictum obviously also challenges the daring scientist to conquer
the world of all qualia hitherto categorized as “secondary.” As the termi-
nology itself suggests, they are second-order derivatives of something
measurable. Paradoxically, then, human sensations and emotions might
one day be given mechanistic explanations not in spite of the distinction
between primary and secondary qualities but because of it (Hegge 1978:
127). The distinction is philosophically important not only for the con-
struction of knowledge but also because it influences the perception of
the human being. All aspects of the human that can be studied as primary
qualities are relegated to a mechanistic worldview known from the natu-
ral sciences, while the rest becomes an epiphenomenon. ‘The distinction
in itself is potentially reductionist from the view of a phenomenologist

(Luckmann 1978) or any other philosophical viewpoint, which sees phe-
nomena such as soul, spirit, and meaning as central to human life.

Moreover, a critical question becomes how and where the distinction
is to be drawn. A way to learn about the distinction between primary and
secondary qualities is to observe how it is drawn at different points in
time. For example, weight, extension, and movement were categorized as
primary qualities from the earliest days of the distinction. Locke held that
heat was a secondary quality because, as Plato already had shown, the
same wind could be felt to be either hot or cold (Cambridge Dictionary
of Philosophy 1995). Evidently, heat has moved to the other side of the
distinction after the notion of temperature was discovered and thermom-
eters invented. The paraphernalia of measurement obviously had the
capacity to displace this quality from one category to the other.

Phenomenological philosophers have argued that the human world is
constituted through human sensation, experience, and sense-making
(Luckmann 1978). It is unacceptable and reductionist to perceive of a
Chinese wall inside the human being which separates the domain of the
natural sciences from humanities (the measurable from the unmeasur-
able). In practice, however, history shows that wall to in fact be non-
existent for all the reasons that the same philosophers have feared. The
worldview of the empirical natural sciences first establishes a domain for
themselves in contradistinction to the “subjective” remainder of the
world. The former then expands continuously, in fact colonizing the lat-
ter, according to Luckmann (1978).

Measurement does not stop in the face of some metaphysical and pre-
social properties inside things or inside human beings, for that matter. In
fact, measurement helps move around our fundamental categorizations
of things.

For Latour (1987, 2004, 2005), there is no “guarantee” for the thing-
ness of things in things. Instead, knowledge about things is a result of
networks and associations that make measurement and inscriptions of
qualia possible.

Interestingly enough, the distinction between primary and secondary
qualities is made and upheld by those interested in an empirical (i.e. anti-
metaphysical) approach to the world only to establish a peculiar meta-
physical basis for the categorization of what can be studied. Latour also

A History of the Concept of Quality 29

insists that the primary/secondary qualities distinction is politically prob-
lematic; if primary qualities define the world we already share (objectively
determined) and the secondary divide us (because they are subjective),
then the bulk of the political work has already been carried out by the
distinction itself (Latour 2004: 47)!

From the standpoint of more recent sociology of science, this observa-
tion reveals a lot about both the constructive character of measurement
as well as the problematic nature of ontological distinctions such as the
one between primary and secondary qualities. The fact that the drawing
of the distinction can be so easily moved by means of practical measure-
ment makes clear that the basis for the epistemological distinction should
not be sought in the domains of ontology and metaphysics, but in the
practical life of methodology.

The implication is that measurement of quality plays an important
role. It helps constitute both the ontology, epistemology, and politics of
quality even in present times.

Quality, Morality, Esthetics... and Commerce

While the term quality continues to play a role in the realm of epistemol-
ogy and the discussion about qualia and their status has remained of great
concern to philosophers, the term quality entered entirely different
domains in the centuries following 1300. We now turn to the use of the
term quality in morality, esthetics, and trade. Unfortunately, philoso-
phers and other specialists who describe the history of concepts have paid
much less attention to the meaning and use of the term quality in these
areas of life than to the epistemological properties of qualia. This is para-
doxical, because the goodness of quality that continues to be quintessen-
tial for the use of the term today was constituted through this transposition
of quality into these new domains.

One of the conditions of possibility for the entry of the term quality
into the moral domain was secularization and the loosening of a religious
and theocratic grip on human life. The Reformation was one of the con-
ducing factors to this long, historical transformation.

Quality also becomes generally known as something good and posi-
tive. Already before 1396, it is used to refer to a grade of excellence

30 P. Dahler-Larsen

(Barnhart and Steinmetz 1988). This opens two very common meanings
of quality, which have remained in use since then: Quality as a category
(of something excellent) (similar to nobility but now used in a general
sense) and quality as a variable (degree of excellence). These remain the
most prominent and frequent connotations of quality. The positivity of
quality infuses or overrides almost any other aspect of the concept of
quality and its application to a variety of phenomena. Whenever you
encounter it, quality is something good, absolutely or relatively, but good
nevertheless.

These connotations of quality remain (Encyclopedia Britannica 1972;
Webster’s Ninth 1990; Oxford Advanced Learners Dictionary 2000).
Quality is absolute (a category of good in itself) or relational, where it
depends on its position in relation to something else. Either way, it is not
common. It is not ordinary. It is extraordinary, fine, rare, or at least com-
paratively good.

This meaning of quality (as extraordinary and/or comparatively good)
builds on the epistemological configurations of qualia known since
Antiquity, but it adds entirely new evaluative dimensions. It is presum-
ably no coincidence that these meanings break through in the centuries
after 1400, perhaps most clearly from 1600 to 1700. As mentioned, one
can imagine that a slow, gradual loosening of the control of religious
dogma over society would allow greater space for the moral and esthetic
dimensions being woven into the concept of quality.

One might also imagine that merchants would like to talk about the
sublime quality of the silk and spices they brought home from the new
world. Quality is an incredibly useful term in trade and commerce for
explaining why similar quantities of goods should not cost the same.
Once there is more than one type of something, once there is a diversity
of goods available, it becomes pertinent to describe their variations. To
the extent that variation in prices must be explained, grades of quality
become relevant.

We know from the history of metrology that attempts were already
made in ancient Rome to standardize cubic measures (for standardized
amounts of, e.g. olive oil and grain). We also know that the invention and
implementation of the metric system in Europe was instrumental in
replacing a plethora of various local measures. How much is a foot? That

A History of the Concept of Quality 31

depends on your foot. The same with inches, pounds, ounces, yards, and
so on. Examples were given of how, when the price of flour went up, bak-
ers started baking smaller loaves of bread to make ends meet. They were
restricted by conventional or feudal price-setting, and they were expected
to bake traditional bread in traditional ways. The only logical response
therefore became to be “flexible” concerning the size of “a normal loaf of
bread.”

The standardized metric system rendered it easier to trust that a par-
ticular amount of something was in fact objectively defined. With the
opening of markets, prices became variable. Quality became a necessary
factor for explaining variations of price given the same standardized
amount of a particular type of good.

To understand how quality can become as widespread as it is today, we
must revisit one more domain where quality peaks and then disappears.
It has to do with the quality of human beings.

Already around 1300, quality takes on the meaning of character, dis-
position, and temperament. After 1400, the term quality is also referred
to in terms of rank or position in society or nobility. Quality becomes
something fine, something above the extraordinary. The positivity of
quality has remained with us to this day, but quality as a label on social
rank has become rare and archaic (Oxford English Dictionary 1989).
J.L. Heiberg (1791-1860) could meaningfully state that “A man of qual-
ity is the term for one whose rank forbids him to pay his tailor,” but a
dictionary suggests that this use of the term expired by the beginning of
the twentieth century (Ordbog Over Det Danske Sprog 1976).

In the modern world, quality can only be used to imply class or rank
in a subtle, indirect sense, such as when intellectuals talk about the lack
of “quality” in mass culture products, while not explicating their view as
elitist. Other political forces can seek to remove the term from official
policies (cultural policy in particular) as an appeal to populist or anti-
elitist sentiments (Husz 2012). This move has been primarily observed in
policies related to “the fine arts” and to reiterate, only by implication.

Another meaning of quality (known to be used in 1596) which is now
lost is that of party or side (in contradistinction to enemy). If quality
creates distinctions between people, this function is not visible anymore,
except by indirect implication. In the contemporary world, distinctions

32 P. Dahler-Larsen

between people are implemented much more smoothly when not man-
aged through a system making these distinctions explicit and thereby
deprives human beings of their dignity (which is one of the reasons why
Apartheid was such a horrendous system). So quality presumably
increased its chances of remaining in circulation in modern times when
it disconnected itself from all explicit connotations of social rank.

The only common way in which the term quality continues to be used
as a label on people is when a person’s intellectual, moral, or other quali-
ties are praised. To reiterate, these are moral qualities and individual qua-
lia, not categories of people.

Exactly as Foucault warned, it is tempting to overstate the later useful-
ness of particular displacements of meanings as if there were an inherent
teleological drive in the history of the concept. Nevertheless, I do empha-
size that a very productive rhetorical opening was made possible by move-
ments in the concept of quality described above: the identification of
quality with excellence and with a grade of good plus the elimination of
the ability of quality to explicitly refer to categories of people. These dis-
placements of meaning make it possible to refer to quality in a way where
it can be used everywhere where there is a market, and it can gain support
and establish alliances, not fronts, because it operates under an uncondi-
tionally positive flag. Quality becomes ever-positive. The road becomes
paved for a broadly applicable concept without opposition. In addition,
the positivity of quality infuses other aspects of quality with ever-positive
meaning.

Quality, Norms, and Statistics

While the meaning of something unquestionably good is maintained,
another radical turn in the history of quality takes place with the advent
of industrialism. An early precursor occurred in the US, where systematic
production of small arms already took place in 1850 without the kind of
craftsmanship known from Europe. The key was conformance to
specifications to enable interchangeable machine parts to fit together
(Reeves and Bednar 1994).

A History of the Concept of Quality 33

While the excellent, the sublime, and the rare have constituted impor-
tant dimensions of quality since around 1400, quality under industrial-
ism ushers in an understanding of quality with almost opposite
connotations. In industry, a quality product is one that conforms to stan-
dards. Variation is deviance. Deviance is bad. Quality and norm become
equivalents. Minimum standards of quality must be met in all aspects of
production. As technological advances and division of labor render mass
production possible, workers are separated from the responsibility for
their final products (Berger 1964). While the quantity of produced goods
increases rapidly, questions about their quality become relevant, espe-
cially in the absence of the pride and self-consciousness of the craftsman.
In the industrial era, the dominant relevance structure sees quality as a
matter of control over all aspects of industrial production at the expense
of the perspective of the individual worker.

As a consequence, in the first half of the twentieth century industrial
organization invents a special organizational function with responsibility
for the quality of products.

Published in 1931, Shewhart’s Economic Control of Quality of
Manufactured Product lays down the principles for the quantification of
quality so that the quality function has a measurable “object” and can
document its progress from one period to the next. It is epistemologically
supported by statistics that make it possible to draw conclusions about
large numbers of phenomena based on a representative sample (Reeves
and Bednar 1994). All variation not induced by sheer chance should be
controlled and eliminated. Statistics gauge how much chance can account
for. The rest is the responsibility of quality management.

World War II intensified the need to ensure that products were actu-
ally functioning in the moment of use and, thus, the need for systematic
quality control.

The second half of the twentieth century is characterized by attempts
at obtaining systematic and holistic grip on quality in the production
sphere. Dictionaries introduce entries such as quality control, quality
assurance, quality management, quality control officer, and so on. These
numerous composite terms pave the way for grammatical use of quality
as an adjective, which occurs from the 1960s onwards.

34 P. Dahler-Larsen

Several recurring problems and trends seek to define that holistic grip
more specifically. Despite several attempts at improving the quality of the
final product, however, some forms of production continue to fail.

This problem becomes particularly intense when the production pro-
cess is long and labor-intensive, and when customers are ready to turn to
other products (International Encyclopedia of Business and Management
2002). It then simply becomes too expensive to postpone quality control
until the manufacture of final product.

Consequently, quality becomes more process-oriented. Attention to
quality is necessary in each step of the production process. Checklists,
algorithms, and other tools are developed to enforce the idea. Over time,
it becomes clear that quality requires the active participation of all
employees, guided by some form of organization-wide coordination and
management. The full price must now be paid for the degradation of the
craftsman so characteristic of industrialism.

An additional influence on the concept of quality comes from consum-
erism. While engineers hold a production-oriented view on quality, mar-
keting personnel focus more on how products fit into people's lives and
how consumers perceive brands. In addition to changing quality recipes
in fundamental ways, the consumerist perspective also adds another layer
of relativity to quality. Maybe a cheap, unhealthy product, a product
without durability, or an otherwise inferior product can be a quality prod-
uct as long as consumers are willing to pay for it. The right of consumers
to purchase and throw away products was seen as new and progressive
(compared to the scarcity they had experienced earlier) (Husz 2012).

Ads now claim how many times a product has been bought, not sold.
New paradigms for understanding consumers emerged. Qualitative
methods proved useful in this endeavor, as focus groups became popular
and ethnographic studies helped marketers understand consumer per-
spectives. Qualitative methods were sold partly as socially progressive,
partly as pragmatically useful (Patton 2002). Gone are the days when
philosophers in ivory towers relegate consumer perceptions to the almost
derogatory category of “secondary qualities.” Phenomenology, culture
theory, and semiotics are mobilized in order to understand—and com-
mercially exploit—consumer perspectives.

A History of the Concept of Quality 35

Marketers, market analysts, and strategists seek influence on how an
industrial corporation defines quality with reference to consumer views.
Marketing discourse installs a logic of its own (Bauman 2001). Service
packages and other initiatives that moved products and (eventually also
design) closer to consumers become relevant for the understanding of
quality. In this light, Callon et al. (2002) talk about qualification and re-
qualification, that is, processes where qualities of products are attributed,
stabilized, objectified, and arranged in the light of passes as a sellable
good ona market. In their view, the old distinction between primary and
secondary qualities becomes increasingly obsolete, as everything that
helps position a product in a favorably in relation to other products can
be “a quality” (Callon et al. 2002: 200).

Processes of qualification and re-qualification are demanding for the
producer. Companies struggle to incorporate the concern for quality—
however defined—into their planning, production, and even their
organizations, as such. An impetus into the conceptualization of qual-
ity in production is articulated along with an interest in organizational
culture. Especially in the 1980s, American automobile manufacturers
found themselves outcompeted by their Japanese counterparts. Japanese
cars were smaller and more fuel-efficient, giving them a competitive
advantage after the energy crises in the 1970s. They were also reason-
ably priced and not lacking in design or durability compared to
American cars. American business consultants began asking questions
about the secrets behind the Japanese success. They found the answer in
organizational culture: Japanese workers were devoted to their com-
pany. They also practiced particular forms of working together, now
known as “quality circles.” Quality circles are immediate, on-the-spot
forms of cooperation where employees across functions and levels con-
tribute to the identification and solution of quality problems as they
appear on a daily basis in the company. Although many of the stories
about Japan may in fact have been fabricated and the importance of
organizational culture overrated, the notion of organizational culture as
a key to excellence was a vehicle that could be used to massage a notion
of quality into the hearts and minds of all employees (Peters and
Waterman 1982).

While the ideals of Taylorism, which dominated the early phases of
industrialism, focused on the structure and division of labor but ignored
the meaning of work, organizational culture was a new paradigm that
filled the gap. Organizational culture as a paradigm built on the earlier
notion that quality required the attention and involvement of all employ-
ees; more than that, however, it added the importance of commitment
and conviction.

It did so in a very optimistic spirit. The somewhat unspecific definition
of culture might have been conducive to this project. Enthusiasm is
needed to win hearts and minds, and enthusiasm sometimes thrives on
what is not specified very clearly.

The notion of culture thus became a vehicle for a broad spectrum of
factors, partly intangible, which nevertheless had to be mobilized in order
to enhance quality and excellence (Harvey and Stensaker 2008).
Organizations cannot leave important factors to chance.

The underlying tension between structure, surveillance, and mea-
surement control, on the one hand, and culture, conviction, and enthu-
siasm on the other—in other words between the “hard” and “soft”
forms of control in organizations—went unresolved. It continues to be
an issue in organizations, whether or not the tension is articulated
clearly (Dahler-Larsen 2015; Laubli Loud and Mayne 2013). While it
is easy to agree that the post-hoc quality measurement typical of the
early days of industrialism is no longer viable, a “culture-oriented”
approach can be seen as an attractive alternative (Harvey and Stensaker
2008), but process-oriented inspection regimes also claim to address
the problem of how to secure quality in a preemptive manner before
quality problems occur. In this view, quality assurance, inspection, and
audit resemble risk management, which is famous for its own set of
problems regarding the social definition of risk (Beck 1992; Power
1997, 2016).

So, not long after industrialism made it clear that quality was an aspect
of human-made products, and thus became something for which some-
one could be held accountable, a new era was inaugurated in which the
control of quality became a problematic industry itself.

Quality, Service, and Audits

As the overall economic structure in modern society gradually puts extra
emphasis on the service industry, quality also becomes important in that
sector. Around 1970, debate addressed whether service is so different
from production that it deserves its own notions of quality; for example,
service is produced and consumed simultaneously. It cannot be stocked.
Services are processes, not products. They are perishable and somewhat
intangible. They also involve customers in a way that makes it difficult to
measure quality after the conventional model known from the quantifi-
cation of (primary) qualities of physical objects (Reeves and Bednar 1994:
426) as well as after the conventional industrial model. However, Levitt
(1972) argued that the most important problem in services was lack of
consistency; in his view, the technocratic mentality of the manufacturer
should be adopted rather than the so-called erratic elegance of the artisan
(Reeves and Bednar 1994: 427).

In Power's (1996) view, one of the most important keys to understand-
ing the contemporary form and shape of the concept of quality is the
imprint it received from quality control as conceived in industrial pro-
duction (Pflueger 2016; Power 1996). This imprint remains integrated in
the concept as quality wanders into service organizations. With this
imprint, there is a relentless focus on the prevention of quality problems
through detailed norms, inspection, audit, and documentation.

A wide range of notions for how to steer quality in organizations (e.g.
Total Quality Management) is already available. Quality is articulated as
a special function in organizations that also becomes visible as identifi-
able parts of organizational structures. Special quality functions are men-
tioned in job postings related to quality development, quality
measurement, and quality control. Quality managers and quality consul-
tants develop their own particular way of thinking, supported by the
establishment of their own organizational field with paraphernalia such
as conferences, journals, associations, and organizational recipes
(DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Meyer et al. 1994). All that is further
required is a transfer of existing infrastructure for dealing with quality
into the service sector.

38 P. Dahler-Larsen

The corollary imaginary of a perfect social world is one where people
follow handbooks, prescriptions, checklists, and standards. It is also a
world in which inspection is needed and warranted. It is a world that
celebrates consistency with minimal standards (or what might be called
defensive quality). This is not the kind of imaginary you would expect
from someone aspiring to the excellent and sublime (or what we might
call offensive quality).

Power (1996, 1997) explains how quality as a general recipe for an
organization results from an interplay between internal and external
forces. Organizations are subject to external audits, reviews, and control
mechanisms. To manage these external demands, organizations set up
their own departments, offices, and functions. In turn, quality managers
seek to make the organization “auditable” in the light of the external
demands. Quality thus becomes a product of a chain of organizational
procedures as well as an entire set of tools and infrastructures that render
quality inscriptions possible, including surveys, report cards, metrics,
handbooks, and so on. In health care, for example, this instrumentation
of quality has taken place since 1970 (Pflueger 2016: 19). Computerization
helps make the collection and management of large amounts of data
possible.

Documentation enhances the possibilities for external audit. Inspecting
organizations are also inspected. Lindeberg (2007) further explains the
importance of quality documentation by showing how organizations not
only combine internal and external factors but are in fact linked in chains
of control which are surprisingly long and complex. Inspired by Power
and Luhmann, Lindeberg stipulates the existence of a whole differenti-
ated social system that governs quality in a self-referential manner.
Kristoffersen and Lindeberg (2004) show how, at the end of the chain,
quality-bestowing organizations use peer-review and mutual recognition
to seek to bestow legitimacy upon each other and thereby themselves.

This configuration of control mechanisms seems to signify an increas-
ingly high level of abstraction as quality is defined in terms of what orga-
nizations see when looking at other organizations, at a great distance
from actual products and services. It also produces an interesting closure
to the open question as to why quality is not in need of a broader (and,
some would hope: critical) discussion of which values constitute the

A History of the Concept of Quality 39

“goodness” that is so inherent in quality. This discussion becomes func-
tionally superfluous when quality organizations legitimize quality
organizations.

A precondition for organizations inspecting organizations is that qual-
ity has become abstracted from the specificities of goods and services and,
as the social equivalent of this, when organizations dealing with quality
have organized themselves into a self-referential system. Social and politi-
cal legitimacy, one can argue in critical opposition to Luhmanian theory,
cannot be bestowed by a system upon itself.

Quality in the Public Sector

Finally, let us consider the introduction of the discourse of quality into
the public sector. In many countries, this took place from around the late
1980s (Bergh 2011). The introduction of the quality discourse is particu-
larly problematic in the public sector, which deals with “wicked prob-
lems” (Harmon and Mayer 1986). These are problems where people
disagree about the boundary and definition of problems, where multiple
values are involved, and where criteria for the solution of the problem are
contested.

One of the factors making the introduction of the quality discourse
possible is that schools, universities, hospitals, and childcare institutions
have become conceived of as organizations (Ahrne and Brunsson 2008).
In that capacity, they participate in fields of organization in which general
recipes for good and legitimate management are defined and circulated
(Rovik 2005). Furthermore, New Public Management as a movement
helped neutralize the public/private distinction, thereby allowing the
transport of quality regimes inspired by private industry and service into
the management of public service organizations. This move is often inter-
preted as the “pushing” of a new (neoliberal) ideology into the public
sector. Perhaps a collapse of meanings undergirding the belief in the pub-
lic sector as a trustworthy provider of grand narratives about progress,
equality, Dannelse/Bildung, and democracy (Lyotard 1982) had already
happened. Perhaps a vacuum had occurred that helped pull in the quality
discourse. The general positivity of the notion of quality helped take the

40 P. Dahler-Larsen

focus away from what might be lost when other discourses lost attraction.
Quality also helped nullify potential social contradictions and conflicts.
Quality is for all, and apparently without opposition. Perhaps quality
helps portray wicked problems as if they are more tame and more
manageable.

Yet, the quality movement has its own problems in the public sector. A
particular problem related to the notion of quality is its overtones of
excellence (something rare and special, not the ordinary). How can this
aspect of quality be maintained together with a political imagery that
demands the equal rights and worth of all citizens?

Here, the quantification of quality probably plays an important role. If
quality is conceived of as a variable (where the scores in one end of a
continuum are defined as “excellent”), any absolute, categorical contra-
diction is dissolved. There is also intense ideological work going on, espe-
cially in welfare states such as those in Scandinavia to redefine the
quality—quantity relationship so as to enhance an imagery where high
quality can be accessible to everyone. In practice, the working class should
also have access to quality education, “high culture,” and so on (Husz
2012). Others argue, however, that in practice what is formerly seen as
elite higher education cannot simply be extended into mass education
without the addition of resources and without consequences for “qual-
ity.” However, this argument can only be stated carefully—and perhaps
implicitly—inasmuch as quality cannot refer to categories of people.
Quality is, apparently, for all. At the same time, the continued attraction
of the notion of quality rests with its continued reference to something
particularly good.

Modern public organizations are characterized by complexity, specializa-
tion, and fragmentation. In the public sector, the need for the overall
coordination of organizational quality is as pressing as it was in industry.
Quality offers itself as an overarching concept, as do other concepts.
Each has a worldview that vies to become the most prominent principle
for the organization of the organization. We have seen “communication,”

A History of the Concept of Quality 41

“sustainability,” and “innovation” become headlines for such conglomer-
ates of management principles, recipes for legitimate organization, and job
titles. All specialists in all of these areas claim expertise. They want good
salaries, an office close to top management, recognition, and influence.

A particularly prominent set of recipes relates to risk management.
Quality control and risk management share a number of practices,
including audit, inspection, checklists, and control mechanisms. There is
also an overlap in worldviews: Steps should be taken before problems
occur. A defensive view of quality resonates well with a preemptive risk
management perspective. Do not do anything surprising. Stick to the
norms. As risk management develops its own perspective embodied in
conferences, bodies of literature, education programs, and management
positions, there can be competition between a quality management view
and a risk management view. They also tell different stories about the
world. Quality suggests improvement. The risk perspective talks about
how to secure survival in a world that is becoming insecure, unpredict-
able, and violent. The integration of these narratives presumably requires
an interweaving of elements into local stories.

In organizations in the real world, there may be overlaps, displacements,
ploys, and reorganizations under different—sometimes equivocal—head-
lines. Quality is only one of these headlines, albeit an important one.

The abstraction following from meta-regulation that we identified ear-
lier also surfaces in relation to public governance. At least from
around 2005, prominent international organizations such as OECD, the
World Bank, and EU develop publications and guidelines about the
quality not of services but of public regulation (Radaelli and de Francesco
2007). At the same time, the ideals regarding regulation are pushed in a
particular direction and regulation is depoliticized (Erkkila and Piironen
2014). Quality does its best to hold on to the role of meta-regulator.

What the History of Quality Has Brought

Quality has a long, rich history. It has gained in prominence. Its journey
from a classical philosophical dialogue into a discourse relevant for mil-
lions of teachers, managers, consumers, journalists, consultants, evalua-

42 P. Dahler-Larsen

tors, and others—if not for the structuration of our society as such—is
nothing less than amazing.

Its flexibility and its ever-positive loading (since 1300 onwards) have
contributed to its successful history over the centuries. The incredible
success of the concept is not due to its precision, because it is not very
precise. Its meanings are rich, varied, and change over time. Some impor-
tant significations remain, while others are lost. The dimensions in the
concept of quality are partly in tension with each other, such as the offen-
sive/maximalist and defensive/minimalist versions of quality. The impli-
cations of reaching for the stars versus not falling below minimum
standards are very different. They represent quite different aspirations
and fears, quite different mentalities, and set in motion different practical
initiatives, but both can be appealed to using the notion of quality.

Some issues remain unsettled, such as the ontological versus the
practical-methodological position regarding the distinction between pri-
mary (measurable) and secondary (non-measurable) qualities.

Some of the domains in which quality occurs, such as the moral/
esthetic and the epistemological, are also in underlying tension or have
not resolved their differences.

The underlying warrants for when and how quality implies statements
of the good have not been in focus among philosophers. This lacuna
regarding valuation stands in stark contrast to the many pages one finds
about the epistemology of qualia, particularly the categories of primary
and secondary sense qualities. While the link between quality and knowl-
edge has been an object of intense philosophical scrutiny, the incorpora-
tion of value into quality is under-cultivated and under-reflected.
Sometimes, the value-laden aspect of quality surfaces when the utility for
customers is compared to price (Reeves and Bednar 1994). Here, value is
evidently reduced to its economic sense.

Apparently, quality has been able to live a life over the course of centu-
ries without interference in the type of philosophical and practical discus-
sions we otherwise have about values. In recent years, the abstraction,
reification, and self-referentiality of quality systems have contributed to
parking this issue. From the perspective of social and political science, the
tacit neglect of important value issues is exactly what demands our atten-

A History of the Concept of Quality 43

tion. The lack of explicit attention to controversies about values is sur-
prising to an evaluator who is trained to see such deliberations as an
integral element in any proper evaluation process. One might also won-
der how it has been possible to maintain a separation between two key
domains in the concept of quality: the domain that deals with values
versus the domain that deals with epistemology (except in one very
important sense, which is that quality is infused with ever-present posi-
tivity). Remember that these domains were linked together in the days of
Plato. Has quality become a way of vot thinking about values and not
linking values and cognition? Is the use of the term quality in fact a func-
tional replacement of debates we could otherwise have?

The history of the concept of quality has produced a rich variety of
meanings, which one can draw from, often without further specification,
and often with positive stardust attached. In a rhetorical perspective, the
notion of quality is attractive because even a carelessly selected set of
meanings of quality is never negatively loaded. At the same time, given its
many layers and avenues of meaning, the concept of quality itself can be
used haphazardly, without specification and clarification. Ambiguity and
positivity go hand in hand.

When the discourse of quality also enters everyday realities, we can
imagine these realities becoming “trading zones” (Stark 2009) for differ-
ent notions of quality. So, in practical situations, people will have to deal
with the specificities of quality or qualities.

Rather than merely subscribing to a myth of general positivity, I shall
propose that quality can be good in particularly selective ways, in specific
situations, and relative to varying perspectives. I shall delineate nine such
perspectives. I do not suggest that in a situation in daily life, only one
perspective counts. I propose that a responsible evaluative statement
about quality would be based on some kind of explicitly and reflexive
reference to these nine perspectives or a frame of reference of a similar
sort rather than merely a vague, unspecified notion of something good.
In that sense, a vocabulary of quality perspectives can be used to analyze
what an otherwise broad, vague, or multifaceted concept might mean
and which potential tensions might be at play among its many
meanings.

Quality Perspectives

Why Distill Quality Perspectives?

The previous chapter showed that, in its totality, the concept of quality is
enormously rich. It carries a number of meanings, some of which are in
tension with each other. Although its ever-present connotation of something
good is always attractive in social use, the concept of quality in toto is, if
explicated, too big and unhandy to move around with. The only place
where all meanings of quality meet is in the dictionary. In practice, people
do not look up all these meanings; they usually talk about something less.

The manner in which quality is rhetorically condensed into something
more specific is particularly interesting because of the ever-present goodness
in quality. In all evaluative contexts, it is appropriate to ask: What do we
mean by “good”? Good in what sense? Good with respect to what? While
the previous chapter took a historical-thematic approach, the following
analysis is conceptual-analytical. The previous analysis was diachronic. The
following is synchronic: Which perspectives do we have today?

A quality perspective is a way of seeing and talking about quality that
conceptually highlights a particular aspect. It is what Weick (in Dean and
Bowen 2000) would call a rudimentary structuring of further selections.
A perspective makes one look in one direction rather than another in

order to find what is relevant and meaningful. It resembles what is called
frames in policy analysis (Fischer 2003).

The nine quality perspectives presented in this chapter all resonate with
literature. They also resonate with viewpoints you can meet in practical
life, although not always in a pure form. The practical world is ambiguous.
People do not always talk about quality in a systematic manner; in fact,
they may talk more in terms of quality “scripts,” which are tentative and
implicit grips on what quality might mean in the situation at hand.

Expressed in the terminology of quality scripts and quality perspec-
tives, I can imagine several sources of ambiguity. One is that a quality
script is held intuitively. It remains implicit. It does not “clinch” in terms
of a particular quality perspective.

People might talk about quality as if they subscribe to one particular
quality perspective without taking responsibility for both plusses and
minuses related to that perspective. They may even shift between views
from one sentence to another. I could imagine a rhetorical strategy that
evokes quality but which benefits from “floating” around and seeking to
subscribe to the benefits of several perspectives without binding
commitments to the blind spots or disadvantages of any of them. It
would be similar to cherry picking all of the advantages of several
perspectives and not taking responsibility for any disadvantages.

If people want a little bit from one perspective and a little bit from
another but they prefer not to think of the downsides of any of the
involved perspectives, we can bring clarity to a discussion about quality
by offering a purified, ideal-typical list of pros and cons of each
perspective.

When quality scripts are made articulate, reflected, explicit, and justi-
fied, they often come close to one of the quality perspectives covered in
this chapter. I shall deal with each of these perspectives as systematically
as possible—let us take clarity as far as we can. Only then can quality
perspectives allow us to lay bare choices that are made explicitly or
implicitly when talking about quality in practice.

In practical life, we may not afford the luxury of just choosing between
such ideal types, but after an analysis of ideal types, we at least think more
clearly about choices, pros, and cons. We also learn something about the
concept of quality as such if it really hosts all of these nine perspectives.

To get to that insight, however, it is necessary to analyze each perspec-
tive in a cool, systematic manner. This chapter offers a definition of each
perspective. I will describe what that perspective identifies as a typical
quality problem. I will describe in which value framework the quality
perspective makes sense and which kind of actors would be likely to find
that quality perspective meaningful. I do this having in mind that the
identities of actors are not given but can be co-constructed, that modern
human beings play multiple, shifting roles, and that actors must create
alliances through arguments that convince others. Nevertheless, the
rough sketch of the (socially rooted) perspective from which a quality
perspective makes sense is a heuristic key; it helps us understand what
sense a perspective may make in the social world. Among the factors
which keeps a quality perspective rooted in social life is how well it fits
with the normative landscape in the social context at hand and how easily
the perspective can be made operational so that it can produce data, facts,
comparisons, assessments, and recommendations which paves the way
for practical action.

So even if the main emphasis in this chapter is conceptual rather than
historical, the conceptual analysis is not disconnected from social realities.
In fact, the emphasis upon each of the quality perspectives may shift over
time (Harvey and Stensaker 2008). Near the end of this chapter, I will
return to the question of whether particular trends in our time tend to
favor particular quality perspectives over others.

A caveat: Perspectives are not categories of things; rather, they are ways
of looking at things. The fact that a given phenomenon plays a role in one
quality perspective does not mean that it is totally excluded from another.
Perspectives do not categorize phenomena; they merely typify different
ways of looking them. As such, perspectives “enroll” phenomena and
provide them with a definite meaning. Two or more perspectives may
overlap in making judgments about an object. However, coincidental
overlap is not the same as conceptual convergence.

Rhetorical devices such as totum pro pars may even be applied to
accomplish alliances between otherwise diverse perspectives. It is analyti-
cally imperative, however, to take the totum out of the totum pro pars
rhetorical figure. Parts should be understood as parts—no more, no less.
Once the relativity of perspectives is revealed, we can then discuss whether

each and every part deserves the same slice of the overall positivity
connected to quality.

Without a notion of quality perspectives, it would be impossible to
recognize differing principles as fundamentally different. The
precondition necessary for this endeavor is clarity. However, one could
philosophically imagine a commitment to a particular quality perspective
that still did not lead to a fixed, stable, and secure notion of quality. The
metaphor for this project is peeling an onion without ever getting to the
core. Is there a stable, essential center of meaning inside a particular
quality perspective, meaning that continued work within a perspective
will only confirm and verify one operational definition of quality? Or is
continued, loyal work within the perspective in fact unable to hold a
fixed version of quality in place? The ambition is to get as much clarity
as possible, but also to recognize that clarity cannot be imposed on what
is messy and ambiguous.

Bearing in mind that quality perspectives can surely be carved out in
different ways (Harvey and Green 1993; Reeves and Bednar 1994), here
is my list.

Quality as an Accomplishment of Official
Political Goals

Definition and Meaning

In this perspective, quality is defined as the accomplishment of set goals
and objectives, which are qualified in a particular democratic way. Only
goals and objectives that have been officially sanctioned in a legitimate
system of government based on representative democracy are considered
valid (Vedung 1997). This principle works as a bulwark against actors in
a polity pursuing their own private goals. In education, for example,
quality is not just what teachers and pupils think it is, but rather the
achievement of goals in a curriculum that have been approved by
legitimate authorities in a parliamentary chain of control.

Quality as an accomplishment of official political goals is not only
relevant from the perspective of politicians, but also relevant for citizens

Quality Perspectives 51

who have legitimate expectations that organizations in the public sector
will deliver what politicians have promised (Vedung 1997).

Many problems contribute to the lack of fulfillment of goals. If humans
are lazy or forgetful, they will not focus the right amount of energy on the
accomplishment of goals. Next, research in policy implementation has
demonstrated that organizations hesitate to implement what has been
officially decided. A number of problems result froma lack of communication,
lack of enforcement of policy, and lacking knowledge or capacity among key
people in all of the stages of the chain of implementation. There may also be
deeper political reasons, including ideological tensions or conflicting interests
that explain why some stakeholders hesitate to work for the accomplishment
of officially binding policy goals. All of these difficulties should be overcome.
The very meaning of a quality perspective that ties into official goals is to
serve as a reminder of the continued relevance of these goals.

The goals are politically and legally binding even without the notion of
quality. The term quality is therefore not mandatory in this context.
There can be policy research, implementation research, and evaluation
without the term quality. The term quality is nevertheless frequently
occurring in policy contexts. Presumably, it adds an extra layer of
positivity to policy and its implementation, thereby contributing to the
reduction of opposition.

Limitations and Ambiguities

‘There are a number of problems with using policy goals as quality criteria.

Goals can be multiple or unclear, in which case they do not constitute
a precise framework for operationalization of evaluation criteria, which
implies finding appropriate and valid indicators of quality. Unclarity can
even be a good thing in policy. The fact that many recent public sector
reforms are enunciated as “quality reforms” suggests that there is presum-
ably good reason to appeal to the concept of quality in a policy context.
When a policy issue is conceived of not in classical policy terms but as a
question of “lack of quality” in schools, universities, hospitals, and so on,
the rhetorical function may be exactly to seek to mobilize the masses
while presenting the reform as a generally good thing, not highlighting

52 P. Dahler-Larsen

specific goals and value choices except for the enhancement of “quality.”
That is why governments of varying political colors seek to implement one
quality reform after another. Quality is a concept without opposition, but
it is also something that always requires improvement.

Whenever there is demand to measure and operationalize quality, any-
thing that appears to be quantifiable, such as “results-based” quality, eas-
ily takes precedence overa careful delineation of quality as accomplishment
of goals, which may be less easy to measure (Bergh 2011), although the
slippage between the two may pass unnoticed.

Bereiter (2002) reminds us, however, that an operationalization of a
hitherto un-operationalized concept changes the concept at hand.
Paradoxically, this mechanism is sometimes an inherent part of
policymaking. The responsibility for figuring out what overarching goals
might mean in a particular context is occasionally delegated to lower
levels ofthe implementation chain. Predictably, ambiguous transformations
of meanings follow.

Political systems can be polycentric and exert multilevel governance.
Given a fragmented, multilevel, and sometimes global configuration of
systems of governance, the political chain of representation in which this
perspective finds legitimacy is sometimes difficult to identify. It is
therefore not always entirely clear when policy speaks in the name of
quality or whether quality speaks in the name of policy.

Although OECD consists of nations with independent governments,
it does not speak for governments; nevertheless, OECD statements are
often influential in political discourse. While political chains of command
may be unclear in complex governance structures, one often has the sense
that producers of quality statements speak as if authority is clear. Harvey
and Stensaker (2008) find considerable ambiguity when quality is referred
to in an international context, across national governments with their
own politically defined notions of quality. So, lack of clarity is one
problem with defining quality in terms of official goals. Another is lack of
attention to side effects.

With official goals and objectives as the only valid criteria, side effects are
not considered. Official goals are frozen ideas. Learning about side effects is
not included. Goals are sanctioned at official meetings, imprinted in strategies
and policy documents, and difficult to change. Large-scale organizations

Quality Perspectives 53

with official goals as their guiding legitimate principles talk more than they
listen; they struggle with feedback and responding to it quickly.

Three is a parallel issue relating to the distinction between explicit and
implicit goals (Dérner 1996). In a rigorous understanding of this
perspective, where quality is defined as the accomplishment of goals, only
initially articulated (i.e. explicit) goals can count. Otherwise, subsequent
assessments would be based on guesswork. As life unfolds, however,
people gain experience, and some of this experience does count in an
evaluative perspective, also politically, but it sometimes counts in relation
to criteria that were not initially stated as important. Why not? Because
life is unpredictable and human beings only know particular criteria are
relevant when there is a clash between these criteria and specific experiences.
Without these experiences, the corresponding criteria do not matter at all.

Here is a practical example. A person accepts a demanding job in
another city because it helps her achieve (explicit) professional goals. She
commutes too much and does not get enough sleep. Her new lifestyle
gives her stress symptoms. Only because of this experience does she begin
to include her health in her career decisions. It was hitherto taken for
granted. She held it as an implicit goal.

An analysis of implicit goals has much in common with an analysis of
side effects. However, the analysis of implicit goals is one step more
advanced. In principle, side effects can be positive or negative, foreseen or
unforeseen with reference to a given set of goals and preferences. The
notion of implicit goals, however, reminds us that goals and preferences
themselves may be dynamic depending on the flow of experiences. Their
degree of articulation changes because their relevance changes over time.
It is not only the effects which matter but also our changing constitution
of them as relevant.

Official goals are sometimes met with resistance by local people who
work with the implementation of policy. The counterargument from
advocates of the “quality as official goals” perspective is that political
legitimacy (remember, this is representative democracy) trumps what is
defined locally as relevant. In that case, “quality” may in fact become
more contested than the ideal-typical perspective suggests. However, if
local contestation is strong enough or politicians wish to take local
experiences into account, policies are sometimes adapted or revised.

If politics includes the making of collective experiences in the face of a
dynamic reality, a perspective that only considers initial and explicit goals
is unsophisticated. However, the clear legitimacy of the perspective of
quality defined as the accomplishment of official goals rests with the
initial, explicit goals. When official goals are ripe for change but have not
officially been explicitly changed, an evaluative gaze that seeks legitimacy
in official goals is caught in an interesting situation where it seeks firm
ground but finds potential instability.

In sum, we have found a number of reasons why a perspective that
defines quality as the accomplishment of political goals may be much less
clear and stable than would initially appear. Goals may be unclear, their
origins and interpretations be distributed in unclear ways, implicit goals
or side effects may complicate the picture, and the dynamic relationship
between experiences and the articulation of experience may make the
perspective practically and philosophically unstable.

as an Accomplishment of Official
Political Goals

The context most favorable to this perspective is one where a political
authority and the goals it sets are seen as legitimate. Furthermore, there
should be stable social environment so that goals do not change too often
and too much. Finally, goals should be articulated clearly in a way that

Quality as Utility
Definition and Meaning

Especially in the sphere of industrial production, it has been common to
define quality in terms of utility. The idea is that although products can
be characterized in terms of a number of properties, which can be more
or less controlled in the production process, a production-oriented view

Quality Perspectives 55

does not pay attention to how the product can be used. Simple little
products have proven incredibly useful. Similarly, just because particular
products can be mass-produced, it does not follow that they are useful for
anyone.

This observation has paved the way for a utility-oriented approach to
the definition of quality. As notions of quality expanded beyond the
sphere of industrial production, the utility-oriented approached made
the same journey. In education, for example, the utility-oriented view
would say that it is of less importance whether a particular educational
program lives up to symbolic traditions of the past and whether a group
of professionals feel that the education program is consistent with their
values. Instead, what counts is that students learn competencies that are
useful in solving real-world problems. If they do, they are also likely to
find gainful employment. The employability of its students is therefore
used as a proxy for the quality of an education program.

The underlying assumption is that the concept of utility enables one to
“cut through” some superfluous layers of symbolism and. subjectivity.
There is a functional reality in utility.

This notion of utility is reminiscent of earlier distinctions made in
economic theory between exchange value and use value. Whereas
exchange relates to the value of a good in exchange for other goods on the
market, the use value of a good relates to how it makes life livable or
pleasant for human beings. Use value stands in a relation to human needs.
Utility-oriented economic theory provides the ideological superstructure
for such thought. Ideologically, the notion of quality as utility has gained
considerable traction as the ideology of economic thinking permeates
modern society.

Limitations and Ambiguities

It is tempting to see exchange value as a social construction defined by the
market in contradistinction to use value understood as a real function,
but the distinction does not hold. Once again using the example of
education: The “exchange value” of an education program may be that it
gives entry into another program that is known to lead to income, status,

56 P. Dahler-Larsen

success, or other aspects of life to which human beings strive. Although
one may criticize some education programs or policies for being excessively
oriented toward careers and incomes, the problem is not that careers and
incomes are artificial constructions. In the search for “exchange value,”
people invest in bonds, diamonds, housing, and education, and they do
not do so because these phenomena are artificial in any sense. These
phenomena have a “use” or “use value” because they have an exchange
value. In turn, as the sociology of economic life has shown, exchange
values hinge on property rights, on the organization of markets, and so
on. Ultimately, there is no way of talking about the “value” of something,
be it exchange value or use value, without running into a wall of social
organization. For example, the “use value” of a car rests with the existence
of infrastructure, roads, gas, and police.

It is therefore an illusion to assume that quality as utility rests on a
rock-solid reality; it is as fluid and evanescent as the social reality within
which it is embedded.

The definition of “use value” therefore ran into problems when con-
fronted with the views of customers on specific goods and products.
Unless expanding the term “use value” almost ad infinitum, it is difficult
to explain why consumers are fascinated with diamonds and why they
pay extraordinary prices for brand products. In turn, we may understand
these phenomena if we inquire into the “secondary qualities” of products
as subjectively perceived in the light of the needs, expectations, and
wishes of users, but that journey can only be made if we are not restricted
by a rigid, reified notion of what constitutes utility.

Social Conditions Favorable to Quality as Utility

This perspective is likely to thrive in combination with a rational view of
the human being. The world should be one in which symbols and the
imaginary play a limited role, so utility can be objectively defined in
terms of a few measurable dimensions. Tacitly, a form of authority is
assumed so that utility can be defined for others. If people speak for
themselves, they often have all kinds of wishes and desires.

Quality as the Fulfillment of the Needs,
Expectations, or Wishes of Users

Definition and Meaning

In this perspective, quality is defined as the fulfillment of the needs,
expectations, and/or wishes of users. Although a large part of the historical
roots of this perspective emerged from consumers in a market, I use
“user” more generically. It refers to all of the addressees of a service or
product, including patients, students, clients, and other recipients of
public services.

One of the main justifications for this perspective is that manufactur-
ers and service providers are often large organizations that talk more than
they listen. Their managers and professionals maintain the status quo,
they hold a production-oriented view, they are technically or professionally
privileged, and they speak management language or Latin either
metaphorically or in fact (as do some medical doctors).

The common consequence of these maladies is a lack of attention to
the needs, expectations, and wishes of users. The remedy is more feedback
regarding the same factors.

More attention will not only lead to greater user satisfaction with the
provided services and goods. Differences in power can be reduced. More
legitimacy can be bestowed on the organization that provides the product
or service. And the organization will be able to deliver better services as a
result of a better understanding of the situation of users (Dahlberg and
Vedung 2001).

Here is an example. A pharmaceutical company had invented a new
product that effectively reduced symptoms of psoriasis. It was marketed
as a cream in a tube.

At one point, the company hired anthropologists to study how patients
used this product in everyday life. The anthropologists found patients
putting an old newspaper on the floor. They would then open the tube,
leave it on the newspaper, step on it to make the cream come out, and
then use the newspaper to apply the cream to their skin. When asked why
they did so, patients would explain that because of the arthritis and weak

58 P. Dahler-Larsen

joints that many of them suffered from in addition to their psoriasis,
their hands were not strong enough to squeeze the thick cream out of
the tube. The marketing people then went back to production and told
them that patients had a problem with getting the cream out of the
tube. Funny enough, said the production people, because we have a
problem with getting it in. The next immediate step was to transform
the product into a gel that could be applied much more easily. The
company then also developed a new version of the product where the
active ingredients were put into a spray so that patients could apply the
product to parts of their body they were unable to reach, such as the
back. However, the company had to work on a new kind of mechanism
that would release the product at a lighter touch compared to conven-
tional spray cans.

Limitations and Ambiguities

The perspectives of various users are not always compatible. As Julnes
(2012) has convincingly shown, the need for an algorithm synthesizing
various views is situation-specific. In public services where users share
the same service, this need is indeed pressing. One example is a public
library in which some users are expecting church-like tranquility.
Others might use the library for group work (students) or play
(children). These groups cannot all agree on appropriate noise levels.
Yet they share the same public space, so some solution must be found.
Care facilities for the elderly are often marked by an inherent tension
regarding the use of common spaces, such as dining rooms. Some
elderly persons find it uncomfortable to eat together with persons with
severe problems with behavior and eating. At the risk of loneliness,
some prefer eating in their private rooms. A “user perspective” without
attention to balances and dilemmas between different users is sometimes
an abstraction.

Instead, it makes more sense to understand quality statements issued
from this perspective as a specific interpretation of a composite configura-
tion of needs, preferences, and wishes informed by a particular under-
standing of value priorities in the situation at hand.

The plasticity of the user perspective renders it particularly prone to
someone stepping in, interpreting what users need, and speaking on their
behalf.

When data about the preferences and assessments expressed by users
are quantified, they sometimes take on a particularly “objective” quality
that possibly stems from their standardization and their similarity to
measurable primary qualities of objects. Consequently, there can be a
stark difference between objectified and typified viewpoints from average
users, on the one hand, and the views and lives of specific users on the
other (Schwandt and Burgon 2006).

Users have experience but no sure way of knowing what to expect.
Expectations are highly amenable to social construction, if not
manipulation. Users may put too much emphasis on kindness and
comfort in service delivery at the expense of the professional knowledge
and skill which characterizes good teaching, health care, social services,
and so on.

Users may not always be aware of what will satisfy them in the future.
While needs, expectations, and wishes all help define what counts as
quality from a user perspective, they are not the same. What users
immediately wish may not be good for them or may not be a good
predictor for that with which they later turn out to be happy. Henry Ford
is quoted for saying that if he had asked people what they wanted, they
would have said faster horses. Similarly, many customers reportedly
rejected a pilot version of the Xerox machine because they believed carbon
paper provided the same result (Sower and Fair 2005: 15).

For example, a qualitative inquiry into travelers’ perspectives on train
travel revealed that what they desired most was seating with as much
privacy as possible. Nevertheless, when asked to recall their most enjoyable
train rides, they typically mention coincidental meetings with new,
interesting people.

Users may need something they cannot explicate, which becomes
painstakingly clear when they cannot talk reasonably about their own
situation (e.g. small children, Alzheimer’s patients). These instances
exemplify to an extreme extent that deriving a definition of quality then
requires an interpretation of what the world might look like from the

Indeed, the very definition of a legitimate “user” of something is con-

sanctioned and change over time. The normative validity of a given user

quality statement based on this perspective can therefore be caught
between the norms of the past and the norms of the future.

A user perspective can be evoked to induce change in business strate-

public policy can use instruments emulating market mechanisms and

ongoing user feedback to which service providers must respond (e.g.
student evaluations, customer satisfaction surveys), with the option of
publishing this data to inform the choices of potential future users. The
voices of users, such as patients, can thus be used to hold service providers

Quality as Expert Judgment
Definition and Meaning

Some objects of quality assessment are complex. A holistic view of quality
is sometimes called for. Users do not always have the insights and the
experience to fully appreciate all aspects of a phenomenon. Especially in
the arts, music, and literature, quality assessments require connoisseurship
(Eisner 1976). Quality inscriptions come in the form of reviews. Reviews
have genre characteristics and are performances in themselves.

A good expert performs in the same way as a connoisseur. Through
training and expertise, experts cultivate their sensibilities toward subtle
aspects of quality in a quality performance. Expertise rests with perceptual
differentiation and insight, which is trained through experience (Eisner
2003). In contrast to a user or consumer of music, who may like or dislike
a particular piece, the expert appreciates and articulates valuations of
genre, composition, style, tonality, performance, improvisation, and
other complex aspects of musical artistry. While arts, music, and literature
are particularly illustrative of the role of connoisseurship, the notion of
expertise evidently also extends into such domains as technology, science,
and policy.

Expertise is shaped not only by knowledge, insight, professionalism,
and experience but also by values and theories (Eisner 2003) and by taste.
The definition of “holistic” assessment is up to the expert. Consequently,
the expert may talk about any or all of the aspects of a performance found
relevant—holistic or not.

The uniqueness of this perspective is that it is the expert who decides.
Explicit criteria may or may not be used. Parts or whole objects can be
evaluated. The strength of quality as expert judgment is that the trained
expert is trusted to have a particularly qualified view and remains free to
focus on whatever aspect is found relevant.

Expert judgments have an educational function. Experts can some-
times see what others cannot, and they can train the perceptual differen-
tiation of non-experts and experts-to-be by appreciating qualities in an
object that otherwise would have been neglected. Scientific peer review is

66 P. Dahler-Larsen

one example of a practice based on expert judgments and a good illustra-
tion of how much one can learn from experts in a field.

Expert judgments require attention and care. The self-understanding
of experts is therefore important and more than a sign of self-ascribed
importance and status. The self-conscious experts will continue to
cultivate their skills and be humble about the limits of their expertise
(Lamont 2009).

Limitations and Ambiguities

While experts may produce insightful quality statements, their reliability
may be very low. Any two experts, even within the same field, often
produce divergent evaluative statements. Expert views rely on training
and theory, and these theories can be very diverse in some fields. Experts’
views sometimes reflect what is a fashionable trend in a field.

Expertise is limited and relative to fields, disciplines, and subjects.
Economists may talk about ethical issues without hesitation, but that
does not mean that their expert status will be respected. Here, the problem
is that “field” is not a category of persons, but rather a perspective on life
defined by a discipline or theory. Many phenomena can thus be viewed
from multiple perspectives, each with their own experts.

Some experts become defensive about their status. They are sometimes
insensitive toward those who do not share their insights. Some experts
restrict their view to a particular paradigm that was dominant in their
field in the formative years of their career. Some architects design houses
that are meant more to impress other architects than to fulfill the needs
of commissioners or future users.

Returning to scientific peer review: Even though it might well be the
“least bad” way to assess the quality of an academic product, it has serious
flaws. Peer experts tend to conform conservatively with existing, already-
established paradigms. Peers are connected in networks that cite and
review each other's work. Two peer reviews of the same text may be at
odds with each other and give different recommendations for
improvement. Peer reviews are sometimes terrible performances in terms
of structure, care, and courtesy.

recent years exemplify attempts at reducing the power of expert judg-

mechanistic or standard-based perspectives on quality.

Judgment

Under social conditions favorable to this perspective, expertise is recog-

rich supply of experts in various areas. There may be rules for how and
when experts are used, including rules regarding limits of expertise and
conflicting interests. If experts disagree among themselves, there should
be social space for a respectable deliberation among them so that their
collective judgment can be respected by themselves and others (Lamont

If organizations hire experts to produce quality assessments, these
organizations may run into problems if the judgments of these experts are
unreliable or in disagreement. Even if experts are guided by common
guidelines and manuals, they may go their own way. It has proven difh-
cult to make experts comply with such requirements. The expert judg-
ment is difficult to codify.

The expert perspective also runs into problems when experts are scarce
or their time is precious or too expensive. A pressing example is peer
review of scientific work, where it has become difficult to find the neces-
sary number of peer reviewers for the increasing amount of research eval-

bibliometric measures in research can to some extent be explained by the
alleged subjectivity and the costs associated with peer review.

compared to the “common opinion regime” (sales and popularity

Quality as Compliance with Standards
Definition and Meaning

A standard is a formula that prescribes uniformity among a class of prod-
ucts or services or in the processes of their production. Moreover, some
definitions mention that standards are “for common and repeated use”
and that they are “aimed at achievement of the optimum degree of order
in a given context” (Brunsson and Jacobsson 2000: 15).

A key moment in the history of standards is industrial production,
where a need emerged for substitutable machine parts. Standards help
create uniformity.

While an official definition of standards emphasizes that they are
“established by consensus and approved by a recognized body” (Brunsson
and Jacobsson 2000: 15), there are in fact many bodies issuing standards.
They can emerge out of authority, custom, or consent (Stake 2004: 13).
Sometimes standards emerge as social constructs out of a particular,
calculative practice. An average is sometimes used as a standard for
comparison even if there is no “consensus” or “recognized body” that has
reflected normatively regarding the appropriateness of that standard.

Standards assume many forms, such as forms of planning, ways of
organizing, product specifications, ways of measuring things, milestones
against which things can be evaluated, and ways of legitimizing things
such as certification and accreditation. Standards can be more or less
mandatory. Standards can be binding in practice, even if there is no legal
sanction for non-compliance. Most people in the West type on QWERTY
keyboards, even if there is no sanction for not doing so. It is just extremely
dificult to purchase any other kind of keyboard. This observation
highlights an important aspect of standards: They are not always adopted
because they are technically superior, but they are useful when some
degree of compatibility and coordination is needed within a larger socio-
technical system.

Quality Perspectives 69

In the light of quality understood as compliance with standards, a
quality problem is defined in terms of unexplained variation around a
standard.

Even ifa particular standard is not necessarily a good indicator of all of
the aspects of quality, stark deviation from a standard is often indicative
ofa problem requiring attention, say advocates of standards. One example
is the monitoring of accounting data for institutions providing child care.
If one of these homes has extraordinary costs on items such as vehicles,
travel, or representation, it may indicate unsound management practices
or improper use of public funds.

Monitoring of the prescription practices of general practitioners
against a simple average may help identify a few who overprescribe due to
inappropriate relations with drug addicts. At the very least, a strong
deviation from the standard requires an explanation, and usually also
action. The advocates of standards therefore often emphasize the
operational advantages of standards.

Notice how closely a quality perspective and definition of a problem
are linked. Once you see the world through the perspective of standards,
quality objects displaying variation immediately pose a problem if this
variation is “unexplained.” The perspective puts an explanatory burden
on those who are responsible for the quality object. This is true for all
quality perspectives, but the rhetorical move is even more smoothly made
with the standards perspective, as it depends less on an external normative
justification, such as “legitimate political goals” or “real user needs.”

In the standards perspective, “not normal” is enough. A quality prob-
lem defined in this way does not rely on a lengthy justification. Once you
measure some dimensions of quality objects, some will almost by defini-
tion score above or below average or above or below a statistically deter-
mined regression line. Voi/a! Unexplained variation! Standards are
extremely functional for the lazy evaluator; or, more specifically, the
evaluator who is not keen on lengthy arguments about the deeper origins
of values. Instead, standards can be in functional operation quickly with-
out much deliberation and/or afterthought.

As Reeves and Bednar (1994: 431) explain: “Conformance to specifi-
cations is the most parsimonious, appropriate, and easily measured

definition of quality. The more subjective definitions of excellence, value,
and meeting and/or exceeding expectations become unnecessary.”

Standards do not always materialize just because we need them. In
most evaluation work, there are no fixed standards and no good way of
fixing them (Stake 2004: 8). Here, Stake presumably means normatively
good, broadly socially acceptable, and/or good with respect to a thorough
understanding of the local situation at hand. In that respect, he is right.
However, standards may function as standards (prescribing uniformity)
even without such normative justification. Functionally speaking, there
may even be “too many” good ways of fixing standards. As Stake (2004:
227) says: “Often standardization is increased not because the quality of
activity is enhanced, but because standardized activity is more easily
monitored and controlled.”

My point—that standards sometimes emerge almost involuntarily out
of social processes—can be illustrated with examples. A municipality
kept digital records of the visits that caretakers made to elderly people
who remained in their own homes. The records documented whether
standards were met both in terms of frequency and duration of visits.
These factors were measured against standards of units of care that were
allocated to the elderly person based on an analysis of need (defined by an
“allocation specialist,” not the caretakers themselves). An administrator
explained that a main function of this meticulously standardized
documentation was that it provided a strong defense against filed
complaints from the relatives of the elderly who questioned whether
sufficient care was provided. It was always possible to explain that care
was allocated according to standardized principles and, furthermore, that
a given number of visits of a given duration actually took place. This
provided a strong argument against stories about “lack of care” that
sometimes circulated among the relatives of the elderly and who relied, in
turn, on the faulty memory of their elderly kin. The somewhat tragic
paradox here is that standards and standardized documentation were not
primarily needed to manage care but to defend the care provider from
complaints from (someone who claimed to represent) recipients of care.

Another example: Universities are interested in documenting the
impact of social research. Researchers are interested in keeping this con-
cept open and multifaceted. Researchers also want recognition of their

contribution to turning research into something with an impact (e.g.

transparency possible. Standards and their ongoing development are
sometimes a result of different principles, such as expertise, user
orientation, representation, and participation (Hallstrém 2000: 93). The
function of standards is to help bring different principles together in a
single organizational form.

coordination and a sense of fairness. If there is a lack of tolerance of
variation (e.g. variations between service providers, between institutions,
or between municipalities), then standards present themselves as the
answer. Certain trends in society, such as the publication of indicator
scores, focus on individual rights, and media representing variations in
service as a great scandal, help make variations intolerable. Standards are
fundamentally helpful in promoting a sense of social order (Furusten
2000: 84) in a manner that may be particularly in tune with contempo-
rary socio-historic tendencies.

Limitations and Ambiguities

The view that quality is the same as compliance with standards is often
criticized. Standards and standardized ways of measuring and testing
things often become goals in themselves. While uniformity becomes an
aspiration (Eisner 1976), its main focus on defensive quality (securing
compliance, not reaching for the stars) stifles creativity and prevents
paradigm-shifting discoveries (Sower and Fair 2005).

If a standards-based definition of quality governs the establishment of
organization structure, reward systems, and human resource practices (e.g.
selection and training in an organization), an escalation of commitment
toward these practices may occur (Galbraith 1983). The standardization
necessary for a conformance-to-specifications definition of quality
sometimes works against an adaptive organization that has the flexibility
to respond to a changing market (Reeves and Bednar 1994: 432).

Understanding quality as compliance with standards, the concept is
deprived of all overtones of the sublime, the extraordinary, the sensitive,
and the carefully calibrated. Consequently, this perspective is antithetical
to productive idiosyncrasy (Eisner 1976). Standards are insensitive to the
particularities of situation and locality, because their very meaning is to
enhance uniformity.

Standards describing services for a category of recipients or users may
not be good descriptors of what the individual members of the category
need or want. Standards established in the name of concerns for a group
of people may not be helpful for specific individuals, but they can still be
justified as “serving the users.” At the same time, standards may in fact
define and limit what individuals can expect.

For example, if a certain number of hours is set aside for a nurse visit-
ing families with newborn babies, a given family may need more or less
time than that. An efficient way of allocating hours would be according
to need, whereas a strict adherence to uniform standards might comply
with management requirements and/or a general sense of justice.

Notice here how standards operate across different types of justifica-
tions or without justification. Standards are often followed by a lack of
clarity about their value basis and legitimacy. Their construction is also

often so lengthy in time and space or their delineation so anonymously

Social Conditions Favorable to Quality as Standards

Quality standards have the most favorable social conditions when the
makers of standards are recognized as legitimate, when standards are
technically and substantially appropriate regarding the objects they
describe, when infrastructures are in place to support standards, when
Opposition to standards is limited, and when the risk of overflows into
other domains does not create controversy (Beuscart and Mellet 2013:
406). Sometimes, there are competing quality conventions. The survival
of a given quality standard depends on many uneven factors, including
bureaucratic or economic interests (Beuscart and Mellet 2013).

Quality as Organizational Recipe
Definition and Meaning

In order to understand the meaning of quality as an organizational rec-
ipe, one must first understand the organizationalization of quality.
Quality sometimes rests not with a particular service but with how the
services are coordinated and delivered, which is best understood as an
organizational phenomenon. Let me give an example: Consider a patient
with Alzheimer’s disease. Her brain makes her believe she is talking care
of small children (as she had many years ago). She now fears that these
children have disappeared for her, since she kept looking for them and
could not find them. An experienced team of psychiatrists is called in
who are specialists in the medication of Alzheimer’s patients. Their task is
complicated, as people respond differently to different types and doses of
medicine: too little medicine would not help her, but too much would
leave her comatose. The specialist team prescribes the calculated optimal

74 P. Dahler-Larsen

dosage based on the existing evidence, but wants to fine-tune it to the
patient’s case after three weeks. In the meantime, her daily caretakers
would make schematic, hour-to-hour observations about her fears,
behavior, attention, and sleep patterns. Based on this feedback, the
medical specialists are able to optimize the medical regime. What worked
in the case at hand was neither the medicine nor the specialists in
themselves; rather, it was the organizational coordination between the
specialists who controlled the medicine and the caretakers who made the
necessary observations and provided feedback to the specialists.

In addition to what is required from medical specialists, it is also organi-
zationally difficult to make sure that busy caretakers produce systematic
observations on an hourly basis (including weekends) unless they all clearly
understand the importance of that endeavor. Everybody should know
where to find the necessary documentation when the specialists return for
their second visit. All this requires organizational coordination.

In most forms of modern service delivery (e.g. health care), service
quality is at least two-dimensional. One dimension relates to the level of
knowledge, expertise, technologies, and so on under the aegis of the
professions. The other is regarding the organizational coordination
between the professions. The need for paying attention to the second
dimension increases as professional services become specialized. Medical
specializations are not logically constructed; some deal with particular
parts of the body, while others deal with particular patient groups defined
by gender or age. Others yet deal with particular disease types. No wonder
there are overlaps. And patients who fall between categories. Organizational
coordination is needed. As a part of this process, professional work is
relocated and reconfigured. The changing, and sometimes less autonomous,
role of professionals cannot be explained only by neoliberalism and New
Public Management. It is a result of a broad and complex set of social fac-
tors (Noordegraf 2016).

Professionals often have problems with organizational coordination
unless it is under their own control, but multi-professional organizations
engaged in cross-disciplinary work need someone to take charge of an
organizational perspective; thus, the organizationalization of quality.

Organizational “meta-craftsmanship” is a concept that suggests how to
handle organizationalization. It ties together quality improvement,

reengineering, customer service, and management.’ Upon recognizing
that organizations have a responsibility for organizational “meta-
craftsmanship” in the coordination and delivery of services (one aspect of
organizationalization), how the organization handles this responsibility
becomes an object of quality itself. How much quality is there in the way
organizations carry out “meta-craftsmanship”? Organizations are
providers of quality, but they are also quality objects. The quality of how
they carry themselves as organizations can be assessed. In a sense, this is a
second-order assessment of quality. The dual nature of the
organizationalization of quality is a result of the fact that the steering of
the provision of quality in coordinated services is itself a quality object.

While meta-craftsmanship may not be a commonly used term, there is
an enormous amount of tools that help describe what it might entail:
project management, coordination, budgeting, organizational develop-
ment, organizational learning, management by objectives, performance
management.

Many terms even emphasize the term quality: quality management,
quality assurance, and total quality management (branded and sold as
Total Quality Management or TQM). These are candidates as guiding
principles for a perspective that defines quality as organizational recipe.
An organizational recipe is a legitimized notion about what constitutes
good management. It is legitimized in organizational fields where norms
of good practice circulate among prestigious organizations, experts,
consultants, and the like (DiMaggio and Powell 1983). Recipes often
come in the form of particular management fashions, and their names
change quickly.

The defining characteristic of organizationalized quality is that in order
to find quality in a given situation, one would look for (the documentation
of) organizational procedures, rules, practices, technologies, and
structures—all in all: recipes—that are more or less implemented and in
place as organizational responses to the challenges of the situation at hand.

To support this documentation, organizations need a special kind
of knowledge that deals with quality systems, information, and

called systematic quality work—seen from the perspective of quality as
organizational recipe.

Limitations and Ambiguities

A fundamental criticism of this perspective relates to its abstraction. It
privileges rules and procedures at the expense of concrete services and
concrete persons. It also privileges documented rules and procedures
above informal ones. This is because the practical function of quality as
an organizational recipe lies in its ability to be externally documented in
the face of auditors, evaluators, inspectors, and others of the same caliber
(Power 1997). Much of management consists of installing procedures
that “make things auditable” toward an external view (Power 1996).

For this reason, quality as organizational recipe is often despised among
professionals in organizations. They often view all of this documentation
as primarily serving managers and auditors and regard the time spent on
documentation as time wasted. They feel that the documentation is less
relevant for “real work.” Yet at least some forms of documentation are
crucial for coordination across time and space (see the initial example of

an Alzheimer patient in this section). In addition, what professionals
sometimes ignore is that given the demands of taxpayers, customers,
clients, competitors, technology, and increasing complexity, classical
professional autonomy simply does not suffice.

An underlying conflict here is one of epistemic perspective.
Organizational managers and their auditors have knowledge interests and
make knowledge claims that are different from those of professionals in
disciplines. The tension between them surfaces when auditors check the
function and operation of organizational recipes that professionals see as
not relevant for the subject matter at hand (e.g. health care, education).
In contradistinction, the power of the auditor’s epistemic regime hinges
on abstractions from particular practices and substantive disciplinary
areas. Otherwise, the knowledge produced by auditors cannot travel and
cannot be made known to external partners.

‘The expertise of the auditor or organizational consultant is constituted
on the basis of a perspective that traverses time and space. In practice, it
is also much easier for the controller to ask for rules, procedures, guidelines,
handbooks, and other documents than to observe the professionals in
practice. The relevance of this abstraction remains a contested issue in
many situations. According to this criticism, auditors check the consis-
tency of documents with other documents but not with other practices.

The “abstraction” that is claimed to be inherent in this perspective is in
fact abstraction compared with the “concrete” practical reality that is
experienced by professionals such as teachers, nurses, and social workers.
Following Latour (1987: 241), for example, there is no reason to assume
that the coordination between different organizational documents is not
a real, very concrete activity. In Latour’s view, there is no “higher order”
or more advanced—or more abstract—cognitive activity involved in
making organizational documentation, calculations, and so on; it is
merely a different set of epistemic and practical endeavors supported by
inscription practices in a particular scale.

The criticism that organizationalized quality is defined by its “abstrac-
tion” is therefore not actually an analysis, it is merely a viewpoint (held by
some, not by others). Latour’s intervention is useful in order to maintain
a focus not on “concrete” “reality” but on the inscription devices seeking
to define reality in particular ways. These inscription devices are likely to

1989). But decoupling or hypocrisy cannot always be maintained.
External audits of organizational recipes are sometimes exercised with

consequences for the organization. The same is true if a performance
audit is linked to money streams and to the reactions among key
stakeholders which influence the reputation or even survival of an
organization (Espeland and Sauder 2007). The impact of external audits
looking at quality as an organizational recipe is therefore often substantial.

While the internal evaluators spend much of their time securing the

have interests themselves and a desire to demonstrate that their work has
real consequences. They are often the arbiters who turn the effects of

recipes into locally relevant solutions and by involving organizational
stakeholders in quality work, such as organizational learning or teamwork

organizations to massage the notion of quality as organizational recipe
into the sinews of daily life in the organization. The tools to do so oscillate
between holistic devices (e.g. integrated learning, systems perspectives,

These numerous practical tools draw on a variety of schools of thought
in organizational theory.

For example, Karl Weick’s theory of sensemaking offers a perspective
in contrast to the most dominant theories of management. Sensemaking
is experience-based and distributed across the organizational landscape. It
is a paradox in organizations that, under crisis and up to potential
disasters, the organizational capacity to make sense of things is diminished
because people’s attention becomes blocked and information-processing
is concentrated at the top of the organizational hierarchy. What is in fact
more needed, in Weick’s view, is an involvement of a variety of experience-
based, interactive forms of sensemaking that allow the organization to
better deal with equivocal phenomena (Weick et al. 2005).

Ina similar vein, there is no such thing as “Total Quality Management.”
“Total” is relevant for external legitimacy but inconsistent with how
sensemaking unfolds in practice (Weick in Dean and Bowen 2000). An
organizational recipe in the spirit of Total Quality Management does not
provide the vocabulary and labels necessary to energize people to explain
to themselves and others what they are doing when they are doing quality
work (Weick 2000). An organizational recipe is faulty when it assumes
that the world is more predictable than it actually is and when it
overspecifies directions and precludes adaptive action (Weick 2000: 164).

The theoretical key uniting Weick’s work on quality with his perhaps
more well-known work on disasters in organizations is the importance of
attending to weak or ambiguous signals, cultivating sensemaking, and
not burying troubles in normalizing standards and procedures.

Weick’s perspective helps explain why the “implementation” of a dom-
inant, comprehensive, or “total” organizational recipe for quality is an
impossible mission and (too) hard work. If an organizational recipe
works, it is because of all of the local sensemaking it sets in motion; not
because of the recipe, as such.

It is therefore difficult to determine the “effectiveness” of an organiza-
tional recipe for quality. Confusion about whether any given organizational
recipe is effective continues as long as the recipe itself is open and

interpretable and most of its use hinges on local interpretations (Ovretveit
2005). The recipe is only a loose idea that has rational appeal, but it has
not been qualified through rough encounters with materiality (Rovik
2005). Those who developed and transported it are not responsible for its
use and consequences. Sometimes, the pushing away of responsibility is a
managerial strategy in itself. Once a particular organizational recipe for
“quality assurance” makes particular aspects of organizations transparent,
managers manage only with transparency, while the responsibility to
make sense out of the recipe and make it work in daily life is delegated to
subordinates (Roberts 2018).

Many, especially professionals, maintain ambivalence when criticizing
quality as an organizational recipe. While it is described as a waste of
time, paper, and money, it actually restructures the organizational reality
in a way that is full of friction when “the rubber hits the road.”

Strides will continue to be made over who has the right to define which
of several organizational perspectives should be dominant. Quality is
sometimes a subordinate part of an organizational recipe with another
name and sometimes a recipe in itself, which may even drive the
development of other recipes (Dean and Bowen 2000). In practice, the
relative status of a recipe for quality may be up for grabs, for example, in
times of austerity, and its anchoring in the organizational landscape may
change so that responsibility for quality is moved (Roberts 2018; Sorensen
and Foged 2015).

The critique that organizational recipes are merely abstract manage-
ment jargon underestimates the many steps that specialists take to weave
their ideas into organizational operations. It also misses reflection about
the philosophical naiveté in saying that some things are “really real” only
if we call them “concrete.”

The strides about quality as an organizational recipe are likely to con-
tinue. While it is true that many factors contribute to a need for organi-
zational coordination of quality, it is also true that many organizational
recipes are simplistic victims of fads in management circles and continue
to cause frustration among those professionals who must deal with con-
flicting demands of organizational recipes, users, professional values, and
limited time and resources.

Quality as Impact
Definition and Meaning

The underlying inspiration for understanding quality as impact often
comes from (a version of) systems theory vocabulary that is easily adopted
in everyday language. In a systems view, there is talk of inputs, processes,
outputs, outcomes, and impacts. Another source of influence comes from
experimental methodology which offers a way in which interventions
and impacts can be causally linked.

From the perspective of this framework, too many people spend too
much time focusing on inputs and processes, and sometimes outputs.
What counts in the long run are outcomes. In the international evaluation
literature, the evaluation of outcomes and impacts is seen as a mature,
well-developed form of evaluation (Furubo et al. 2002). The whole point
of distinguishing between inputs, processes, outputs, and outcomes is
often to motivate people to direct more attention to outcomes and
impacts.

Outputs occur in the interface between the delivery system and the
recipient, while outcomes are understood as the changes in the lives of
people after they have been in contact with some program, intervention,
or service. Outcomes are the long-term changes after the contact is over.

82 P. Dahler-Larsen

Outcomes can be short-term or long-term. Although terminologies
may differ, impact often refers to relatively long-term outcomes.
Furthermore, while “outcomes” may be desired end-states, where the
causal link with the intervention or program is not always implied or
clarified (Funnell and Rogers 2011), the term “impact” usually
incorporates such a causal link. Impacts are causal phenomena. They are
effects of interventions. Impacts and effects are relevant because of their
familiarity with broader notions of effectiveness.

Quality as impact is particularly pertinent in medicine and social work
(where the vocabulary is often one of “interventions”; see below). It is
also becoming pertinent in research, where a typical contemporary
question is how research contributes to society.

Quality as impact may overlap other quality perspectives, such as those
defined by goals, utility, or user needs. A particularly distinctive feature,
however, which the inscription of quality as impact must one way or
another take seriously, is how to account for the causality implied in this
version of quality. This perspective is born with an inherent methodological
challenge, but it also offers a solution.

A randomized controlled trial (RCT) is often seen as the best way to
determine a causal link between an intervention and its impact. Once
evidence for this causal link is available, a number of initiatives are set in
motion to spread best practices. These include education, courses,
supervision and manuals (here quality as impact overlaps with quality as
standards or as organizational recipes). Together, the experimental design
and the strict control of subsequent practices should lead to a maximum
impact. This combination of causal thinking and controlled interven-
tions is unique for the perspective here called “quality as impact.”

Limitations and Ambiguities

The notions of input, process, output, and outcome are less obvious than
they appear. They assume a definition of the system to which these
phenomena are related. System is, however, an analytical construct that
depends to some degree on the perspective of an observer and their pur-
poses (Bateson 1972). The world comprises multiple, interacting systems,

Quality Perspectives 83

and the “outcome” or “impact” does not always give itself. We are interested
in a particular “system” when we want “it” to “do” “something” for us. The
definition of system is relative to a relevance structure. Whether we are
interested in pest control or in the diversity of ecosystems rests with
different relevance structures.

Some interventions influence people who make other interventions,
and it is not always meaningful to assume that all of outcomes rest with
citizens, and that there is a list of correct outcomes that can be used as the
ultimate checklist.

While the notion of impact is often inspired by systems theory, this
theory is not a value theory, so it does not help discriminate between
good/bad impacts. Nor is that distinction made by the methodological
thinking related to the determination of causal links. With quality as
impact, a particular “form,” “paradigm,” or “mentality” is enhanced, but
this is often void of exact criteria unless some criteria are taken for granted
because of social conventions (e.g. in medicine, where cure and survival
rates might do the job). In other areas of life, such as education, there
may be less agreement about conventions. For example, is a more boring
form of education something desirable if it produces better international
rankings of students’ performances?

More often than not, criteria are “brought in” to supplement the sys-
tems view with ideologies or values that are not strictly part of the defini-
tion of quality as impact. While this permeability makes the perspective
open and useful for the transportation of many criteria, the perspective
itself is unable to sort out conflicts between them. In social work, for
example, a particular program might prove to help drug addicts get rid of
hard drugs and escape a life of crime, only to become dependent on
methadone. Whether or not this means “clean’—for not to mention
whether life on methadone is a good life—remains a question for
discussion, unless value questions and policy questions are buried under
the techno-methodological veil of whether or not the intervention is
“evidence-based,” in which case quality-as-impact has succeeded as a self
evident perspective on quality. Strictly logically speaking, however, an
outcome is an outcome in a system the definition of which cannot be
evidence-based.

The indeterminability of value criteria in this perspective remains clearly
visible in many instances. An additional example is the impact of research.
Although difficult to encircle and even more difficult to measure, there is
a need to produce inscriptions of “quality as impact” to justify university
funding in times of austerity. While some believe that the impact of science
can be measured in terms of patents, technological innovation, and the
like, the same criteria are not relevant in social research.

Inspired by the terminology of input, process, output, and outcomes,
some argue that the ultimate goal of social research is the use of research
results in political decision-making. However, this view runs into a
multitude of problems. Some research is basic, whereas other research is
applied. The impact of social research unfolds over different periods.
Social research plays a cultural role as well as an instrumental role (Biesta
2007). It helps interpret social life in a much broader sense than a focus
on political decisions would suggest. Research finds that use of social
research is collaborative, interactive, and contextual (Prewitt et al. 2012;
Wimbush et al. 2012), so it would be difficult or unfair to attribute it to
a particular research result itself and to only one result. It would be fairer
to include interactive sessions and dialogue into the notion of the research
process itself, but then you cannot subsequently separate the “research”
from its “impact.”

Sometimes it is a stream of research or school of thought that is influ-
ential. Last but absolutely not least, there are some political decisions that
researchers would not want their research to support even if they could.
Again, the perspective on quality defined as impact cannot identify—but
sometimes assumes—the goodness of impacts.

Interestingly enough, the current attempts at capturing the impact of
social research are caught between the almost indefinite and unlimited
nature of impacts, on the one hand, and the desire to capture them in a
methodologically rigorous way on the other. The best compromise
currently seems to be asking researchers to provide some form of systematic
narratives that illustrate particularly striking examples of the impact of
their research. These kinds of inscriptions do not live up to the high ideals
advocated by strict causal methodology. Paradoxically, research managers
at different levels in the university system who are otherwise proponents
of strict, objective, and rigorous methodology are now pulling out their

hair as they suddenly have to seek help from otherwise despicable qualita-
tive narrative methods just because of a political need to document
impacts. At the same time, there is an intense demand for all kinds of
digital devices that capture traces of research, each of them claiming to
capture “impact.” Perhaps technology here determines what counts rather
than the other way around.

Another point of contention is how interventions need to be struc-
tured to fit into the imagery connected with impacts. The causal method-
ologies used to document causal impacts in a trustworthy manner have a
structuring force on the interventions, programs, projects, and services,
the impact of which is to be measured. More often than not, sound causal
judgment depends on rigorous control of a methodological inquiry, at
best in the form of a randomized, controlled experiment. It is necessary
to implement the intervention with fidelity, which requires training,
handbooks, and surveillance. It is also necessary to deny the control
group access to the services provided in the intervention to avoid polluting
the experiment (Droitcour and Kovar 2008). The point is that the
methodology required to identify the causal impacts of an intervention
with some degree of trustworthiness has a very structuring force on
interventions themselves. The attractiveness of the quality-as-impact
perspective therefore hinges upon the willingness to structure interventions
as defined preemptively by the inscription device needed to document
their impacts. Not all politicians or managers, for example, are willing to
explain to beneficiaries that their access to services will depend on
randomization; they often leave such tasks to other agents in the
implementation chain.

An additional problem with the impact perspective is that although a
particularly structured intervention may be shown to have a desired
impact, at least statistically, someone who is considering a practical
situation may find that the intervention structured in this particular way
is impossible, unfeasible, or inappropriate in the practical situation at
hand. The given practical situation may be complicated, fuzzy, or difficult
to interpret. A wide range of norms and values to be taken into account
does not afford a strict separation of means and ends where only ends
count as “impacts” (Biesta 2007). In practice, there is often intense pressure
to act here and now (Schwandt 2002). So even if the perspective aims at

codified information about which interventions work best, the practical
applicability of that codified information may be limited. (This problem is
parallel to the ones encountered in quality as standards and in organizational
recipes).

In practical circumstances, it is also debatable how much rigor is
demanded from a methodology that produces causal propositions. In
some situations, a preliminary understanding, a tentative causal link, or
even a warning about potential side effects may be enough to reduce the
uncertainty regarding the choice of a particular practical action. In other
situations, methodological standards are set so high that even fairly obvious
knowledge is deprived of its right to be called evident. In this light, the
problem is not so much the methodology required by the perspective on
impact, as such, but rather the normative, political forces that send
methodological requirements up and down, depending on expediency. So
the key question is not about paradigmatic disagreements in the philosophy
of science regarding the ontology and epistemology of causality. The key
question is the size of the gap between the methodological textbook and
real life. The issue is also who manages that gap and who has the right to
issue a waiver exempting us from the strict version of methodological rules
(or not) so that we accept or reject findings in a particular case.

Intra-scientific and practical debates have made clear that the concept
of causality is not as unequivocal as some of its advocates believe. In fact,
there are multiple schools of thought and their many advocates are in
disagreement. For example, the search for universal laws that allegedly
characterize experimentalism and statistics are criticized by critical
rationalists who find causality only in the configurations of contexts,
mechanisms, and outcomes (Pawson and Tilley 1997). Diverse practical
interests in causality manifest themselves in different practical questions,
which require different methodologies and lead to different answers (Stern
et al. 2012). In other words, quality as impact does not speak for itself. It
is not univocal. Once you start working within the perspective, your
statements and conclusions will depend on your own positioning and
your specific questions, even if you choose the one perspective that makes
highest claims on scientific and methodological rigor. Furthermore, while
the best methodological designs to produce causal inference help defend

against some of the specific threats to validity, no design offers general
protection against all of the threats in all situations (Cook and Campbell
1986).

At the same time, criticism of quality as impact is sometimes over-
stated. For example, a utilitarian ethic is contrasted with an ethic of duty
and/or care. The distinction is sometimes used to make the claim that
advocates of impacts and effects only evaluate human activity in terms of
its consequences (a utilitarian ethic), while they are blind to other ethical
perspectives. While that may be true, it cannot be concluded, as is
sometimes implied, that a utilitarian orientation is by definition unethical.
Attention to effects and impacts would be ethically warranted when we
will be confronted with the consequences of our actions even if these
actions are primarily motivated by other ethical principles.

Attention to effects and impacts is important in many practical-
political situations. If criticism of methodologies delivering causal claims
becomes too sweeping, it is no longer impossible to say that smoking
causes cancer or that education influences life chances. On that basis,
Latour (2004) recommends that the critical analyst “moves closer” to
how causal claims are produced in the detail rather than “moves away”
from them at all.

When we move closer to causal claims, we see tensions in the interface
between the specificity of methodological rules and the broadness of the
impacts we wish to capture, and we will see ambiguity in how rules for
causal claims are applied to specific situations.

Quality as Excellence

Quality as excellence is a perspective that builds on one of the classic
semantic components in the concept of quality: the sublime, the special,
the exquisite, and the rare.

At the same time, it paradoxically shares with a number of other per-
spectives (standards, organizational recipes and impacts) a particular
“emptiness” or “lack of specificity” as regards specific values or substantial
criteria according to which quality is defined. Something is excellent if it
is in a special category of itself above others, but it can be excellence in
any respect. Quality as excellence has to do with relative positioning.
Quality as excellence and social competition are therefore often connected.

The general meaning of this perspective often rests with its elective
affinity with a social ideology favoring competition, often at the global
level. Quality as excellence has gained particular traction in an era of
competitive capitalism. A ranking system (e.g. league tables) is the
epitome of this perspective on quality. Yet, the term excellence as such
does not carry specific meanings that allow it to pinpoint what an
excellence perspective must mean.

In the light of this vague perspective, a quality problem is often defined
as a lack of a good ranking or, more widely applicable, the lack of a
strategy or ambition directed toward maintaining or improving one’s
ranking.

Limitations and Ambiguities

With quality as excellence as a dominant perspective, the whole world is
defined as one quality zone, meaning that the same goals, criteria, and
ways of thinking are assumed to apply everywhere (Meyer 2008). One
problem therefore relates to the relevance of “excellence” in general versus
quality in a particular situation. To serve the needs of a specific situation,
an “excellent” solution may be less relevant or even inappropriate.
Schools and other educational institutions may be caught between
their concern for serving local communities versus an interest in “excellent”
results on a ranking scale. In a similar vein, research oriented toward

Quality Perspectives 89

close interaction with local stakeholders and collaborative problem-
solving may have problems documenting their “excellent” quality in
wider, perhaps international, contexts; but that does not mean that the
research did not serve a noble purpose. Locally relevant research may help
educate the people, create public debate, and promote social innovation
(Chavarro et al. 2017).

Another problem occurs when “excellence” is translated into opera-
tional criteria. In particular, the transformation of various quality criteria
into a hierarchical ordering is problematic. In the assessment of research
quality, for example, geographical arenas are often conflated with hierar-
chical levels of excellence. If something is nationally recognized, it is
therefore by definition not as excellent as that which is internationally
recognized. In practice, “international” often means published in the
USA or in an Anglo-Saxon language area. Consequently, the researchers
living in these areas and by nature publishing in their county’s journals
are by implication “born excellent.” A post-colonial lesson from anthro-
pology seems relevant here: International systems of comparison often
have a surprisingly local (and Western) epistemic anchoring. Basic cate-
gorizations are not culture-free (Sahlins 1976).

Yet, notions of international and other forms of ranking seem to have
a strong impact on minds and mentalities. For this reason, there is
demand for “theories” that help explain why the successful have become
successful and, by implication, what someone should do to imitate their
success. Subscribing to such theory and being a rational actor in relation
to a broader social field constitutes what is seen as legitimate behavior
(DiMaggio and Powell 1983; Meyer et al. 1994).

Some of the theories explaining what is required to achieve excellence
are very soft theories. For example, most sports clubs have a strategy
explaining what to do in order to win the league championship one of the
following three years. Logically, some of the strategic theories are bound
to fail. There is not enough space at the top of the league for so many
winners. Quality as excellence is by definition a positional good.

When this perspective is in play, discussion often arises about whether
comparison is fair and the playing field level. Universities in many coun-
tries are expected to be as excellent as Oxford, Cambridge, Harvard, and

Stanford, without taking into account the differences in financial
resources, teacher/student ratios, languages, and other factors.

Competition sometimes leads to peculiar behaviors. In research, the
prestige of a journal depends on its journal impact factor (JIF), which
is calculated on the basis of the number of subsequent citations of
journal articles. Some editors are said to encourage their contributors
to adopt citation practices that help enhance the journal’s JIF. If suc-
cessful, the integrity of the JIF is compromised. As an increasing num-
ber of scholars wish to publish in the most prestigious journals, the
burden on reviewers has increased. If nothing other than publications
count for the “excellent” researcher, however, there is no incentive for
researchers to carry out reviews. It becomes difficult to recruit review-
ers (Bauer 2016). One way to do so is to ask scholars who are cited in
the article under review. They are presumably experts in the field and
wish to carry out their review with integrity, but they also have their
own interest in the publication of articles in which they are cited.
These mechanisms are difficult to uncover, because the peer review is
anonymous. Yet, if there is a structural bias in favor of positive and
mutually beneficial—but hidden—exchanges between editors, review-
ers, and authors, then the fairness of the competition is not what it is
assumed to be.

The fairness and validity of rankings is often under debate. Shrewd
university managers refer to university rankings only when their own
position is good or improving. Otherwise they remain silent. Quality as
excellence is a perspective that often puts the competitors in ambiguous
or awkward positions with regard to which kind of behaviors are legitimate
to enhance one’s position and when and how much the ranking system
should be accepted as legitimate.

Social Conditions Favorable to Quality as Excellence

This perspective thrives in situations where global competition is seen as
the relevant context for quality assessment. It requires a center of calculation
which is able to capture data and produce quality inscriptions which
circulate widely in time and place. The perspective is particularly powerful

at the local level when influential stakeholders around an organization add
pressure on the organization to comply with criteria of excellence (Espeland
and Sauder 2007).

Quality as Practice
Definition and Meaning

Quality as practice is a perspective on quality that is deeply rooted in
theories of practice. It resonates with Aristotle's notion of phronesis
(practical wisdom or judgment) (Schwandt 2002) and Dewey’s notion of
situated qualitative thinking (Dewey 1931).

As moral-ethical beings, humankind is already “thrown” into concrete
sociopolitical and practical situations. As hermeneutics would suggest, all
of our interpretations of life are bound to this thrownness as an inescapable
starting point (Schwandt 2002). In Dewey's words, the starting point
(also for scientific thinking) is the “experience of the common man”
(1931: 101).

Our situations are already “multi-dimensional,” meaning they are insti-
tutional, historical, political, relational, corporeal, and personal at the
same time. Phronesis, or practical wisdom, does not come from compliance
with a given set of criteria (be they legal, scientific, utility-oriented, etc.),
beginning instead with a careful appreciation of understanding the
situation. Over time, good practitioners learn to recognize patterns in
situations they consider typical. They incorporate a sense of good practice
as part of their habitus or “tacit knowledge” (Polanyi 1966). Habitus is
often as much corporeal as it is cognitive. For example, travel to a country
where traffic drives on the left side of the road, and your typical, intuitive
way of moving around in the cityscape becomes awkward. Even if you
intellectually understand where the traffic is, your sense of orientation
and your bodily movements are difficult to change.

The corporeal aspect of practice is clearly illustrated in the case of
sports stars and musicians. Playing the guitar requires coordinated fingers
together with a sense of harmony and rhythm. Learning to play includes
overcoming resistance of a genuinely bodily nature.

92 P. Dahler-Larsen

Becoming a competent practitioner requires training and_ skill.
Malcolm Gladwell has written that mastering practically anything
typically requires at least 10,000 hours of training. There is a reason why
this training is called “practice.” Good practitioners continue to learn as
they adapt to changing situations, appreciate ever more nuanced aspects
of these situations, and refine their skills. Good practitioners share with
experts and connoisseurs (whom we encountered earlier) the cultivation
of perceptual differentiation, which is trained through experience. Yet, an
expert perspective and a practice perspective are not the same. Expertise
travels and can be called in, whereas a practice is collective, interactive,
habitual, and situated.

Practice is neither “planned” nor “implemented” but often intuitive
and improvisational. Against the backdrop of tradition, competence, and
institutional setting, good practice is often defined by small innovations
and the understated display of extraordinary skill. Good practice is
therefore only partly habitual. It is also sometimes emergent and
dependent on play, innovation, and the mood of the day, even if it hinges
on skill, experience, and craftsmanship (Sennett 2008).

This becomes clear in the case of a soccer player who sometimes passes
a ball into what appears to be a surprisingly empty space, while one of his
teammates has already intuitively grasped the idea before everybody else
and is moving into that space at exactly the right moment, thereby
making the pass a stroke of genius. Exactly how fine an item of practice
it is could not be known before the move was made. ‘The good practice
almost relied on its own inexplicable execution. But it is possible to
examine the situation in toto in awe and simply exclaim, “Oooh, that was
good!” In Dewey’s (1931: 102) terminology, “translation into specific
terms gives a partial and inadequate result.”

Schwandt explains this phenomenon in terms of a position that con-
trasts with a conventional criteriology. Criteriology suggests that there
must be some external or transcendent desiderata with reference to which
differences in knowledge positions, ethical stances, or practical choices
can be reconciled. Criteriology is a quest for permanent criteria of ratio-
nality founded in a desire for objectivism and in the belief that we can
and must transcend the limitations of socio-temporal frameworks of
knowing (a.k.a. “situations”) (Schwandt 2002: 95). In contradistinction,

Quality Perspectives 93

practice theory is “anti-criteriological.” True enough, good practice
may lean on criteria (be they legal, ethical, theoretical, etc.), it may
refer to a particular interpretation of criteria, but the point is that there
is no algorithm that transforms a given set of criteria into good practice.
No criteria exhaust the meaning of good practice. It is “good” in an
irreducible sense. In the terminology of the introduction of this book,
quality as practice embodies itself as intuitive quality scripts rather
than as formalized quality criteria. In the practice perspective, quality
rests with the total configuration of the situation into which it is
situated.

Lamont (2009) reports on a fascinating study of the practice of profes-
sors in a cross-disciplinary panel reviewing applications. They must reach
a decision about the allocation of research funds. Their practice is com-
plicated in part due to conflicting understandings of what constitutes
good research in the different fields and subfields. Lamont unpacks the
different meanings that professors attach to evaluative criteria, such as
originality, significance, methods, feasibility, and more “evanescent”
aspects of research. She also attends to “cognitive contextualization,”
meaning that the weight attached to a given set of criteria takes into
account what is considered appropriate for the field or discipline of the
proposal under review (Lamont 2009: 106).

The interaction between experts takes place with reference to highly
advanced norms, but also tact, dialogue, and courtesy. Panelists lose
credibility if they push their own field (Lamont 2009: 137). On the other
hand, panelists who establish credibility over time may increase their
influence on collective decisions.

Even if Lamont admits that disagreement and even horse-trading may
occur, her study brings us closer to an understanding of the many social
mechanisms which help enhance a form of practical decision-making
that most of the participants find respectable, given the complexity of the
task and given the time constraints under which committee work takes
place.

Here is an example that resonates with Lamont’s work. In a committee
that reviewed innovative and cross-disciplinary applications for PhD
projects, the secretariat asks two or three panelists to prepare a recom-
mended decision about each application before the meeting. It turns out

94 P. Dahler-Larsen

that panelists who accept a proportion of proposals that is starkly different
from the proportion of proposals that are ultimately financed will, unless
there is a systematic reason found in the allocation of applications to each
panelist, either be overruled very often or will contribute to unfair
decisions by promoting too many of “their own” applications. For that
reason, the secretariat calculated the initial acceptance rates from each
panelist, so that these rates were on the table and could inform the
common deliberation. One panelist openly shared in a meeting how he
had increased his acceptance rate, so that it would not be unreasonably
difficult for a proposal if this particular panelist had taken part in the
initial review. The panel collectively calibrated and adjusted the
instruments used in quality assessment. The ongoing and reflexive fine-
tuning of instruments is a pattern found in other literatures on practice
and multiple orders of worth (Stark 2009: 27).

In her study, Lamont finds that the panel participants identify with
what they see as the highest standards. Their sense of integrity is essential
to the collective belief that the process is fair (Lamont 2009: 136). The
same sense of integrity is essential for the ongoing pursuit of the perfection
of the collaborative instruments and decision-making processes in use.
Even if the participants’ sense of a fair process might be partly illusory,
their investment of their own sense of integrity in the very process
contributes to making the practice as good as it can be under the
circumstances (which includes huge amounts of paperwork and time
pressure during meetings).

Lamont’s observation of the importance of a practitioner's sense of
integrity resonates with how Schwandt anchors the very theory of
practice in the existence of people as moral beings. Practice cannot be
separated from how we define ourselves. Faced with the dilemmas and
difficulties that define practical situations, people blaze a trail for
themselves guided by their sense of the kind of human beings they want
to be. Our sense of who we are stands and falls with how we conduct
ourselves.

According to the practice perspective, quality problems can come from
an insufficient understanding of the situation, from lack of training or
experience, from moral failure, or simply from the insurmountable of
messiness of the human condition. Problems do not come and go merely

Quality Perspectives 95

because you “choose” a perspective (or not). As German hermeneutic
philosophers would say, there is no uberwinden of such problems, only
verwinden (Vattimo 2005). The difference is difficult to translate, since
both mean “overcome.” But iiberwinden has a sense of something
victorious which is not found in verwinden. The meaning is that quality
problems cannot be “resolved,” but you can “deal” with them in more or
less qualified ways, depending on the exercise of practical wisdom.

An advocate of the quality as practice perspective might say that, in a
given practical situation, all of the aforementioned quality perspectives are
in fact somehow already taken into account. Nobody would deny that
political goals deserve respect, that users should be attended to, that
competition is sensible (at least up to a point), that some criteria matter,
and so on. A practical philosopher might argue that, seen in isolation,
each of already-presented perspectives is merely an ideology. All of the
perspectives may have some partial thing to say, but the tricky thing is
how to interpret its specific meaning in a given situation and which weight
each of many concerns should be given in a particular practical situation.
That can only be determined after an interpretation of the situation.

A quality problem can be exacerbated, however, if external criteria are
imposed on practitioners, forcing them to prioritize only aspects
corresponding to these criteria, thereby ignoring the moral complexity of
the situation in toto. The whole meaning of the practice perspective rests
with the notion that good practice is a response to a situation, so an
in-depth understanding of the situation is required to make careful inter-
pretation possible.

Limitations and Ambiguities

‘This perspective is fine when it comes to understanding a practice that is
merely “good” in Dewey's terms. However, the perspective is less helpful
when it comes to discriminating between “good practice” and just
“practice.”

While the strength of the quality as practice perspective is that it ques-
tions any pre-given set of criteria as standards of good practice, until the
situation at hand is sufficiently understood, the weakness is that, since it

96 P. Dahler-Larsen

tends to see all practice from the “inside” of the situation, it also affords a
permissive view on what good practice then might be.

Although philosophically, good practitioners constantly review their
own practice, the term “practice” often becomes mobilized in real-life
political discourse as a (general) defense against performance management,
audit, comparison, in fact against any critical review of a given way of
doing things in a given setting.

Although the best proponents of this perspective advocate ongoing
self-critical reflection upon practice, the philosophical foundation of this
perspective also lends itself to being rhetorically abused to defend all
kinds of practices.

This has to do with the inherent link between the practice and the
understanding of the situation in which it is embedded. Good practice is
relative to a particular understanding of the situation to which it is a
response. However, “the situation cannot explain itself in a proposition,”
as Dewey (1931: 98) explains. It is not and cannot be stated explicitly.
Although aspects of it can be highlighted and discussed, the situation in
its entirety cannot. Quality is captured only inside a quality script which
remains intuitive and therefore not fully articulated.

“Knowing the situation” is an epistemic privilege that allows one to
make judgments about what constitutes quality as practice, but reference
to a known, but unarticulated “situation” does not always constitute a
transparent foundation for democratic reasoning and critical argument.

While situations are at least partly defined by their boundaries in time
and space (Stark 2009), analysts who seek to understand good practice
too often seem to know how to define the situation at hand. The practical
situation is very often defined from the standpoint of a small group of
professionals in a “community of practice” or in terms of some sort of
locality (e.g. a local school). How can one know that practice is solidary
with the local? In school politics, for instance, most analyses of practice
examine a situation where local school teachers struggle with national
tests and standards that are more or less irrelevant in the local situation.
But is the delineation of national school policy—with or without tests—
not also a difficult practical question? Is it also not practice to engage in
national debates, to develop a cool new computer system for national
tests, to write a book for an international audience, or to send a rocket up

Quality Perspectives 97

into space? The recent, broad application of “the practice turn” in social
science would evidently answer: Yes, practice manifests in all kinds of
situations, in many areas of professional activity, and in many walks of
life (Antal et al. 2015; Stark 2009).

Yet, there is a particular pattern in a typical way of situating practice
studies on the micro level. It equates “the situation” with “the local
situation” (Stake 2004). More often than not, practice is seen as one thing
and society as another. As a consequence, society-making is not regarded
as practice. Instead, society provides all of the challenges, such as
indicators, competition, and globalization, which practice has to struggle
with, sometimes juggling multiple “orders of worth” (Stark 2009). More
often than not, practice itself is portrayed as heroic against these influences
from something “outside” of “the situation.” The underlying idea seems
to be that practices were better off if society left them alone.

Again, however, this heroic and usually tragic picture of practice rests
with an understanding of a “situation” that others in society may not
share. Thrownness and “situatedness” are in themselves not enough.
Consider an animal trainer in a theme park. It requires many years of
training, practice, patience, and courage to train wild animals to perform.
However, the overall idea of keeping wild animals in captivity for
entertainment purposes no longer fares well in Western culture. The fact
that the animal trainer’s job requires highly advanced skills and is difficult
as seen from “within” has simply lost relevance. It might be a complicated
practice, but the rest of society does not recognize it as a good one.

Diane Vaughan’s excellent study of the Space Shuttle Challenger
disaster is not articulated as a study of practice, but can be read as such.
She demonstrates how the disaster resulted not from external pressures
to launch the rocket before it was ready or from scrupulous, risk-taking
managers. Instead, she shows how ambiguities about safety and early
warning signals were overlooked, eliminated, and/or buried in
documents. The factors leading to the disaster were in fact normalized in
NASA. They were, in my interpretation, part of practice. But neither the
term practice nor the situation to which it refers seems to guarantee good
practice.

The quality as practice perspective has an ambiguous potential to turn
in two directions. The first is a conservative one in which “thrownness”

98 P. Dahler-Larsen

means being relegated to situation that cannot be defined and articulated.
Any practice—also “good practice’—rests with this thrownness. The
primary rhetorical use of this version of practice is that it provides a
defense against the forces of the modern world (e.g. science, globalization,
management). When it is criticized, this practice defends itself as rooted
in “the nature of teaching” or “the essence of our identity in this
community.” This view of practice is conservative in its political
implications: Existing practices should be protected.

The other direction in which the quality as practice perspective can be
taken is one where the situation in toto may be impossible to enunciate,
but it remains open for curiosity and questioning. The perspective is
helpful in appreciating the difficulties, the many “orders of worth” at
play in contemporary society (Stark 2009), but there are no guarantees
to be found in any inner essence of either the “situation” or “the practice.”
There is no original first way in which we find ourselves thrown as
human beings. Indeed, school teachers are teachers in a school, but they
are also teachers in a society. Practices are essential in the sense of
important but not in the sense of something natural and essential inside
things and situations themselves. Practices are a way of looking at human
activity, be it at any level of analysis you may find relevant. Society-
making is also practice, even if it is difficult and incorporates many other
practices.

This non-conservative version of practice theory (and of its hermeneu-
tic foundation) is in my view consistent with contributions from
Rosanvallon (2009) and Vattimo (2005). Rosanvallon describes democ-
racy as inherently situated and historical. Democratic “learning” can
therefore be seen as a form of situated practice that must constantly
“invent” itself, as it is not a function of any universal or ahistorical prin-
ciple. With Vattimo (2005), we learn that there is no ultimate first way of
being a human being, so there can be no first-order guarantees from
which principles of practice can be derived. As an alternative, Vattimo
offers the notion of pensiero debole, weak thinking, the purpose of which
is to help weaken epistemic privilege and weaken authority. In that sense,
Vattimo uses the anti-criteriological principles of the practice perspective
to weaken the perspective itself.

Concluding Reflection

The presented perspectives on quality are fundamentally different. The
fact that they can be meaningfully identified and separated—without
necessarily exhausting the concept—shows that quality as a concept is so
rich that it cannot both be grasped in totality and have any degree of
specificity at the same time.

Notice how each of the perspectives subscribes to a particular under-
standing of what “the quality problem” might be. The link between the
two is tight. The relevance of a given quality perspective in a situation
depends on whether the corresponding quality problem can be found
and is of concern to some in the given situation. Therefore, an identifica-
tion of quality problems may be a fruitful way of finding out why or why
not a given quality perspective is relevant in a situation. It would be a
shame to work on non-existent problems.

Conversely, once a quality perspective is chosen, it also defines the
kinds of problems people may have. The nine perspectives define quality
problems in as many ways. So although some may find some problems
more pressing in some situations than in others, there is no direct way in
which an analysis of reality can inform us about which quality perspec-
tive should be applied to which situation. A perspective transports its
own quality problems with it that happen to fit miraculously with the
perspective. Nevertheless, understanding the problem-perspective dyad
brings us closer to what it is that makes various perspectives relevant and
what it is that people may disagree about in a given quality situation. In
turn, our analysis cannot do what can otherwise be done rhetorically
with an unspecific use of the term quality, which is to conceal problems
and differences under an ever-positive veil of obscurity. If we refrain from
any specificity and use quality in a broad, all-encompassing political
rhetoric, quality can “suck up” all of the problems mentioned in the sum
of all these perspectives. It is a rhetorical free ride to refer to quality as
long as it is unrelated to the specificities of perspectives, situations, and
problems.

In addition to the usefulness of an inter-perspective analysis of quality
suggested in this chapter, there is also the intra-perspective dimension.

If a perspective is “worked through” with sufficient care, insistence,
and meticulousness, it often turns out to be “unstable” or “non-essential.”
We can “peel the onion” and not get to a core that remains stable. For
example, political goals are hazy or shifting. Implicit goals become explicit
and change the evaluative framework. Users have autotelic needs and
wants separated from the use of things (Bauman 1983), and they do not
become happy when they get what they want; or they become happy
about something that they were not able to explicate in advance. Users
are also sometimes in irrevocable disagreement with each other. Standards
are created in the moment of calculation. There is no solid rock under-
neath them when they change. Practices are sometimes self-reflexive. So,
even in a strict logical domain, quality perspectives can be logically sepa-
rated from each other, but that still does not provide them with an essence
which guarantees their stability in use.

‘There is no guarantee in the empirical world that quality perspectives
occur in purely logical, separate forms. They may be appealed to only in
the form of hunches, assumptions, or rhetorical moves. To create and
hold together alliances, tacit overlaps between some of them may be
emphasized, such as “standards” in the interests of “users” or vague goals
turned into a notion of comparative “excellence” or the “practice” of
being “impact-oriented.” Since many of the quality perspectives have an
elective affinity with the views of particular stakeholders (e.g. goals with
political principals, user needs with users, standards with bureaucrats,
practice with professionals), there is likely to be some form of “ambigu-
ous policies” (Baier et al. 1986) or “productive misunderstandings” (Stark
2009) creating overlaps or alliances between various types of quality
agents and weaving them into stories of quality (Weick in Dean and
Bowen 2000: 163).

Presumably, in the greater socio-historical stream of shifting significa-
tions and imaginaries, some perspectives and alliances between perspec-
tives will gain more traction at some points in time than others. In an era
of bureaucratization, standards will flourish. In an era of globalization,
excellence will be demanded. It has also been suggested in the analysis of
the nine perspectives that the codification and ease of operations in time
and space is something that helps select some perspectives before others,
especially in relation to the production of quality assessments across time

Quality Perspectives 101

and space, beyond singular situations. For example, the expert perspective
and the practice perspective by definition score low on codification, and so
does the user perspective (unless it is reduced to a few quantifiable dimen-
sions). On the other hand, the standards perspective, the organizational
recipe perspective, and the excellence perspective are particular fit to be
transformed into procedures which pave the way for management of large-
scale quality zones extending through time and space.

Still, given our understanding of the opacity of the concept of quality
so far and given the need for alliances and consensus, it would be futile to
establish a grandiose hypothesis about any linear development here.

Instead, I will pursue another strategy. A strategy is needed to help
explain how quality becomes fixed in particular ways in particular situa-
tions. By fixed I do not mean stable forever, I just mean fixed enough for
long enough time to make an argument or statement about quality or to
carry out some sort of evaluative undertaking. In order to grasp this, I
turn to quality measurements and the agencies performing them because
they play an important role in stabilizing what would otherwise be elu-
sive, opaque, vague, or multi-dimensional quality.

Quality Inscriptions: Their Makers
and Their Consequences

The previous chapter made clear that the concept of quality itself is spa-
cious enough to embrace a wide variety of perspectives. Furthermore,
within a given perspective, no one can guarantee that (the social con-
struction of) quality remains stable and fixed.

In order to determine what quality might mean more specifically in
specific contexts, this chapter is concerned with how quality inscription
takes place, who makes such inscriptions, and what the consequences of
quality inscriptions are. This topic is not peripheral. In education policy,
for example, the mechanisms used to secure quality assurance and evalu-
ation have been more in focus than quality itself (Kaukko et al. 2018:
180).

By quality inscription I mean a form of codified documentation of
quality. Inscription devices comprise methodological, statistical, organi-
zational, and practical tools that render visible and transform otherwise
complex, ambiguous realities into figures, scales, indicators, numbers, or
categories. Without quality inscriptions, it would not be possible to pro-
duce comparisons, ratings, or rankings (Dahler-Larsen 2014).

An inscription turns “pieces of matter into written documents”
(Latour and Woolgar 1986: 51). “Inscription” means that a particular
interpretation of the quality of a quality object finds a solid form so that

P. Dahler-Larsen, Quality, https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-10392-7_4

it can travel in time and space. More often than not, codification implies
quantification using scales, rankings, and so forth; in principle, how-
ever, inscription can assume any form or shape that maintains and sta-
bilizes some discernable traces of the quality object. The inscription is
presented as if it “is” the object. The point is not a philosophical one
about whether the object that is referred to “really” “exists” in some
ontological way. It is just enough to note that there would be no contact
with the object in the way it is defined through the inscription were it
not for the inscription itself. An inscription helps produce “the occa-
sional conviction of others that something is a fact” (Latour and Woolgar
1986: 105). Many inscriptions are less successful. Quality inscriptions
are statements the further fate of which are in the hands of others
(Orlikowski and Scott 2008). They are performative acts which can fail
(Butler 2010).

Nevertheless, quality inscriptions often play an important role in
defining quality in practice. We have seen in historical and conceptual
analyses that quality is radically polysemic. The measurement of quality
therefore plays an important role in providing quality with specific mean-
ing and direction in a given situation. Measurement requires a machinery
for inscription. Inscriptions carry an imprint referring back to the actors
who made them. Remember that quality perspectives are linked to under-
standings of problems. In turn, since problems and frames are important
political devices (Stone 2012), quality inscriptions can be more politi-
cally significant than they appear. The definition of quality that an
inscription offers is a social factor in itself (Koselleck 2011). Quality
inscriptions can be used to make particular appeals to people to act in
particular ways and to enact the quality object in particular ways. We
will return to constitutive effects of quality inscriptions later in this
chapter.

Inscriptions and Organizational Hinterlands

Inscriptions are only possible in particular social contexts. Three observa-
tions allow us to move theoretically closer to an understanding of how
inscription takes place and who has the capacity to make inscriptions.

Quality Inscriptions: Their Makers and Their Consequences 109

The first observation is that the codification of inscriptions requires
infrastructure and machinery. For example, sending out a digital survey
that measures customer satisfaction requires computers, digital contact
with respondents through mailing lists or webpages, computerized tech-
nology to manage and analyze survey responses, and so forth. Infrastructure
and machinery are also necessary to make results available to the poten-
tial users of the quality information. The availability of computers and
the Internet have played a decisive role for the production and dissemina-
tion of evaluative data about quality. In any specific situation, computers,
programs, networks, capabilities, and competencies need to fit together
in a specific manner in order to produce a particular inscription. As
Latour (1987) explains, what is needed from a center of calculation is not
some extraordinary or extraterrestrial cognitive capacity—it is instead a
practical and operational way of compressing large amounts of data into
concentrated inscriptions. The center of calculation thus avoids drown-
ing in data. Similarly, data must be compressed, transported, and pre-
sented to relevant audiences in digestible form.

The second observation: Once the demands on the infrastructure
become sufficiently complex, organization becomes necessary. For exam-
ple, the production of internationally comparative test results as an indica-
tor of school quality requires a highly complex machinery that depends on
expertise, technology, and political support. Tests must be developed by
experts, translated, and calibrated. To produce comparative results, the
organization must have legitimate access to schools in many countries. The
test machinery must not only reach out to the individual students in many
countries but must also do so in a manner that accounts for the factors
influencing the comparability of the data, including controls for cultural
bias across nations. Technology must be developed and alliances built with
experts and hundreds of local, political, and administrative gatekeepers.
There must also be infrastructure (data presentation formats, media, net-
works with governments) that allows a producer of quality inscriptions to
spread the use of data into the relevant quality zone. By quality zone I mean
the time and space in which the quality inscription is meant to be relevant.
In Programme for International Students Assessment (PISA), the quality
zone is the global system of competing school systems. The scale of this
quality zone is indicative of the scale of the organizational apparatus needed.

110 P. Dahler-Larsen

Few organizations other than the OECD will be able to produce results
that are as successful as PISA assessments in all these aspects. Because of
the high threshold of organizational requirements to produce and diffuse
such inscriptions, it is almost impossible to develop and implement a
viable alternative to PISA.

The third observation is that although some form of independence
from the production of the quality object itself can be a key ingredient in
trustworthy quality measurement, independence is always relative. There
is no such thing as total independence of everything else in this world.
Here, the notion of relevance structure mentioned in the introduction is
a helpful reminder. A relevance structure is a particular way of being
engaged in the world. Organizations also have relevance structures.
Organizations already carry an “imprint” of how they are anchored insti-
tutionally (Scott 1992). Institutional anchoring relates to money, sup-
port, expectations, sanctions, and ways of thinking.

Again, the example with the OECD and PISA studies is illustrative.
The OECD is an international organization promoting economic devel-
opment. The relevance structure and favorite mentality that permeate the
OECD are economic ones. When the OECD engages in measuring qual-
ity in schools, then, the larger framework in which they define quality is
embedded in an economic way of thinking. In an OECD perspective on
school quality, the national school systems are conceived of as competi-
tors in a global marketplace. So while the quality assessment may be
“independent” (in the sense of being made “at a distance”), it is not inde-
pendent from a very particular kind of relevance structure and institu-
tional commitment.

In a similar vein, a concept of “good governance” was coined by the
World Bank in the late 1980s. The Bank did not have legal capacity to
interfere directly in member countries’ internal affairs. Although “good”
can mean many things, in the case at hand “good governance” in fact
embodied an economist view with a focus on market-led steering (at the
expense of other values such as democracy, environment, and social cohe-
sion) (Erkkila and Piironen 2014: 349). This view resonated with the
dominant philosophy in the World Bank as well as with economist ideol-
ogy at the time.

Studies in the sociology of knowledge have shown that the production
of a particular knowledge requires a large set of connections between
multiple elements in a whole “hinterland” of constructions undergirding
innovation (Bijker and Law 1992). To understand how an organization
produces quality inscriptions, a useful key is an analysis of the organiza-
tional hinterland.

The hinterland ties the organization to the world in particular ways
while simultaneously allowing the organization to act in a specific man-
ner. The hinterland may include an ideology, a self-understanding, alli-
ances, expectations, and promises that induce the organization to inscribe
quality some ways and not in others. And not to forget: resources.

Making a quality inscription requires a technical infrastructure and
organizational machinery that are usually costly. Seen from the perspec-
tive of the organization producing the inscriptions, it is important that
these costs do not become a burden on itself. It therefore either has to
receive generous external support or enroll “free” contributions from
partners in the organizational hinterland. Common ways of doing so are
to use inputs that employees have to provide during normal work hours
(documentation of public services) or ask consumers to provide ratings
(consumer reviews).

Consequently, even if quality machineries are expensive to run, some
of their costs are (1) external to the inscribing organization, (2) not mon-
etarized, and therefore (3) not visible. But resource dependencies remain.
It is therefore beneficial for quality-inscribing organizations to command
or recruit others to work for them or, alternatively, find financial models
that are profitable and do not disturb the alleged integrity of the quality
inscriptions. This can be difficult.

No example illustrates this point better than the discussion on credit
rating agencies (CRAs) following the 2008 financial crisis. A CRA is a
company that assesses the debt instruments (bonds and other securities)
issued by firms or governments and assigns “credit ratings” to these
instruments based on the likelihood that debt will be repaid (Rom 2009:
640).

While you might be tempted to believe that credit ratings merely have
to do with creditworthiness, according to the terminology circulated
among specialists, CRAs in fact gauge the “quality of the underlying

112 P. Dahler-Larsen

asset” (White 2012: 105) or “financial quality of the securities” (Dufthues
and Weterings 2011: 342). Credit ratings are, thus, inscriptions of qual-
ity. Investors use them to gauge the quality of the financial entities in
which they invest (Rom 2009: 640).

While the dominant CRAs use a variety of rankings comprising cate-
gories (e.g. AAA or Al+), the critical distinction is whether or not a par-
ticular entity is “investment grade.” Many bonds categorized as
“investment grade” by the CRAs were proven by the 2008 financial crisis
to be “junk bonds.” The realization of this fact, which occurred too late
and on a massive scale, led to a “cliff effect.”

Credit ratings have been used for more than a century. One of the first
known examples is Moody (still in business), which rated the bonds used
to finance railroads. This was an “investor-pays” business model, meaning
that potential investors could purchase a copy of Moody’s publication
before deciding in which railroad bonds to invest.

After the crisis in the 1930s, credit ratings were used in the public
regulation of banks. In 1936, a source mentions that US banks should
not invest in something “speculative,” but should base their investments
on “recognized rating manuals” (White 2012). Similar regulation was
extended to pension funds in the mid-1970s. Some financial institutions
have bylaw restrictions commanding them to react to credit ratings on an
ongoing basis when composing their portfolios (Scalet and Kelly 2012:
479).

In the late 1960s and early 1970s, the CRA business model changed
from an investor-pays model to an issuer-pays model (Scalet and Kelly
2012). One contributing factor, which illustrates the rule of the pudenda
origo (lowly origins) of social change, is that the emergence of cheap pho-
tocopying rendered it too difficult to make a sufficient number of poten-
tial investors pay for publications with credit ratings (White 2012: 99).
Furthermore, bond issuers themselves became increasingly interested in
their own ratings, partly because they were subject to regulation (White
2012).

The issuer-pays business model had severe consequences. When an
issuer of a financial entity decides to choose a CRA, it can play rating
agencies against each other. While several ratings agencies might be con-
sulted, only the most favorable rating is published. Conversely, CRAs

Quality Inscriptions: Their Makers and Their Consequences 113

also have commercial interests. They can offer consulting services to issu-
ers who want to know how to achieve the best ratings possible. After
selling these consulting services, the CRAs can then sell a rating. A CRA
is subject to conflicting interests when assessing the quality of a financial
entity, which it has helped develop (Rom 2009; Scalet and Kelly 2012:
482). Objectivity flies out the window if the payment received by the
CRA in fact depends on the rating. Altogether, these mechanisms pushed
“CRAs to offer favorable ratings in the interests of their own profits”
(Scalet and Kelly 2012: 482).

Until the 2008 financial crisis, numerous complex financial products
were sold that consisted of inferior securities packaged in ways that were
not very transparent. At the same time, CRAs produced many more
credit ratings without increasing their staff correspondingly, and some of
their ratings were based on procedures lacking in transparency. They also
made out-of-model adjustments (Rom 2009: 647). In retrospect, CRAs
were also criticized for not monitoring the rated assets properly and
responding too late to market tendencies.

According to the CRAs, however, not all fluctuations should be
reflected in changed ratings. The CRAs wanted to “rate through the cir-
cle” so that temporary ups and downs would not lead to continuous
changes in ratings. For that reason, downgrades occur infrequently (Rom
2009: 645). On the other hand, when it becomes clearly visible that a
rating requires revision, it may come so late that a “cliff effect” occurs, as
in 2008.

While incompetence, greed, over-optimism, lack of proper procedure,
and the increasing complexity of the financial products all might have
contributed to the disaster (Rom 2009), it is instructive to study the con-
sequences of the issuer-pays business model. This model created a par-
ticular set of dependencies in the organizational hinterland of CRAs,
which seriously damaged the solidity of their quality inscriptions.

This observation justifies two related questions, the first relating to
whether the criticism of CRAs is not too easy: Will not all assessments of
the quality of financial assets be tentative? And would we not be dissatis-
fied if procedures were followed slavishly—and also if they were not?

Whether or not it is true that CRAs are too reluctant to lower existing
credit ratings, the CRAs learned from the disaster and changed their

practices after 2008. When they in fact performed a “less expected down-
grade” (of Austria, France, and Italy in 2012) (Eijfinger 2012: 212), were
these countries not dissatisfied then? After all, the assessments of credit-
worthiness are just that: assessments. They are more or less tentative. And
will all such assessments not make some happy and some unhappy,
regardless of the exact quality of the assessment? Is it too much to expect
perfection in credit ratings?

The next, related question goes one step further. Why focus so much
on the (lack of) perfection in credit ratings? If people (including espe-
cially investors) are so unhappy with credit ratings, why not simply ignore
them? Is it not somewhat pathological to blame the CRA when some-
one’s investments lose value? Should investors not know that they are
taking a risk? Should they take more responsibility for their
investments?

The question is asked not for rhetorical reasons but for theoretical
ones. What is missed in how the question is asked is the public and insti-
tutional function of credit ratings. Credit ratings cannot be ignored
because they play a functional role in relation to the buying and selling of
financial entities. Regardless of whether one “believes” in their consis-
tency with “real” creditworthiness, creditworthiness refers to payments,
which will or might be made in the future, so it is unknowable until it
becomes inscribed. Once inscribed, however, it becomes known. Until
payment is actually made, a credit rating is the best proxy available for
that payment. On that basis, economic life is regulated. When banks and
pension funds can(not) buy or sell particular bonds because of the credit
rating, these ratings have a real function that cannot be ignored. This
might be called “over-reliance,” but “creditworthiness statements have
attained the force of law” (Eijffinger 2012: 913).

Advanced discussions on how to improve the regulation of CRAs must
therefore consider the complexity and paradox in the social function of
credit ratings. For example, the certification of CRAs (to improve the
credit ratings’ trustworthiness) may increase reliance on CRAs, which
may also exacerbate cliff effects. The engagement of public authorities in
the rating of credit may lead the public supervisor to be held accountable
(Eijfinger 2012: 915). As an alternative, some dream of several new
CRAs other than the three dominant ones that have existed since 2000

(White 2012), but entry into this market is difficult for newcomers. And
can newcomers be trusted?

Credit ratings are institutional mechanisms. They are social construc-
tions of reality. People “invest” their hopes, anger, and arguments in the
“quality” of the credit ratings not because they have an interest in the
inherent quality of the financial entities but because the credit ratings are
already linked cognitively and institutionally to expectations, prices,
interest rates, and therefore to the buying and selling of assets.

Thus, it is important to understand organizational hinterlands of
quality-inscribing organizations because they make an imprint on how
quality inscriptions are made. Next, quality inscriptions and their conse-
quences are more closely linked than conventional terminology suggests.
It is reduction to assume that first a good quality inscription is made that
is true to reality and then the quality inscription has some real effects, but
only if the inscription is valid. “Valid” or not, the inscription works in the
moment of its inception. Our example shows how social ties and connec-
tions with a quality inscription—the “investment” in it, if you will—
have already been made. Consequences are already linked closely to the
inscription and are just waiting for its enunciation.

A reality-to-become is, thus, part of the organizational hinterland. No
claim is made that there is only one such possible reality. The point is
merely to warn against a theoretical model affording anyone to stop time
as quality inscriptions are made and then to start time again when one is
ready to accept their consequences. A vocabulary must be developed to
describe how quality inscriptions help create reality through connections
and associations (Latour 2005). Some of these are already packaged. In
the case of CRAs, expectations from sellers and buyers and authority-
imposed regulations are already part of the landscape in which credit
ratings operate based on a particular business model. It is a set-up ripe
with consequences.

For example, when South African Minister of Finance Pravin Gordhan
presented his budget for next year, he was under pressure. Everybody
wants him to do more than he can. In what appears to be a clever political
move, he presents his budget with a lions-against-buffalo metaphor (if
lions cooperate, they can kill the biggest buffalo). He manages to raise
income taxes and taxes on cigarettes, beer, wine, and alcohol while

keeping his deficit under control and setting a little money aside for edu-
cation, old age grants, and social grants. All this, he claims, pays due dili-
gence to the credit ratings of South African bonds (Cape Times, Feb. 23,
2017). The next day, the Fitch CRA declared that “the budget showed
deficit reduction remained an important policy aim, but political and
social pressures would test government commitment to fiscal consolida-
tion” and, furthermore, that “the main challenge to fiscal consolidation
comes from factional tensions in the African National Congress (ANC)
which are diverting political energy from economic reform” (Cape Times,
Feb. 24, 2017).

The situation is not one where credit ratings are produced and later
have consequences; they are already inside South African politics. They
are involved and involving. Minister Gordhan has already taken them
into account as he prepares his budget.

In other words, a quality inscription is constructed in a particular
manner due to its particular hinterland, which at the same time paves the
way for a set of consequences. The next section is devoted to what I call
the constitutive effects of quality inscriptions. By constitutive, | mean
how a description helps construct, form, and shape the reality it claims to
describe. As already suggested, the constructed/constructing aspects of
quality inscriptions are closely intertwined.

Constitutive Effects of Quality Inscriptions

Remember that the inscription presents the inscription of the object as if
it were the object itself, so it is fair to claim that the inscription has con-
stituted the object (as it appears as that particular kind of object). An
inscription also performs a “call” for someone to act upon the inscription
and to do work on the quality object. In the case with CRAs, notice how
closely these aspects are related. Once a certain credit rating drops, the act
of “selling” is sometimes almost automatically triggered, at least it might
be, when the credit-rated entity is subject to regulation. Notice also that
a self-fulfilling prophecy might unfold: The market value of the entity
decreases as people want to sell it. The rating is more than a description.
It is an interference.

Quality Inscriptions: Their Makers and Their Consequences 117

The consequences of the interference are potentialities that may be
enacted more or less depending on circumstances. “Constitutive effects”
is my term for how a quality inscription influences reality while it claims
to describe it.

In my earlier work (Dahler-Larsen 2013), I have suggested that consti-
tutive effects can be observed in different domains, such as

— the content of some object or practice,

the timing of activities related to that object or practice,

the social relations of those involved,

— the broader worldview in which the object or practice is situated.

For example, testing in schools leads to “teaching to the test” and a
revision of the curriculum (content). It helps organize the timing of ped-
agogical activities (especially up to a test), it influences social relations
(e.g. when teachers or schools compare test results), and it constitutes a
particular view of schools and education as sites for performance.

One of the key mechanisms that helps enforce constitutive effects is
simply that human attention is limited. When a particular form of qual-
ity inscription is encoded into an organizational procedure, that proce-
dure likely has an impact on how people enact the shaping of a particular
social order because the procedure “steals” attention away from other
potential ways of seeing the world (Dahler-Larsen 2013). The notion of
quality itself also guides the orientation of people in a particular direction
since it is often connected with a self-evident notion of “good” or “bet-
ter.” It is usually not necessary to question whether grade A++ is better
than A+ or whether a higher rank is better than a lower one. Evidently,
the pressure to act upon a given quality inscription varies from one situ-
ation to another and can only be determined empirically. However, there
is a link between the notion of quality itself, its organizational life, and its
constitutive consequences.

In the following, I wish to inquire into constitutive processes in further
detail. (A quantified inscription will be used as particularly typical and
illustrative, but a quality inscription in qualitative form would be within
reach with a slight change of argumentation). In the following, I explain
the mechanisms of constitutive effects in terms of seven processes that are

118 P. Dahler-Larsen

both logical and social: categorization, abstraction, commensuration,
connection, interpellation, temporalization, and fixation.

Categorization

Quality inscriptions define categories to which quality objects belong,
the aspects of the quality object that are worth evaluating, and the labels
signifying varying degrees of goodness.

Any quantification rests on a fundamental categorization of what is
being tallied, including individual and collective identities to which
numbers can be ascribed (Porter 1995: 42) as well as all kinds of quality
objects and their properties. The practice of counting is embedded in or
dependent on a specific kind of categorization. Sometimes it is the count-
ing itself that mobilizes categorization. Consider, for example, quotas for
people of particular ethnic backgrounds in particular social positions.
Quotas may be seen as a helpful way of alleviating former inequalities in
some countries. As Neville Alexander (2013) points out, however, such
arrangements conserve the ethnic or racial categories of the past. The act
of counting and distributing people according to ethnic categories main-
tains and stabilizes the categorization system.

Categorizations are cultural and institutional products, but more than
one relevance structure can be at play among those who apply them to
practical purposes. A focus on some aspect of the quality object as the
defining aspect is needed; in the terminology of Schutz (1975), a typifica-
tion. It is the “typicality” of something that connects the object and the
broader category. Schutz explains that the same object can be typified in
different ways, a phenomenon referred to as indexicality (Schwandt
2000). Ambiguous situations are those where typifications do not give
themselves. Nevertheless, categorizations have performative implications;
in a very fundamental sense, categorizations help get things done.

For example, if a quality education of carpenters requires students to
spend 12 weeks at a carpenter’s workshop, it is necessary to define such a
workshop. And if there are not enough carpenters who accept appren-
tices, the educational institution will have to provide a workshop itself,
even if it may be problematic to categorize it as “a carpenter’s workshop.”

It will be tempting to do so, however, if the school thereby solves a quality
problem for itself and its students.

It makes a difference whether or not an object is constituted as a qual-
ity object. The aspects of life that are seen as relevant for an assessment of
“quality of life” are those that somehow should be attended to, reflected
upon, and changed, if necessary. Other aspects of life may be seen as not
relevant, or unchangeable.

To qualitize a phenomenon is to make it subject to distinctions.
Hochschild (2004) reports on a survey in which a manager’s family mem-
bers are asked to rate how the manager functions as a private person.
While the survey makes family life “a quality object” that the managers in
focus must consider, it also makes it an object of rational management
practice, as the “relevance structure” of the company where the managers
are employed and their ability to perform there has motivated, informed,
and structured the survey.

Categorization also applies to a quality object “in the capacity of” a
particular property or its “belonging to” a group of phenomena of par-
ticular ideological or practical interest. For example, when a social service
is understood in terms of categories such as inputs, processes, outputs,
and outcomes, then the placement of what constitutes quality in an
object within those categories makes a difference, as these categories
already signify specific social ideologies and purposes.

For example, consider an ideology that identifies quality in public
management with an outcome-oriented approach. Once care for the
elderly becomes seen as a quality object, caretakers are compelled to
define intended outcomes, such as an increased “ability of the elderly to
do their own daily chores,” “increased quality of life,” and so on. These
outcomes are inherently difficult to achieve, as the situation of elderly
individuals deteriorates predictably. Those who take care of the termi-
nally ill often experience tension between a philosophy of caring and the
“outcomes” consistent with a utilitarian ethic. The problem here, then, is
not only the categorization of something as a quality object but the
simultaneous incorporation of the quality object into a very particular
regime of quality measurement which thereby subjects it to a particular
form of social control where only “outcomes” are given attention and
priority.

To insist that the “outcomes” of services are defining for quality is to
combine quality with a particular ideology of management and steering.
Perhaps surprisingly, however, the very definition of “outcome” is socially
flexible, to put it mildly. Although some tend to think that the definitions
of inputs, processes, outputs, and outcomes are cast in stone, the practical
handling of quality objects under evaluation suggests otherwise.
Bureaucratic processes can be treated as “outcomes” if the purpose is a
more efficient implementation of legal requirements. Incomes can be cat-
egorized as outcomes when external funding is used as a proxy for research
quality. Even in an instruction booklet used to educate evaluators, where
the example was a family trip with an outcome called “strengthened fam-
ily bonds,” travel as an activity was nowhere to be found and the output
was called “tickets” (W.K. Kellogg Foundation 2004).

What needs to be understood about the categories of inputs, processes,
outputs, and outcomes is not how particular phenomena are sorted “cor-
rectly” but rather, in constitutive terms, what is produced through this
categorization, the difference it makes for an object to be described in
terms of its “outcome,” and the differences the particular mentality of
categorization engrained in this exercise make for the world.

The perils of categorization can be complicated and dynamic. How
should research production be measured? In Denmark, this problem is
taken care of by a national bibliometric research indicator system
Bibliometrisk Forskningsindikator (Bibliometric Research Indicator)
(BFI). It was introduced to measure research production so that research
funds could be reallocated depending on research production. The BFI is
constructed according to a Norwegian model designating two levels of
publications: Level 1 is comprehensive and inclusive, where the only
requirement is that the journal (or publisher) uses peer review and can be
found on a recognized list of serious outlets. Level 2, which comprises no
more than 20% of the publication outlets in each sub-discipline, is the
finer category and gives more points. Groups of recognized scholars in
each sub-discipline decide the allocation of outlets into each of these
groups.

The purpose of the two-tiered system is to prevent the type of simple,
constitutive effects found earlier in Australia (Butler 2003), where the
number of publications was used as an indicator, which led to increased
quantity but lower quality (measured as the number of articles in presti-
gious journals). All in all, then, the BFI is introduced as a measure of
research quantity which is allegedly inclusive and fair and corrects suffhi-
ciently for research quality.

To avoid the unintended redistribution of funds, the indicators are
normalized so that funds are only redistributed across universities, not
across fields (e.g. natural sciences, humanities).

The Ministry of Research, which reviews the bibliometric registrations
leading to the BFI, has found that “too many” publications “primarily
from Danish publishers” are registered as “research,” whereas an analysis
they have had carried out casts “doubt” on the scientific nature of these
publications.

As criteria relevant for sorting a publication as a research publication,
guidelines describe “categories of publications,” peer review, the use of
scientific method, the contribution of substantially new knowledge, and
the intended target groups of the publication. The underlying agenda
seems to be that before these guidelines existed, too many researchers
have registered too many dubious products simply to earn bibliometric
points. Moreover, perhaps too many of the academic groups involved in
defining lists of journals have included too many journals at level 1 that
are not seriously academic. The motivation to do so is that the higher the
number of publications in level 1, the more can be admitted into level 2,
which is the prestigious one. The rule that makes this unfortunately link
between the two categories is that only 20% can be allocated to level 2.
Therefore, a higher denominator allows for a higher numerator.

In terms of categorization, the notion of “new knowledge” or “original
work” is not always easy to apply. When new empirical data are reported,
this categorization may be easy to make. But there are sometimes concep-
tual or methodological problems standing in the way of scientific prog-
ress, and a researcher very often considers the development of a field,
discusses the obstacles that need to be overcome, and proposes some ways
forward in the way to think about key issues. All of this may constitute a
contribution to the field, but the “new knowledge” criterion cannot

always be applied in a very exact manner. When it is good, even a fairly
pedestrian introductory book to a field organizes what is known about a
field in particular original, and hopefully clear manner. Only neo-

about it. When we struggle with how to organize what is known, we can
also contribute to new knowledge. If the “new knowledge” criterion is
strictly applied, review articles that synthesize what is known in a field

most useful and most cited articles.

Another criterion is the use of the publication in relation to intended
target groups. Especially in the social sciences, research on a contempo-
rary topic can be relevant among practitioners, policy-makers, journal-
ists, academics in think-tanks, key people in trade unions and the public
administration, consultants, and others. The fact that a book contributes
to the “dissemination” of knowledge does not take the research out of the
book. It can still be based on scientific method, including critical think-
ing, and still be peer-reviewed.

In small countries there may be, say, five researchers who are interested
in a book because it relates closely to their own research, while there may
be 500 or 5000 practitioners among the readers. If you consider the
actual function of the book, it is in purely quantitative terms more dis-
semination of research than it is a contribution to research, not taking
into account the uncomfortable little fact that, in some areas of knowl-

contribute substantially to the production of new knowledge production
regardless of whether they are technically called researchers (Gibbons
et al. 1994; Prewitt et al. 2012). Identifying research as something that is
directed at university-based researchers is therefore somewhat old-
fashioned. It assumes an old, linear model in which research is aimed at
researchers in their own circles. Furthermore, one could conceive of one’s
role as a researcher as one that writes for society, not for researchers.

Recently, a new problematic categorization has been added to registra-

Researchers are asked to categorize whether a presentation is “interna-

Geographical locations are hereby conflated with social recognition, to
the extent that the three levels of recognition are understood to be hier-

another one, although they are logically clearly different.

The measurement process sets a whole range of practices in motion
aimed at categorizing stuff that then becomes measured as “research” and
“research products,” which give “bibliometric points” and some degree of
“recognition.”

Abstraction

Consider a course evaluation system at a business school. It may include
dimensions such as content, theory/practice balance, coherence, struc-
ture/format, level of student activity, tam atmosphere, communication
on digital website, and student learning in terms of new perspectives,
better understanding, and use in practice.

It does not matter which subject is taught. A course on “Methods in
social science” is compared without further reservations to the one on
“Hospital management.” Some might argue that it is unfair to compare
“relevance for my practice as a manager” across these various subjects. But
comparison presupposes abstraction.

In fact, abstraction is fundamental for particular codifications of
knowledge. Reading requires an abstraction from what letters otherwise

look like. The art of calculation implies abstraction from the substance of
what is being counted. Modernity is characterized by a vast increase in the
abstraction of measures (Nisbet 1966). While pre-modern measures were
often anthropocentric (the distance of 3 ft) or highly contextual (a day’s
journey), modern measures are abstract and only connected to physical
objects through highly mathematical and scientific formulas. Originally,
a meter was 1/10,000,000 of a quarter of the circumference of the globe
as measured by Méchain and Delambre (Alder 2002). Now, according to
Wikipedia, it is defined by the General Conference on Weights and
Measures as the distance traveled by light in a vacuum in 1/299,792,458
of a second. The point is that metrics become increasingly abstracted
from properties that make intuitive and experiential sense to us.

To avoid excessively easy criticism of all measurement, it is worth not-
ing that many measures were developed out of a need not only for coor-
dination (think: train timetables) but also for fairness and justice. For
example, market transactions are perceived as more fair and transparent
when all parties know which quantity is delivered and how to resolve
conflicts over this should they occur. Kula (1996) shows how it became
difficult to coordinate action, trade, and commerce over distance when
measures were defined differently in different locations.

An interest in objectivity has played an important role in the history of
measurement (Porter 1995), although research also shows that what
passes as objective measurement of complex phenomena is sometimes a
result of a pedestrian compromise between a variety of social forces
(Desrosiéres 2002).

Modern society is organized on the basis of fundamental measuring
devices regarding volume, distance, time, and money. Monetarization
means the translation of substantively and experientially different phe-
nomena into things that are comparable on the same scale and therefore
exchangeable. The abstraction inherent in these processes is sometimes
debatable. This becomes especially pertinent when abstraction means
ignoring the specificities of the situation that, for some, is inherently part
of the definition of quality.

International comparative studies of education sometimes ignore these

A fundamental question in comparative statistics is whether it is pos-
sible to ensure that a phenomenon under measurement actually means
the same thing to people in different countries. Here, native English
speakers should be reminded that terms equivalent to “achievement” and
“performance” do not exist in many other languages. On the other hand,
other languages also hold certain key terms central to educational think-
ing (e.g. Bildung) that might not be directly translatable into English. If
we respect the historical, contextual, and situated productions of mean-
ing in education (Névoa and Yariv-Mashal 2003), it is impossible to
believe that the establishment of any comparative quality inscription
device (whether articulated in English or not) takes place without consid-
erable abstraction from national and local specificities.

A loss of meaning can only be registered from some perspectives, not
from others. If one meaning system (A) is in a position to demand that
another meaning system (B) presents its statements in such a manner that
they can be understood from A’s perspective, that is, although well-
intended, equivalent to establishing power over B when B is not in
position to demand the same thing from A (Becker 1996). Perhaps the
issue—whether indicators mean the same to people in different national
contexts—is not that relevant in practice. Perhaps it matters less which
specific meanings from national and local contexts are ignored or glossed
over. What matters more is that another transportable meaning system is
produced from which these specific differences do not count if they can-
not be translated into this “global” vocabulary. This functional superior-
ity of a global inscription device may be more important than its
(perceived) lack of methodological validity.

device might be that it produces a common set of categories; a joint,
