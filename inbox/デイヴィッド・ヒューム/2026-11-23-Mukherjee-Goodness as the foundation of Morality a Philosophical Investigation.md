INTRODUCING THE RESEARCH PROBLEM

‘The concept of "goodness" has long been a cornerstone of ethical discourse, serving as a
foundational elementin moral philosophy. In his seminal work, Foundations of Ethics, Sir
W. David Ross astutely observes, "There are two compelling reasons why any student of
ethics must devote attention to the nature of goodness. Firstly, we habitually regard
certain dispositions, actions, and individuals as morally good; however, to comprehend
the notion of ‘morally good’ clearly, it is essential to understand both the concept of
goodness in general and the distinguishing characteristics of moral goodness. Secondly, a
significant portion of our duty ~ indeed, according to a widely accepted theory, our entire
duty ~ is to bring into existence that which is good."! Considering Ross's remarks, it
becomes evident that elucidating the real nature of moral goodness is one of the most
pressing questions confronting contemporary students of moral philosophy. Although
current trends in moral philosophy tend to prioritize practical issues over meta-ethical
concerns, the fundamental conceptual issues in moral philosophy remain pertinent and
continue to occupy a central position in the field, Nevertheless, the nature of moral values,
their justification, and their relationship to naturalistic frameworks remain contentious
issues, This research problem, titled "Goodness as the Foundation of Morality: A
Philosophical Investigation" seeks to explore the role of goodness in moral philosophy,
particularly through a naturalistic lens. The concept of "goodness" and its multifaceted
senses, encompassing both moral and nonmoral contexts, presents a complex and
intriguing philosophical challenge. The term "good" is employed in diverse contexts,
thereby raising fundamental questions regarding the feasibility of establishing a unified

account of goodness within a naturalistic framework.

‘This research endeavors to explore the distinction between moral and nonmoral senses
of "good" and investigate the viability of a naturalistic explanation that encompasses
both. A central issue in this inquiry pertains to the nature of moral values and judgments.
Unlike factual or descriptive statements, moral values are often perceived as inherently
normative, thereby posing a significant challenge for grounding them within a
naturalistic framework. This study will critically examine whether moral values can be
reconciled with naturalistic explanations without compromising their normative force.

Furthermore, this research will evaluate the Naturalistic Fallacy, which asserts that

SIGNIFICANCE OF THE STUDY

As Professor Alasdair Macintyre astutely observes in his seminal work, A Short History
of Ethics, “asking and answering moral questions is one thing, and asking and answering
philosophical questions about morality quite another thing..the fact that in asking moral
questions of a certain kind with sufficient persistence we may discover that we cannot
answer them until we have asked and answered certain philosophical questions"? The

importance of this research lies in the essence of Maclntyre's statement. Philosophical

quiry is an integral component of ethical inquiry, as revising, reinterpreting, or
modifying ethical norms and principles necessitates philosophical thinking. Normative
ethics, which can be regarded as a first-order moral theory, must be complemented by
meta-ethical theories, which operate at a second-order level of inquiry to comprehend
the notion of moral goodness, it is essential to first understand the nature of morality
itself. This underscores the need for ethics to be grounded in a robust foundation of
ultimate standards of ethical values, which serve as the guiding principles for all ethical
actions. In this context, the present study assumes significant importance, as it seeks to
explore the fundamental questions surrounding morality and ethical values.

OBJECTIVE OF THE RESEARCH

‘The primary aim of this research endeavor is not to provide an exhaustive examination of
the diverse perspectives on goodness espoused by various moral philosophers and
writers. Rather, this study seeks to understand their insights to establish a robust
foundation for moral standards, thereby informing the justification of ethical norms and
principles. Specifically, this research aims to address the ambiguities surrounding the
multiple meanings and senses of the fundamental ethical term "good," The focus of this
inquiry is to elucidate the intrinsic meaning of moral goodness, recognizing that while
relative ethical norms and principles may undergo changes, their underlying foundation
should remain constant. Although moral principles and judgments may exhibit variability
across different societies, itis posited that certain fundamental notions of moral concepts
possess timeless, truthful, and unchanging qualities. Consequently, while conceptions of
rightand wrong may fluctuate, the concepts of right and good should ideally be universal.
The objective of this research is to extract the essential meaning of absolute goodness,
thereby providing a justification for moral standards and judgments.

Chapter 3: MOORE'S OPEN QUESTION ARGUMENT - REFUTE WITH NATURALISTIC
ARGUMENTATIONS This chapter critically examines G.E, Moore's philosophical inquiry
into ethics, particularly his concept of the "open question argument." Moore posits that

property. He critiques naturalistic ethics, arguing that attempts to define "good" in terms
of natural properties commit the naturalistic fallacy. In response to Moore's critique, this
chapter explores naturalistic argumentations that refute his open question argument.
Ayn Rand's objective ethics framework, for instance, posits that morality is a code of
values guiding human choices and actions, grounded in objective reality. Rand argues that
human life is the standard of value, and that which promotes survival and well-being is
considered good. John Rawls’ theory of goodness as rationality is also examined. Rawls
categorizes good into three distinct categories and argues that a rational plan for an
individual can determine their good. His theory of good is inherently dependent on the
principle of justice, emphasizing the importance of universality, restrictedness, and free
accessibility. Ultimately, this chapter concludes that a naturalistic moral standpoint,
