HAT honest Chinaman, Confucius,

would be greatly astonished if he were
alive today and heard so many boys and
girls quote him as the author of their
breezy quips — “Confucius say.” A
thoughtful, high-minded teacher, he died
all of twenty-four hundred years ago after

traveling about China in an oxcart, offer-

ing advice to the high and lowly among
his countrymen. But his teachings were
noted for their deep thought, and his say-
ings, still treasured today among the Chi-
nese, mark him as his country’s greatest
sage and philosopher.

When Confucius was born, his father,
who was a soldier, was already seventy
years old. Three years later he died.
His family was left in extreme poverty,
but the undismayed mother succeeded in
some way in giving her son the best edu-
cation of his time. Confucius did not
disappoint her. When he was only seven-
teen, he had become the manager for a
rich landowner.

As time passed, the youth became more
and more of a thinker. His mind dwelt
a great deal upon problems of right and
wrong and how men. should live. When

‘homes of the great, he lived in luxury,

but other times he had to put up with
considerable hardship. His teachings,
however, made his name known through-
out China, and he gained many more dis-
ciples. :

When Confucius was sixty-nine, he
gave up his wandering life and returned
to Lu to pass his last years. Sorrow
about broke his heart. First, his only son
and then his two favorite disciples died.
One morning as he moved about his home
on his staff, he was heard to croon:

“The great mountain must crumble,
The strong beam must break,
The wise man must wither away like a
plant.”

Later in the day he took to his bed, and
he never got up. After seven days he was
dead. This was in the year 478 B. c.

With a great ceremony he was buried
by his disciples. A group of the latter
even built huts beside the grave and lived
there for three years. As _ centuries
passed, the powerful emperors of China
placed tablets beside the grave, doing
honor to the country’s great philosopher
and teacher.

Today, in the city of Kiuh-Fau where
40,000 descendants of Confucius are said
to be living, the grave with its great
mound and a marble statue in front, is
visited by many Chinese. The sayings
of Confucius have also been preserved,
though, of course, they are not the joking
expressions which so many young people
have been quoting.

PREFACE

Tue idea of writing a small book of this kind came to
me when I was reading a pamphlet on The Presenta-
tion of Christianity in Confucian Lands. I believe that
most persons think of Confucianism as it prevails in
China as being the simple and direct teaching of Con-
fucius. Such an idea is an error. During the twenty-five
centuries of its existence, Confucianism has undergone
many changes. It has seemed to me that a simple book
in which the development of modern Confucianism
should be followed, and in which especial attention
should be given to the half-dozen men who have
altered early Confucianism to its present form would
be worth while. I know no such book in English.
Pére Wieger has written an interesting and able book
of this kind in French, Histoire des Croyances Re-
ligieuses et des Opinions Philosophiques, in which,
however, he traces the history of Taoism and Buddhism
_as well as Confucianism. His book also is addressed to

students who have gone more deeply into philosophy

than the readers of this book are supposed to have
~ done.

Much time has passed since the idea arose. Finally
the work is done. It falls far short of expectation. It
contains much less of independent and original matter
than was anticipated and much more of quotation
from the works of others. It had been my intention to
apply to myself the well known and oft-quoted words
of Confucius: “A transmitter and not an originator.”
But that would really be too great an assumption.
Rather, I am as a stringer of beads. The thread upon
which the beads are strung is a frail and a weak one.
And are the beads well chosen, sorted as to size and
shape, strung with due reference to color and artistic
effect ?

The English form of Chinese names of men and
places is a serious problem. Each competent writer or
translator seems to prefer his individual transliteration.
So great is the difference that it is sometimes difficult
to identify a name appearing in two different authors.
On the whole I have tried to follow Giles, in spite of
his constant and ugly use of diacritical marks. In mak-
ing direct quotations from other authors, however,
there is no change made in the spelling. As a result

Chinese personal names present another difficulty.

Unless one knows all three names, he will become con-

graph. Often it will seem that different men are being
mentioned.

The book is largely quotation from various authors.

quoted can rarely be in doubt. The first mention of a

THE SAGE

For many years my students and I have talked of
“circles of greatness”. When the name of a man of
achievement came before us, we endeavored to
place him in his proper circle. Would he figure
in a circle of fifty thousand of the world’s greatest
of today, or in history? Could he be given a place
in a circle of five thousand, or of five hundred?
Could he possibly be one in the circle of the fifty
greatest? Or, of ten? What are the criteria of
greatness? When the names of two great men are
presented before the mind at once, which is en-
titled to the higher rank? Which is the greater,
Philip of Macedon or Alexander the Great? Of
the three great men who consolidated Japan—
Nobunaga, Hideyoshi, Iyeyasu—which deserves
the greater credit? Many and varied were our dis-
cussions. On the whole we inclined to grant great-
ness to men who wielded a large influence, and
we agreed that breadth and duration entered into
the matter. The man whose thought, word, deed,

influenced great numbers through a long period

of time was truly great. We sometimes attempted
a list of the ten-world’s-greatest circle. We never
had a complete consensus of opinion. There was,
naturally, considerable diversity of opinion as to
what constituted worthy achievement. Still there
was greater agreement within a list of ten than in
a list of fifty.

Usually Confucius was named in our circle of
ten. The vast number of people who have been
influenced by him, the long period during which
his influence has operated, the tenacity of that
influence even at the present time, were urged in
favor of his inclusion. There can be no question
that he belongs within an extremely small circle.
Many dislike him; many consider him a much
over-estimated man; many feel that his influence
has done more harm than good. But any man
whose influence is deeply felt by hundreds of
millions of individuals twenty-five hundred years
after his death was surely a great man.

There are three aspects of Confucianism. It
may be considered (A) a system of ethics; (B) a
philosophy; (C) a religion. These three aspects

should be kept distinct. Confucius was a practical
man, rather than a theorist. He taught human re-
lations and their duties; he indicated what the
superior man would be; he was interested in pol-

itics and good government. He was primarily 4 or

teacher of ethics. He was not a great philosopher.
He cared little for metaphysical discussion or for
explanations of the cosmos. When he was gone
and commentators dealt with his teachings and
the books which he transmitted, a Confucian
philosophy arose. Definition of terms, theories of
the universe, discussion of ultimates, metaphysics,
all came in time. Confucius himself taught no
religion. He avoided discussion of the super-
natural. For him human conduct was the all-
important thing. In his definition of the human
relations, he did not recognize man’s relation to
deity. One of his chief claims to respect is that he
neither claimed to be divine nor founded a re-
ligion. Yet there has grown up a religion of Con-
fucianism, in which Confucius himself has come
to be practically an object of worship. Confucian-
ism is then three things at once—an ethical system,
a philosophy, a religion.

Confucius was born at Tsou, in the state of Lu
in the year 551 B.C., almost twenty-five hundred
years ago. His father was a man of great strength
and courage. He was more than seventy years of
age when his son was born, child of a second
and young wife. The first wife had given him
nine daughters and a concubine had borne him
a crippled son. The second marriage was for the
specific object of procuring a fit representative to
continue the family. They were left in poverty.
The boy was a diligent student from early child-
hood, though little is known of the details of his
education. His regard for his mother was great
and she appears to have stimulated his ambition
and encouraged his studies. He had to go to work
at about seventeen years of age, entering public
service. Young as he was, he seems to have made
some reputation with the ruler of the state. He
married at nineteen years of age and within a year
a boy child was born. On the occasion of this
birth, the duke sent congratulations and a present.

There is some mystery in regard to his married
life. It seems not to have been altogether happy.
It is commonly believed that he divorced his wife

and that they lived apart for many years. Giles
says that she “was a downright Xantippe.” Others
speak of her in kindlier fashion and even seek
excuse for her. Quite likely Confucius was a
somewhat difficult husband. His devotion to
study, his absorption in teaching, his austere man-
ner, his unbending propriety of deportment,
might command respect; they hardly tended to
great warmth of affection. Some writers deny
that there was a divorce. The child born from
this marriage cherished a great affection for his
mother and when she died indulged in unre-
strained grief. It is even said that Confucius, him-
self, on hearing of her death “was much moved,
and spoke of her in affectionate terms.”

In 529 B.C. the mother of Confucius died and
he observed the regulation three years of mourn-
ing. This involved his withdrawal from his official
duties and practical retirement from the interests
of ordinary life. When he finished his mourning,
he returned to his home, opened his house to stu-
dents and began his active work as a teacher. He
had already had disciples, but from now on this
was his most serious occupation.

Confucius is said to have been of more than
average stature, unusually dark in color, and ugly
in his personal appearance. It is probable that the
description of him as given by Soothill would
apply at this time: “He was careful about his dress
both for day and night wear and particular about
its quality, style and congruity of color and season.
He was most scrupulous about his food, both as
to quality, mode of preparation and service; he
would eat nothing in the least tainted or badly cut.
In wine he allowed himself liberty, but not license.
His public bearing was punctilious to a degree,
and in private he permitted himself no undue
freedom—not even in bed. He was gracious and
kindly but never fully relaxed himself, even to
his son, to whom it is incredible to imagine him
as ever amusing. He was a true sportsman, giv-
ing his bird its chance, and only shooting it on the
wing. He was also, we may judge, able to ride
and drive and not merely to sit in a carriage, and
it seems evident that he was well acquainted with
archery. His habits and character may be summed
up in one sentence: ‘If his mat were not straight,
he would not sit on it. He was a punctilious

gentleman of the old school, to whom our mod-
ern laxity, not to say flippancy of manner, would
have amounted to immorality.”

In 523 B.C. he made a special study of music
and is said to have soon surpassed his teacher.
With his disciples, Confucius constantly dis-
coursed upon good government. He was anxious
to take an active part in governmental reform
and was ever looking for some ruler into whose
service he might enter. The prince of his own
state, Lu, knew his abilities but was of a weak
character and surrounded by worthless advisers.
The times were marked by disorder and confu-
sion. The Empire was no longer united, but had
fallen apart into a large number of small states,
each with its petty ruler. It is true that there was
still a nominal emperor of the Chow dynasty,
but he had little power even in the area left to his
control. In the state of Lu there was almost chaos.
Three families ran things and each strove for
advantage; there were dissensions even within
these families. Plottings and intrigues were so rife
that a man of principle had little inclination to

enter politics.

An incident that occurred about this time had
considerable influence upon the young teacher.
In one of the three families there had been a
minister named Mang He. When he was near
death he stated his desire that his son Ho-chi,
later known as Mang I, should become a disciple
of Confucius. The young man, together with his
friend (brother, or cousin?) Nan-kung King-
shuh, became hearers of the teacher. This not only
added prestige to his work; it opened up to him
opportunities for which he had wished. Learning
that he desired ‘to visit Lo, in Honan, where the
Emperor had his capital, they made his wishes
known to the duke of Lu, who placed a chariot
and horses at the disposition of Confucius and .
supplied facilities for his visit. It was an excep-
tional opportunity to examine archives, to see
treasures, and to learn details regarding cere-
monies and procedures of olden times. It is true
that the Emperor gave him no interview, but
Confucius made good use of his privileges. He
here collected material for his later work. And it
was at this time that he perhaps had an interview
with the old sage, Laotze.

Returning home Confucius found the situation
in the state of Lu as bad as ever. In fact Duke
Chao was forced to flee for refuge to the neigh-
boring state of Ch’i, where he died eight years
later. Confucius followed him to Ch’i, where he
was well received by the ruler, and it looked for
a time as if he might receive an appointment.
The jealousy of the court, however, worked
against his receiving an offer which he could ac-
cept with dignity and he soon returned to Lu.
There he spent the next fifteen years in study and
in the instruction of disciples, who flocked to him.
He might, no doubt, have had appointments to
office, but was unwilling to mix in the quarrels
and vulgar politics of the time. Among those
who were conspicuous at this time was an officer
connected with the Ke family. It is said that he
sought an interview with Confucius, which the
teacher avoided. However, meeting on one occa-
sion, Yang Ho said: “Come let me speak with
you. Can he be called benevolent who keeps his
jewel in his bosom and leaves his country to con-
fusion?” Confucius replied: “No.” “Can he be
called wise, who is anxious to be engaged in pub-

“No.” The other added: “The days and the

Not long after, he was really appointed gover-
nor of Chung Tu. His success is said to have been
remarkable. Legge says: “As magistrate of Chung
Tu he produced a marvellous reformation of the
manners of the people in a short time. According
to the ‘Family Sayings’, he enacted rules for the
nourishing of the living, and all observances to
the dead. Different food was assigned to the old
and the young, and different burdens to the
strong and the weak. Males and females were
kept apart from each other in the streets. A thing
dropt on the road was not picked up. There was
no fraudulent carving of vessels. Inner coffins
were made four inches thick, and the outer ones
five. Graves were made on the high grounds, no
mounds being raised over them, and no trees
planted about them. Within twelve months, the
princes of the states all about wished to imitate

Naturally success in any degree approaching
this aroused enmities, both within the state of Lu
and outside. There had been difficulties between
Lu and Ch’i. The Duke of Ch’i feared the grow-
ing prosperity of his neighbor. He made several
efforts to destroy or weaken him, which were
thwarted by the wisdom and care of Confucius.
He felt that the safety of himself and of his state
lay in separating Duke Ting from his wise min-
ister. To gain his end, he pursued a crafty scheme.
Knowing that Confucius would not remain in
the employ of a licentious and pleasure-loving
monarch, he pretended friendship and sent Duke
Ting as a present one hundred and twenty fine
horses and eighty beautiful and accomplished.
dancing girls. The ruse was altogether successful.
Ting neglected his duties, plunged into a period
of pleasure and paid no heed to the warnings and

ure, going away slowly and by easy stages. He

We shall not go into details of those thirteen
years. Attended by his disciples, Confucius wan-
dered from state to state looking for some ruler
who would use his services. He was often treated
with respect and provision was made for his sup-
port, but nowhere were conditions such that he
could put his program of reform into operation.
Sometimes he suffered hardships; more than once
he was in danger. At last he was recalled to Lu,
in 483 B.C. But conditions were still unsatisfactory.
The duke had no serious purpose of reform. The
teacher was an old and broken man. His wife
had died in 484 B.C.; his-son died in 482; his
favorite disciple Yen Hui died in the same year,
or earlier; the faithful, outspoken disciple Tzu
Lu died in 480. Confucius was a lonely, heart-

Before ending this brief sketch of the life of
the sage, we will mention two incidents, which
have earned much comment. The one is prob-

Confucius had one son, born during the first year

was an attendant at his father’s lectures, but
there was no demonstration of warm affection

Very little writing can be certainly attributed
to Confucius himself. He was in no real sense the
author of the Classics. The Five Classics making
up the recognized canon are, as listed above—
the Shu-King, Shi-King, I-Ching, Li-Chi, and
Chun Chiu. Confucius was a profound admirer
of the achievements of the past, of the rulers of the
good old days; he was a diligent collector of
ancient materials and he combined these and ar-
ranged them to the best of his knowledge and
ability. He says of himself: “I am a transmitter,
not an originator.” The Shu-King, or book of
History, consists of annals ranging from 2357 to
627 B.C. The Shi-King, or book of Odes, consists
of 305 poems, of varying length and differing
styles, some of which are believed to date back to
the Shang dynasty. The I-Ching, or book of
Changes, is ascribed to King Wen; it carries a
commentary by his son, the Duke of Chow; Con-
fucius may have added an appendix. It is written
in an extremely difficult and obscure style. Con-
fucius attributed great importance to it and said:
“Given a few more years of life to finish my study
of the I-Ching, I may be freed from great errors.”

Nintoku. These Koreans introduced Chinese
