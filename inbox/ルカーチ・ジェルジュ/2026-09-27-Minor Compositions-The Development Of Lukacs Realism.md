TOTALITY AND FEMINIST LIFE:

READING SILVIA FEDERICI
ON LUKÁCS’S AESTHETICS

Stevphen Shukaitis

In 2009, as I was finishing my PhD on the formation of collec-
tive imagination within autonomist thought, I found myself
growing increasingly disillusioned with the academic rituals
that marked the work’s conclusion. The UK viva process – so-
litary, closed, opaque – felt antithetical to everything that had
inspired my work. Over the years, I had gained so much from
conversations with comrades. It seemed only right that the
conclusion of my doctoral research should also take a collec-
tive form. That desire to refuse the privatized rituals of aca-
demic legitimacy was deeply informed by a broader ethos of
mutual contribution and communal learning. So, in addition
to the usual closed-door defense, I organized a public event
at 16 Beaver in New York City: a kind of counter-viva. It was
a warm, spirited evening, held in that unique art space which
had long served as a site for cross-pollination between artis-
tic and political experimentation. Silvia Federici and George
Caffentzis joined me in conversation. I’d long admired both
their work and ideas and continue to. And I don’t mean admi-
re in that nice way in which you’re supposed to say kind things
about thinkers you respect, but something more substantial:
truly working with others’ ideas in a way that is to be present
with them even when they are not there – the very form of
collective imagination I had been studying. At one point in
the conversation, Silvia turned to me and said, “Did you know

out influence of Lukács’s work on aesthetics and politics of the
time, offering a sustained confrontation with Lukács’s theory of
realism. But what can we make of the non-appearance of Silvia’s
organizing work in her academic writing? That absence is telling
and compels readers to trace submerged political arguments: the
ghostly presence of her activism in a work that presents itself
as literary theory and philosophical critique. It’s a challenge that
invites us to read not just for content but for resonance, tone, and
subterranean alignment with the wider currents of revolutionary
thought at the time.

Lukács argues that Marxist literature must represent social

relations in their totality, capturing the interconnectedness of
individual experiences within the broader socio-economic sys-
tem. He believes that only by depicting the totality of capitalist
society can literature reveal the underlying class dynamics and
historical development driving social change. Silvia approaches
this critically on several levels. Firstly, she argues that Lukács’s
theory of realism is not Marxist at all, but rather an extension
of bourgeois idealism rooted in nineteenth-century German
philosophy. For Silvia, Lukács somewhat bizarrely wants Mar-
xism to act as a completion of German idealism (rather than the
famous “turning on his head” that Marx invokes). Secondly, she
contends that far from resolving the crisis of value that moti-
vated Lukács’s early literary work, his later theory finds itself
ensnared in it. Thirdly, and most significantly, she suggests that
analyzing Lukács’s realism offers a privileged vantage point for
understanding a deeper crisis within Marxist thought itself. Sil-
via identifies three main stages in Lukács’s intellectual develop-
ment and insists on a continuity between them. His conversion
to Marxism, in her reading, does not fundamentally shift the
trajectory of his thought, but rather simply substitutes one
theoretical apparatus for another while maintaining the same
idealist commitments. “How this synthesis of idealistic theo-

ries could be accepted for decades as ‘Marxist theory’ and be so
influential in twentieth century European culture is one of the
questions this dissertation will address” (5).

Her critique extends beyond Lukács as an individual thinker,

touching a raw nerve in the broader project of Marxist aesthe-
tics. As she puts it early on, any serious critique of Lukács’s rea-
lism forces us to confront the limits – and perhaps the impos-
sibility – of a unified Marxist theory of aesthetics. By the end
of the dissertation, Silvia seems to step back from the ambition
of a Marxist aesthetics altogether. Instead, she gestures toward
a different orientation: theory as a tool, more akin to literary
criticism than ontological system-building. If anything, her af-
finities lie with Brecht: his suspicion of totality, his materialist
dramaturgy, his commitment to breaking the illusion. As I re-
turn to the dissertation now, it’s not as an expert but more the
participant of a study group in its best form: a collective explo-
ration among comrades. We’re not reading to assert authority
over the material but to encounter it together, asking questions,
making connections, speculating, and doubting.

The “Lukács revival” taking place during the time Silvia was

writing her PhD focused on his revolutionary writing rather
than his earlier work on aesthetics. Silvia is interested in the
underlying conceptual continuity across the seemingly drastic
breaks in Lukács’s oeuvre. She argues that themes such as the
role of the artist in the world, the nature of the soul, and the
search for form in artistic work become generalized. As his
works develop, the position once occupied by the artist is taken
up by the Communist Party. Against the backdrop of the auto-
nomist movements of the time, this use of idealist bourgeois
frameworks to argue for the correctness of the Communist Par-
ty is doubly troubling. Silvia contends that Lukács does not re-
solve the contradictions within his thought but rather expands
them on a broader scale, universalizing both categories and
contradictions. He adopts artistic production as a model for

x

political action, yet paradoxically shows little engagement with
contemporary art or with the actual conditions and processes
of artistic labor.

One of Silvia’s central arguments is a critique of the concept

of reification. She sees reification as a conceptual tool that gives
the appearance of drawing from Marx, while in fact remaining
within an idealist framework. As she slyly puts it, “Lukács goes
to the factory with the eye of Bergson and in front of the assem-
bly line discovers that the major attack done against man is that
on the line there is no durée” (78). Silvia argues that the Hegeli-
an-Marxist perspective distorts what she views as the most cru-
cial aspects of Marxist analysis. Rather than serving as tools for
struggle, these concepts become methods of interpreting the
world in ways that ultimately justify its current state of things.
As she writes, “the assumption of the Hegelian dialectics, which
postulates an identity between the rational and the real, ultima-
tely leads to a reconciliation with the given, beginning with the
reality of capitalist relations” (97).

The core issue Silvia identifies is a Marxism that prioritizes

alienation and reification over exploitation. As she notes, “un-
like exploitation, which in Marx implies a specific class relation
and class antagonism, reification in Lukács affects everybody
who lives in capitalist society, subjectively and objectively” (100).
In doing so, Lukács abandons the specificity of the Marxist ana-
lysis of exploitation, reducing it to a generalized background
condition that affects everyone. Here we can draw links with
her better-known writing, which is focused on understanding
the specificity of gendered and social reproductive labor and
its structural exploitation rather than collapsing an analysis of
it into a framework of alienation. What emerges in Silvia’s wri-
ting is a demonstration of how seemingly abstract philosophical
and conceptual debates are, in fact, deeply connected to – and
often serve as proxies for – political disputes within the realm
of political organizing.

This orientation feels especially important because the ques-

tions Silvia raises remain unresolved. What is the relationship
between aesthetics and political thought? Can there be a Mar-
xist theory of art that does not fall into idealism? How do mo-
ments of crisis – political, philosophical, economic – shape and
destabilize our understanding of the world? In the same way
that I did not want the discussion of my own graduate work to
be held behind closed doors but rather shared with friends and
comrades, I would say the same thing about these hitherto un-
derappreciated aspects of Silvia’s dissertation, which sits squa-
rely in the thick of debates on aesthetics. Thus, she is writing
at a time when major shifts were underway in both political
struggle and intellectual discourse. In Italy, autonomous move-
ments were redefining class struggle, focusing on unwaged la-
bor, gendered work, and everyday life. Simultaneously, debates
within and outside of the Italian Communist Party and among
New Left intellectuals wrestled with the legacy of Hegel, the
uses and misuses of dialectics, and the future of Marxist theory
itself, taking struggle outside of the classical sites of production
and into the street, home, and university seminar.

Silvia positions herself against the ontologization of Mar-

xism: the tendency to turn historical materialism into a closed
system or philosophy of history, which at the time was still the
dominant position of left-wing parties. “When we speak of
Marxist philosophy,” she writes, “we do not include Marx in the
picture, for Marx never attempted a philosophy of history and
even less an ontology” (6). This is a powerful claim. It echoes
similar critiques made by radical thinkers of the time – among
them, the Situationist International – who viewed theory not
as a blueprint but as a weapon, a means of intervention rather
than interpretation. It shares something with Rancière’s cri-
tique of the exalted position of philosophy,8 and a preference

INTRODUCTION

Lukács’ aesthetics has formed the basis of Marxist art theory
from Benjamin’s investigations of the impact of mechanical re-
producibility of artworks to Fischer’s speculations on the origin
of art. Thus, analysis and critique of Lukács’ realism imply a
confrontation with the whole project of a Marxist aesthetics in
this century. In this context, our first concern is to differentiate
three levels of analysis in this dissertation. First, it is our main
thesis that what Lukács has proposed as a theory of realism
as the foundation for a Marxist aesthetics is in fact an exten-
sion of bourgeois idealism within the tradition of 19th-century
German philosophy. Secondly, we will show that far from over-
coming the crisis of values that plagued his early literary work,
Lukács’ mature theory of realism is still in this crisis. Not only
is his conception of realism riddled with internal contradictions,
as Tito Perlini points out in Utopia e prospettiva in György Lukács,
his supposed resolution to the “crisis” in his later aesthetics is,
“at best, still in the realm of Utopia.”1

Thirdly, analyzing Lukács’ realism provides a privileged

viewpoint to understand the crisis of a whole phase of Marxist
thought, whose effects are still present on the contemporary
scene. Proof of this fact is that, up to this day, a correct critique
of Lukács’ work and its significance in the Marxist tradition
is lacking. The first breakthroughs are in the Italian writers A.

On the one side, the basic problems that shaped his long

literary career are already present from its very beginnings. On
the other, the “metamorphoses” Lukács underwent never really
affected the basic table of his values, being rather attempts to
ground them on more solid foundations. This is not to deny
that the world historical events which Lukács witnessed influ-
enced the development of his thinking. But in a sense, everyt-
hing changed so that everything could remain the same. What
remained unchanged in Lukács is a basic idealistic perspective
that not even his encounter with Marxism and the proletarian
revolution was able to undermine. What Lukács has defined
as his “conversion to Marxist realism” is actually a switch from
subjective idealism (tinged with neo-Kantian overtones) to an
objective idealism along the lines of Hegelian historicism. It was a
switch from Romanticism to Classicism, from the Idea as pure,
transcendental sub jectivity to the Idea as an objective rationa-
lity unfolding in the course of the historical process. Marxism
taught Lukács to substitute the “productive forces” for the He-
gelian Logos. This substitution, however, by no means altered
his idealistic conception of history where man’s only task is to

be the bearer of a predetermined teleology (the “historic mis-
sion of the proletariat”) and every revolution is a revolution in

enlightenment process whereby man perceives what “progress”
requires, i.e., the rational in the real. Not accidentally Lukács’
Marxist realism is founded on the typical presuppositions of
idealist philosophy: the assumption of a metaphysical reality

SECTION I – NEO-CAPITALISM AND THE CRISIS OF
THE BOURGEOIS INTELLECTUAL

Lukács began his literary career with the typical problematics
of the bourgeois intellectual at the turn of the century when,
under the impact of new historical forces, a sense of impen-
ding catastrophe and a crisis of values were pervasive both in
art and philosophy.

A world in fact was dying. The economic depression of 1870,

the rush to colonial expansion, the growth of social democra-
cy and the wave of proletarian struggles which swept Europe
culminating in the 1905 revolution set in motion processes
which quickly changed the face of 19th-century society. When
Lukács went to Germany in 1909, Europe was rushing to war.
But behind the inter-imperialistic struggles more macroscopic
phenomena were paving the way to a new phase of Western ci-

vilization: the development of monopoly capital, the bureaucra-
tization of the State, the growth of mass production, and first
of all the increasing specialization work that was developing not
only in the factories but also in the universities.

All these phenomena shaped the problematics of contem-

porary intellectuals, whose common element is an unmista-
kable pessimism concerning the destiny of Culture and their
own social function. It is no accident that the first victim of
the new socio-economic order was the ideology of liberalism,
with its optimistic confidence in the powers of the individual
to determine his own world. Not only was the rationalization
of work confronting the intellectual with impersonal forces
clearly out of his control. Culture itself seemed at stake, for its
increasing subordination to the needs of industry undermined
its autonomy. Moreover, the new division of work, including
cultural work, introduced a specialization and fragmentation
of knowledge that subverted the possibility of Culture as a
totalizing operation. Thus, the lament of the skilled worker
in the factory over the loss of his skill, destroyed by the intro-
duction of Taylorism, was echoed by the intellectual worker,

intellectual – the classical, universalistic humanist – who was

SECTION IV – SOUL AND FORM

Form, taken as an ontological, ethical and aesthetical catego-
ry, is such a “holistic” concept in Lukács that in developing its
implications he lays the foundations of his thought. Later, his
terminology changed, yet the philosophical “system” implicit
in Form remained the essence of his aesthetics at every stage
of his life. Thus, to analyze the notion of Form is to analyze
the entire project of Lukács’ aesthetics in its most fundamental,
most permanent and most contradictory elements.

As we have seen, like the neo-Kantians, Lukács rediscovers

Subjectivity as the ground for the constitution of a new “To-
tality.” Lukács, however, faces the same problem that plagued
neo-Kantianism, i.e., the need of finding an objective foundation
for subjectivity itself. For after the crisis of Hegelianism, the
assumption of Subjectivity seemed doomed either to Subjecti-
vism (solipsism, relativism, skepticism) or to Noumenism, i.e.,
the acceptance of an unknowable reality. In either case, objec-
tive knowledge in the realm of the Geist, i.e., Geisteswissenschaft,
would be impossible.

A common solution from Dilthey to Max Weber, from Spran-

ger to Gestalt psychology was the postulation of objective forms
(or structures) that are present in Subjectivity, and are carriers
of intersubjective values, for they represent what is typical and
universal in subjectivity itself. What this means is exemplified
by Dilthey’s “psychic structures”: mental structures common

and empirical event. This not only is a specification of the na-
ture of his realism, but an indication of Lukács’ lifelong com-
mitment to a realist viewpoint. Always in Lukács, Art is a ref-
lection, a representation of reality. The reality art refers to is the
Idea, now taken as Subjectivity, later as Historical Reason. But
in both cases: (a) reality is the referent of art, (b) art’s referential
character is based on an absolute isomorphism between the art
forms and the forms of reality, (c) this isomorphism grounds
not only the possibility of art as a correct reflection of reality,
but also the possibility of knowledge itself. That “concordance
between content and form” he so much valued in the Greeks
is already achieved by the young Lukács in reduc ing content
and form to an isomorphic substance. This is why in his early
works there is little concern with epistemologi cal questions. As
subjectivity is at once subject and object, form and content of
knowledge, the subject knows its object because it is its own
reflection or, better, a moment of its self-reflection. In this,
however, he differs from the later realist for whom the episte-
mological question is more problematic due to his need not to
“contradict” the materialist standpoint of the Communist Inter-
national (priority of matter versus mind, lack of isomorphism
between the two).

We can anticipate that Lukács was to “solve” the problem by

“mediation,” i.e., choosing the “third way” (tertium is always da-
tur in Lukács). Between materialism and Idealism, he resorted
to REALISM, which in his later system is par excellence a catego-
ry of mediation. “Realism” allows Lukács both to maintain the
independence and priority of “reality” with respect to the forms
of consciousness and at the same time assume their basic iso-
morphism (a position that would have been untenable if instead
of “reality” Lukács had spoken of “matter”).

SECTION V – FORM AS THE ULTIMATE JUDGE OF ExISTENCE

As our analysis of Form should have indicated, for the young
Lukács Subjectivity knows itself, because the forms it takes are
moments of its self-realization. That is, Subjectivity knows itself
because it makes itself. But how can subjectivity make itself, when
it has been displaced from the world of action, i.e., when all ac-
tion occurs in the interiority of the soul? It is in answer to these
questions that all the contradictions in the theory of Form ex-
plode to the point that an ethical choice becomes the only way
out of the crisis.

Subjectivity knows and makes itself by willing itself to be. For

being a purely transcendental form, it remains a categorical im-
perative that finds its realization in the very act by which it po-
sits itself. What follows then from the theory of Form is an ethic
of duty and responsibility, where to give form to one’s life is to be
“faithful to oneself,” i.e., to be authentic:

Form is the highest judge of life. Form-giving is a judging
force, an ethic; there is a value judgement in everything
that has been given form. Every kind of form giving, every
literary form, is a step in the hierarchy of life possibili-
ties: the all-decisive word spoken about a man and his fate
when the decision is taken as to the form which-his life-ma-
nifestations can assume and which the highest moments
of his life demand.65

Form. First, while posed as the ultimate reality, Form is never given
and appears rather as a duty, a task, in ideal. Second, the freedom
of Form from the world is actually à la Nietzsche, acceptance of
necessity, amor fati: destiny. Third, the life of the soul, being a recur-
ring encounter with destiny, is only preparation to death.

What is manifested by each case is that in the triumph of

Subjectivity lay the germs of its defeat. For its self-realization
is infinite repetition, i.e., re-positing of the same essence (in
Hegelian terminology, it is a “bad infinity”). Its absolute activity
is submission to an absolute passivity. Its voluntarism is nothing
but a stoic, resigned fatalism.

What saved Lukács from his Weltschmerz and opened his way to
a more “mediated” view of the world? Undoubtedly it was his
“faith” in art, whose value for him never entered into crisis but
led his way out of it. For in the very “fact” of art,90 Lukács found
an empirical given that at the same time contained a “reference
to value,” i.e., an immanent teleology. It is with this realization

a theory of art and his first step out of the crisis. “Works of art
exist – writes Lukács – how are they possible?”91 This question
has both aesthetic and ethical implications, for to establish the
conditions of existence of the art-work is to find and give basis to
value in the world. As we have said, the very existence of art qua
Fact is an immediate proof for Lukács that meaning (value) is

very nature of its function is inevitably a structure.

Like the neo-Kantians, Lukács discovers that Subjectivity

qua form can be realized only through its objectifications and
that only through these “objectifications” can the knowledge

objective moments of the spirit (art, history, etc.)?

At this stage, however, Lukács is still one step away from

Hegelianism. He begins with the objective moments of the Geist

The philosophical transition towards a complete acceptance of

the Absolute), but also by the historical events he was to live.

a positivistic given (the particular structures in which it embo-

fatalism, extreme activism and extreme passivism are bound to
coexist. Where the artist seems most active, he is the unconsci-
ous carrier of a predetermined system. Vice versa, the passivity
of the artist is the mark of a higher-level of activity of which
artistic creation is only one moment. Not accidentally, culture
still appears problematic and Lukács is afraid of falling into con-
ventionalism or Romanticism, the polarities of his life.

Lukács’ impasse stems from the fact that his quest for art is

determined by an a priori commitment to the Absolute. Here
is the basis of his realism and problematicism. For despite his
cries of despair about the degeneration of contemporary art,
despite his questioning the possibility of art being at home in
the world, Lukács never doubted the absolute value of Art. He
never doubted the existence – as removed from the world as it
may be – of the Absolute Idea. This explains the true paradox,
already evident in his early works, whereby a man who devoted
most of his life to the study of art was so uninterested and lite-
rally ignorant of the actual art production of his times. For not
only is Lukács’ interest in art totally Eurocentric (unlike Scho-
penhauer, for example, the East did not exist for Lukács, much
less Africa). Even within the limit of the European art world, he
practically ignored (except in a most superficial way) any form
of art that was not literature (painting, films, music, etc.)

In conclusion, Lukács’ problematicism that proved to be a

continuous feature of his career is the result of a more funda-
mental monism, where consciousness – qua form, idea, reason
– is the one and absolute reality. As in the German Idealist
tradition, the contradictions that Lukács faces are inherent
to this reality because it is only by externalizing itself – by
posing itself as another – that this reality can know and the-
refore realize itself. Hence, dualism is the passing moment of
an essential oneness.

That Lukács’ monistic idealism is always “behind the scenes”

while in the foreground all that appears is dualism is undoub-
tedly a product of the historical period in which he lived. The
effect of this dualism is real enough, but its source is like the
original sin of Christian theology: the Idea has fallen from its
golden Greek Age; quite literally, there has been a historical fall
in consciousness. But if the Idea has once historically lived – as
the Greek World irrefutably proved to Lukács – then the idea
can definitely be recuperated in the world.

Lukács does not provide yet a “philosophy of history” to

answer the questions that naturally arise: why was the golden
totality of the Greek world abandoned and what steps have led
to the most “non-integrated” civilization in world history. But
in the last pages of Theory of the Novel he speaks of the already
glimpsed possibility of a hew totality:

This world is the sphere or pure soul-reality in which man
exists as man, neither as a social being nor as an isolated,
unique, pure and. therefore abstract interiority. If ever this

The very possibility of such a totality testifies to Lukács’ be-

lief in the apparent nature of his dualisms. Even though the tota-
lity has the character of a Utopia, just looming on the horizon,
the signs of its coming are written everywhere, for those who
can read. From the forms of the art world to forms of the soul,
the world is already for Lukács a complex of hieroglyphics po-
inting to the new realization of the Geist.

Ultimately, then, problematicism remains in Lukács because

Lukács’ real interest is not in eliminating an apparent dualism
behind which lies a basically isomorphic reality. His situation
can be expressed in the terms of hermeneutics: the world is a
text written in a strange language though the meaning of the
text is known. Lukács’ problem is to fit the translation of the in-
dividual words and phrases into the already given meaning. The

SECTION VII – BETWEEN TRAGEDY AND UTOPIA

It remains to be clarified what was at this stage Lukács’ relation
to Marxism, both as a theoretical perspective and a political pro-
ject. Was Lukács in 1909 a “revolutionary without a revolution,”
as A. Arato maintains?104 While granting the seemingly apolitical
status of his early writings, Arato claims that Lukács’ problema-
tics is pervaded by a radical anti-capitalism, i.e., the sense of the
complete lack of value of the bourgeois form of existence. This
claim, however, presents more than one problem. Undoubtedly, a
critique of capitalism is implicit in these works. But what was the
basis of this anti-capitalism? We will approach this question from
three different angles. First, we have to clarify what was Lukács’
conception of capitalism and the reasons for his critique. Second,
we will examine his relation to Marxism. Finally, we will examine
his relation to the revolution.

Even a cursory reading of Lukács’ early works reveals that

Lukács’ critique of capitalism was more under the influence of
Weber and Simmel than of Marx himself. This had nothing to do
with the fact that in his youth – as he later admitted – Lukács had

a superficial knowledge of Marx. A Weberian and Simmelian in-
terpretation of capitalism was constant in his life. It is revealing,
e.g., that his admittedly Weberian critique of capitalism we find in
his early works, and particularly in HDMD (the only place where
this critique is explicit) anticipates the themes later developed in
the “revolutionary”’ History and Class Consciousness.

Under the influence of Dilthey, Weber, and, most of all, Sim-

mel, what Lukács attacks in capitalism is the division of labor,
which he takes as the root of the crisis of Kultur and the crisis
of “modern man.”105

“The essence of the modern division of labor consists preci-

of the worker, by coordinating them to objective ends which are
external to his personality and consequently have no relation
with it.”106 Three consequences follow from this process: (a) less
and less personality is transformed into the work of those who
perform it, so that man cannot express himself in what he does
and is cut off from the world of action; (b) work assumes an
objective life and dominates the individual from outside: once
production is taken away from the worker the true producer
becomes capital itself; (c) all forms of work become the same
and uniformity governs all aspects of life from bureaucracy to
education to the industrial work process. Under such conditi-
ons individuality becomes problematic while all values are in-
creasingly subjectivistic and relativistic. For the interiority of
the soul provides the last refuge in a world where man is conti-
nuously threatened with the loss of his personality and cannot
find or create any value.

What Lukács criticizes in capitalism and the capitalistic

division of labor is the loss of teleology in the work-process (i.e.,

far as the revolution opens the possibility for a new foundation
of Culture, i.e., for a “true realization” of the Absolute in the
world. Similarly, he accepts the proletariat only to the extent
that it can prove to be the “true heir of classical German philo-
sophy,” and the carrier of the values the bourgeoisie had posed
as ideas but had been unable to realize.

In conclusion, whether in 1909 Lukács was or was not a re-

volutionary, it is clear that the revolution he was aspiring to was
a cultural revolution, and by the same token that the socialist re-
volution appealed to him only as the promise of a new Kultur. It
is also clear that what Arato defines as his refusal of capitalism
stems from the viewpoint of a bourgeois manqué – that rejects
capitalism because he realizes that the new capitalist organiza-
tion of work has left no social space and function for his skills
and thus has literally displaced him from the world.

considering Lukács’ “revolutionary” period. Much has been
written on the suddenness with which Lukács joined the Com-

The False Adventures of Consciousness

SECTION I – THE POSSIBILITY OF RATIONAL ACTION

In 1918 Lukács joined the Communist Party and began an intense
period of political activity which, within a short time, signaled
him as the most controversial theoretician in the West. As it is
well known, History and Class Consciousness (hereafter referred to
as HCC) – Lukács’ major work in this period – soon became the
focus of a heated debate within the Marxist camp, which in some
ways has continued to the present.1 Hailed on one side as the
recovery of the “revolutionary core” of Marxism, condemned on
the other as “deviationism,” the perspective developed by Lukács
in HCC has remained the best example of that variety of Hegeli-
an historicism that goes under the name “Western Marxism.” In
1924, after being attacked by the Comintern, Lukács recanted his
position, bowed to orthodox dialectical materialism and after a
prolonged period of silence2 returned to his work in aesthetics.

first task Marxism is called on to perform is solving the ontologi-

Only nominally had Lukács overcome Kant’s duality between

the rational and the empirical by posing the realm of empiria as
non-essential and thus non-real. Aside from its internal incon-
sistencies, this assumption faced greater difficulties in the face
of Lukács’ attempt to prove the possibility of rational action,
which his de facto disposal of empiria as illusory excluded. For
the devaluation of empiria was the devaluation of history, which
in the theory of Form appeared as the realm of the irrational,
the arbitrary, the very symbol of the limitations humanity encounters
in the path towards self-actualization.13 Yet, if rational action was
to be possible, history could not be dismissed as irrational or
illusory but had to be proven continuous with meaning, value
and reason.

conceptuality. Undoubtedly, Praxis is a more objective category

however, he was deeply indebted. This is especially true of Sim-

That Bergson plays such an important part in Lukács’ per-

spective is not accidental. Among 20th-century philosophers,
Bergson is the one whose ontology is more directly shaped on
the process of Art-creation. Reality, for Bergson, is a creative
life-process, where each moment is tied to the others by purely
internal relations and is the birthplace of infinite possibilities.
As in the organic rhythm of life, nothing in this process has
any reality except the process itself, where subject and object,
conceptual and preconceptual, past and future are fused into an
ever present, generative act.24 On this basis, already in Bergson
we find a theory of reification, i.e., the assumption that objects
are a product of the falsifying activity of the intellect, which
introduces distinctions and separations in what is a living, orga-
nic and therefore indivisible flux. It is the intellect, then, that
creates the world of things and, more in general, the illusion of
an independent material reality. For in reality no things exist,
but only actions. As Colletti points out in Marxism and Hegel,
Bergson’s theory of reification is mainly confined to a critique
of the intellect.25 At the same time, this epistemological critique
contains the germs of a social critique of science and technology,

For Hegel’s dialectical method promised a historical overco-

logical order progressively unfolding in the course of historical
becoming. Against the “bad infinity of Simmel’s alternation bet-

had plagued his previous position, while avoiding the dangers

logical stages in the course of historical becoming. Thus, He-

its elements to an undifferentiated uniformity”35 but introduces
between them a principle of order which is the guarantee of
their intelligibility.

Within the dialectics of the whole not only is every moment

connected to the others by purely internal relations, but even
contradictory elements lose their seemingly irrational character.
Far from being negated (as in the theory of Form) or collapsed
into an immediate identity (as in the romantic totality of Lebens-
philosophie), in the Hegelian dialectics contradictions are assumed as
necessary and therefore rational moments in the process where-
by the totality realizes itself. For otherness – the very moment
of alienation – is required by the logic of the whole which must
externalize itself – pose itself as “other” – in order to achieve
self-consciousness and self-knowledge.

From a socio-historical perspective this means that even ca-

pitalist reification appears as an intelligible process, once un-

In this context, the assumption of the dialectical method ap-

pears to Lukács as the answer to both the ethical stoicism and
the epistemological dualism that had characterized neo-Kan-
tian methodology. For the assumption of a dialectical relation
governing the development of the real implies that reality, and
first of all history itself, is the manifestation of an intrinsic ratio-
nality which operates even in its most contradictory aspects. In
particular, it implies that what we take as epistemological and
ontological contradictions (subjectivity and objectivity, ideality
and facticity, conceptuality and empiria) are not separate orders
confronting each other from the outside, but the extreme po-
larities of a unitary process, each necessitating the other for its
existence.

This means that neither subjectivity nor objectivity, neither

conceptuality nor empiria are fixed givens but each acquires its
properties through the other pole of the dialectical relation by
which they mutually exist. What follows then from the assump-
tion of reality as a dialectical totality is that there is an episte-
mological and ontological continuity between what appear as
different and even opposite aspects of reality. Indeed, the very
separation between ontology and epistemology has no reason
to exist, for it is impossible to separate what we assume as an
independent, objective reality from our conceptual formulation
of it. Thus, according to Lukács, from a dialectical viewpoint no
separation exists between history and nature (and therefore his-
torical and natural sciences).36 For Nature, which is the utmost
embodiment of supposedly immutable reality preceding in its
determinations every form of consciousness, is a socio-histori-
cal construct, forever changing, relatively to the transformati-
ons in our theoretical and practical attitude towards the world.

itself on a higher level. That is, capitalism is the product of the
“ruse of the Geist” which must totally subjectivize itself in order
to know itself as totally objective: Idea become flesh, Will beco-
me Reason, Form which in forming the world takes the entire
world as its content.

We have pointed out, however, that by the nature of the

Hegelian dialectics to which Lukács subscribes, every negation
becomes a new position and vice versa. In this case, the posi-
tivistic subsumption of capitalism as a necessary stage in the
development of a historical teleology brings Lukács back, full
circle, to an existentialist dimension. For when the contradic-
tions of capitalist society are reinstated by the dialectics as a
historical necessity the door is opened to their assumption as an
inevitable human condition. Thus, it is no surprise that in HCC,
as in his previous literary writings, historical determinism and
voluntarism go together, and that the ultimate message of the
book is an ethical appeal to assume one’s responsibility:

Just because the (immanent) meaning of reality shines
forth with an ever more resplendent light the meaning of
the process is (hidden) ever more deeply in the (momen-
tary, spatial, and temporal aspects of phenomena). The
path (of) consciousness throughout the course of histo-

more arduous and requires an ever-greater responsibility.60

What remains to be developed is to what extent Lukács’

conception of “totality and Historical dialectics.” The first
major difference between Lukács and Marx is that Lukács un-
derstands capitalist society through the category of “reification”
rather than, as in Marx, through the analysis of accumulation of
surplus labor and exploitation. Three major consequences fol-
low from this change in perspective. 1) Lukács’ critique centers
not on the exploitation of labor but on the alienation of man in
front of the machine; 2) capitalist relations are explicitly pre-
sented as a reality of consciousness, which in themselves have
no material reality; 3) the identification of capitalist relations
with reification-alienation opens the door to an existentialist-
humanistic critique of capitalism, where reification appears as a
universal social disease, a universal “human condition.” For un-
like exploitation, which in Marx implies a specific class relation

from the factory and the intellectual (who lets the “faculties of

SECTION III – CLASS CONSCIOUSNESS
AND FALSE CONSCIOUSNESS

What remains to be analyzed is whether Lukács succeeds in
answering Weber’s skepticism concerning the rationality of po-
litical action, as well as his criticism of socialism. As we have
seen, the attempt to go beyond Weber is one of the major tasks
of the book. Yet, HCC is possibly the most Weberian of Lukács’
works. For instead of changing presuppositions, Lukács accepts

It is primarily in his analysis of capitalism and socialism that

inevitably placed at the level of consciousness. Thus, echoing
Weber’s distinction between capitalism and socialism as two
different forms of rationality (formal and material, respectively),
Lukács argues that what distinguishes the capitalist from the
proletarian is the structure of their consciousness.

concept of class is defined not in terms of any social and econo-

essentially characterized by its degree of approximation to the
consciousness of the whole.

It is no coincidence then that in trying to answer Weber,

Lukács is forced to wage an epistemological battle which sup-
posedly demonstrates the superiority of the “proletarian view-
point.” For as soon as consciousness is taken as the distinguis-
hing factor between different political positions also the choice
between them becomes an act of consciousness. In front of this
outcome, Weber had confined political choices to the realm of
the irrational. Starting from the basic “fact” of universally alie-
nating social relations, only irrational elements could in Weber’s
view determine the choice between different social “values.” In
this respect Lukács parts ways with Weber and tries to ground

the proletariat which are modelled on the development of the
Hegelian Geist rather than on the movements of the contempo-
rary working class.

Lukács, in fact, tries to demonstrate the objective necessi-

ty and rationality of the proletarian viewpoint by (a) assuming
history as a rational, teleological process, i.e., as Reason and
Progress, (b) postulating a perfect isomorphism between the
reasons of history and the reasons of the class. In particular,
Lukács postulates a complete identity between the viewpoint of
the proletariat (or the socialist perspective) and the perspective
of the Totality of History; with the result that the intentionality
of the former appears to be grounded in an objective rationality.

It is from the viewpoint of this “historical optimism” that

Lukács rejects Weber’s refusal of political action as irrational as
well as his criticism of socialism. However, to what extent Lu-
kács’ solution is rooted in an act of historical faith rather than
in the dynamics of social and class relations is shown by the fact
that throughout HCC he is continually confronted with con-
tradictions and that only through a metaphysical leap is he able
to reconcile his own presuppositions. These contradictions are
particularly evident in his analysis of the proletariat and class
consciousness which is the keystone of his conceptual scheme.

Lukács starts from the premise that the proletariat is the

first historical agent that in knowing itself knows the totality
of social and historical relations. On the one side, then, Lukács
assumes that the consciousness of the proletariat is the self-
consciousness of the Totality. At the same time, however, Lu-
kács maintains that the capitalist organization of work induces
a “false consciousness” in the empirical class which prevents it
from achieving a totalizing perspective on history and society.
Lukács argues, in fact, that capitalism destroys work as a teleo-

107

logical and holistic activity, since the proletariat no longer pro-
duces the totality of the object but is confined to partial and
fragmentary operations. Furthermore, capital submits the work
process to laws out of control of the proletariat. Thus, instead
of being active in his work the proletariat becomes totally pas-
sive; instead of being a Subject it is reduced to a total Object:
mere labor power, an instrument of capitalist will. As a conse-
quence, the proletariat cannot understand the world (beginning
with social relations) as its own creation, and takes these rela-
tions as natural and immutable things.

But if the condition of the proletariat is a condition of “false

consciousness” and alienation how can the proletariat be the
self-consciousness of the whole?

It is significant that in trying to solve this contradiction Lu-

kács resorts to the Weberian notion of “objective possibility,” a
methodological category which in Weber indicates the types of
actions an agent would perform if it were perfectly conscious of
its ends and environment. Weber uses this concept to construct
a series of ideal-types, i.e., a typology of forms of consciousness
(as, for example, the Protestant “ethic”), which allegedly explain
the forces operating in historical actions.

In this sense there is an important continuity between We-

ber’s ideal types and objective possibilities and the many forms
which had populated Lukács’ early writings (as, e.g., the essayist
or the tragic hero) with the difference that Weber never postu-
lated a Totalizing Subject behind his ideal types as Lukács did
in the theory of Form. More over, in the young Lukács, not only
were these ideal forms the manifestation of a higher principle;
one of them (the essayist) provided a privileged perspective on the
Totality for it reflected not a particular aspect of Form but Form
as such in all its transcendental purity. With changed termin-
ology, the same dynamics operates also in HCC where first Lu-
kács shapes a proletarian class consciousness as an ideal-type,
an ideal form of consciousness and then in order to escape from

arbitrariness and relativism identifies this consciousness with
the Totality itself. Thus, in more than one way class conscious-
ness plays in Lukács’ new period the same function the essayist
played in his first literary writings: both are ideal-types of con-
sciousness but at the same time they are privileged viewpoints,
privileged perspectives through which the Totality reveals and
actualizes itself as such. We must add that far from being a coin-
cidence this “similarity” is an important indication of the conti-
nuity between the problematics of the young Lukács and that of
the Marxist revolutionary. That also in HCC Lukács must resort
to the assumption of a particular perspective in order to show
that the Totality can manifest itself in the world is symptomatic
of his inability to overcome the metaphysical presupposition of
his early period where the assumption of reality as an Absolute
Totality continuously reproposed an unbridgeable gap with the
realm of empiria. Most important, Lukács’ conception of “class
consciousness” and in general his use of the notion of objective
possibility well indicates the ideological nature of his answer to
Weber. Like Weber, Lukács confines political and social change
to a confrontation between different forms of consciousness,
i.e., to a battle between different values and ethics. However,
unlike Weber, he assumes the one of these perspectives gi-
ves the objective historical truth, for it is not one perspective
among others but the perspective of the Historical totality. The
problem is that in this case truth and objectivity are reached by
means of an arbitrary identification between a particular histo-
rical reality (the viewpoint of the proletariat, class conscious-
ness) and a metaphysical process which being a priori given and
totally completed must necessarily be outside of history. Lukács
makes it clear, in fact, that in order to reach class consciousness
we must assume the Totality, and most important, that the con-
text of this “objective possibility” is a priori determined by the
Totality itself. As Lukács puts it:

By relating consciousness to the whole of society it be-

is neither the sum nor the average of what is thought or
felt by the single individuals who make up the class.72

This means that what class consciousness must be is not de-

of the proletariat but it is a priori deduced from an ideal reality,
from whose viewpoint all historical action is already defined
and completed.

In this context, however, the categorization of class consci-

ousness as an objective possibility appears as a tautological pro-
position that simply restates the premises that it was supposed to
demonstrate. For the gap still remains open between class con-
sciousness as consciousness of the Totality and the consciousness
of the empirical proletariat which by Lukács’ own premises is
equated with ordinary thinking, immediacy, the here-and-now
and therefore is considered unable to view history as a whole. In
other words, the assumption of class consciousness as an objec-
tive possibility fails to overcome the paradox, which runs deep at
the core of Lukács’ perspective, of a class consciousness the class
does not possess. Thus, in front of the split between a class which
is divorced from its alleged consciousness and a consciousness
which is divorced from the class it allegedly belongs to, Lukács is
forced to search for yet a new level of mediation.

SECTION I – BEYOND THE MIDDLE POINT

The 1930s mark the beginning of a new phase in Lukács’ in-
tellectual development: a new radical break with the past, in
the course of which all his philosophical and political categories
appear to change. Undoubtedly, the immediate cause of this re-
valuation was the condemnation of HCC by the Third Interna-
tional in 1924 as an example of Left-wing extremism. However,
the critique of the Communist International would not have
had such a devastating effect on Lukács if new historical proces-
ses had not completely changed the political situation in Euro-
pe. It was the rise of fascism as well as the Stalinist turn of the
Communist International that for Lukács, as well as for many
European intellectuals, marked the end of an era and imposed
the need for a new course. In both cases, what came to an end
was the belief in the imminence of the revolution, i.e., the belief
that the revolution was “on the agenda” which had shaped the
political context in which HCC had been produced.

process of theoretical reformulation, whose starting point was

The transformation Lukács’ perspective undergoes after his re-

applies to every sphere of the real.

In Lukács’ view, the change with respect to HCC is comple-

Thus, in assessing the change Lukács’ perspective undergoes in
the 1930s, it is appropriate to say that Lukács brings to their
logical conclusions his previous premises while eliminating
the internal contradictions still present in HCC. From now on,
Marxism becomes explicitly the science that studies the laws
governing the movement of the real, and, as such, the science of
all sciences, capable of legislating for every discipline, from phi-
losophy to aesthetics to the natural sciences.

basis of his interpretation of historical materialism. In fact, what
