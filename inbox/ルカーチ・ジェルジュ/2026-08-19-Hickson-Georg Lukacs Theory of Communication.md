KBSTRACT

This paper develops Lukacs's interpretations of
Marx's historical dialectic, reification, and class consciousness ané
relates these elements to contemporary communication theory. For
Lukacs, the core of Marx's writing is the historical dialectic, the
most vital interaction of which is the relation between subject and
object in the historical process. This subject/object relationship
occurs only in the proletariat and involves reification, whereby the
individual worker is “instrumentalized" and the object of tae
individual's work is "naturalized." Lukacs believes that reification
creates only a minimal consciousness of alienation; so the next step
is the development of a class struggle, in which many workers
recognize their alienation and transmit this recognition to one
another. Since there remains a possibility for the proletariat to
develop class consciousness, this paper expresses the need for
communication theory scholars to develop a clearer understanding of
the history and role of the dialectical method, in order to develop
some answers to the problems of dehumanization, alienation, and
reification prevalent in Western culture today. The paper concludes
that a reinterpretation of Lukacs's writings offers "building blocks"
with which one might develop a methcd for predicting and analyzing
human communication. (RL)

THE COMMUNICATION THEORY OF GEORG LUKACS

Born in Budapest, Hungary, in 1885, Georg Lukacs displayed an interest
in literature at an early age. In 1906, he obtained a doctorate in
philosophy in Budapest, but his intellectual hunger continued after he had
received his ''terminal degree'' so he attended lectures of the sociologist~
philosopher Georg Simmel at Berlin University; in addition, during the years,
1913-1914, he became a member of the intellectual circle surrounding German
sociologist Max Weber. The impact of the works of Georg Lukacs varies
depending upon the critic. In an Intel lectyal portrait of Lukacs, George
Lichtheim called him ‘an important writer.''' Perhaps, though, this is the
bias of many biographers. ‘Lukacs said of himself that he was "'part of this
‘pathetically grotesque’ group of intellectuals."

Essentially, Lukacs' work indicates that he was a man in search of him-
self. He wanted to be a revolutionary in a country without revolutionary
fervor. He wanted to be a philosopher, but his central European background
tended to thwart energies in that direction. He wanted to be an aesthete,
but he apparently lacked the creativity. tna hostile work, Professor
Victor Zitta claims that the man (Lukacs) had failed both as a poet and as
a philosopher, thus he decided to become a literary critic and essayist.
Zitta writes that Lukacs was "active in the domain of intellectual life
where excellence does not necessarily bring fame and where creativity and
significance do not come the easy way, where genius Is muffed by the fact
that it has to subordinate itself to commentary and analysis."

Yet it was in the roles of critic and essayist that Lukacs found success.
Although he attempted to participate in politics at the party level and in
the government (as Deputy Commissar and as Commissar for Education), his
activities in politics were little more than cursory. As an eesthete, his
contributions would have to be considered negligible when, compared with other
Marxist writers, such as Bertholt Brecht and Thomas Mann.

There are available, then, both positive and negative evaluations of the
contributions of Georg Lukacs. Probably his most significant contribution,
however, was the writing of History and Ciass Consciousness. In the preface
of that book Lukacs states that his goal is to present_"'an interpretation,
an exposition of Marx's theory as Marx understood it." This task
certainly would be a monumental one, yet Lukacs adds: ''Marx's warning not 6
to treat Hegel as a ‘dead dog' has gone unheeded even by many good Marxists."'
It Is here that we find the essence of the contribution of Lukacs: he placed
Hegel back Into Marxism. We must remember, however, that Lukacs repudiated
the contribution later when_he sacrificed ''intellectual integrity on the
alter of party solidarity."

The central focus of this paper will be to develop three elements In

the work of Lukacs: dialectic, reification, and class cogsciousness and
their relationships to contemporary communication theory.

For Lukacs, the core of Marx's writing was In his method. Writes Lukacs:

The method to which Lukacs refers is obviously the historical dialectic.
This dialectic's most vital interaggion is ''the relation between subject and
object in the historical process." The central problem for this dialectic
is to change reality.

To develope this method, as with any method, one must first begin with
facts. The social scientist, however, must discover his data differently
from his physical and biological counterpart. Facts must be developed in
totality from history, not from the compartmental ization of variables,
"Concrete reality is, therefore, the category that governs reality."
Reality is a process, ever-changing, a totality, a social process.

The underlying premise of the dialectic is: ‘''It is not men's
consciousness that dtermines their existance, but on the contrgry,
their social existance that determines thelr consciousness. '"' Only
through capitalism did consciousness ever become possible. In this process,
the proletariat became ''at the same time the subject and object of its own
knowledge.''! Because economic circumstances created the proletariat, it is
only through a knowledge of the historical, process that the proletariat can
give direction to any action it may take.

The first premise of the dialectic is that history is comprised of
subjects and objects; and this combination posits a tetality.

Second, this totality can be understood only by object~subjects.

Third, there is but one group in capitalistic society that can be
object-subjects-~the proletariat.

Fourth, the proletariat must be aware of a second dialectical relation-
ship in phe philosophy of history. This is the dialectic of theory and
praxis. !

Fifth, through the development of reification and eventually class
consciousness, the proletariat is to be able to determine the proper approach
to the theory-praxis dialectic and ultimately to grasp the ''moment'' for class
conflict.

Andrew Arato, writing on Lukacs theory of reification, defines the term

It was Lukacs contention that 19th century Marxism could not transcend

Because of material relations between persons (as in a consumer rélationship)

Instead of human beings beoming ‘'things'' as a result of their work,

human qualities. In developing his approach to reification, Lukacs was never
criticized by his hostile critics (neither the Stalinists nor the bourgeois).
Lukacs' originality in this realm lies in his bringing together Marx's
critique of the political economy and the development of Western rationality

Although Lukacs is concerned with a subjective dialectic of reification,
we can discuss the subjective and objective aspects of this dialectic. At the
first level, we assume that all of realily is contemplation; this is the
objective side. Lukacs contended, however, that one could not arrive at
the subject-object interaction by way of assuming philosophy (as did Hegel)
to be obj ggt ive. Instead, we must find a subject-object'in the same
location. By critiquing German philosophy, Lukg¢s arrived at the same
conclusion as did Marx through a different route. Success is found in
the proletariat, the identical subject-object of the historical process.
Thus, the first dialectic involves the subject-object of the investigation,
the proletariat. The second dialectic is that of mediation, the objective
historical process. It should be noted at this point that this notion of
Lukacs' has been criticized by Habermas for its creating of objective
necessity as opposed to an objective pagg Oi tity; that is, Habermas claims
that Lukacs' position is deterministic. Lukacs' notion still has some
value, depending on how one interprets his mediation process.

Class Consciousness

According to G. H. R. Parkinson, the "objective possibility’ is at the
core of Lukacs' consciousness. Parkinson emphasizes that in this view, class
consciousness ‘is not the sum, or even the average, of what the individual
members of a class think, feel, etc.; it is what they would do in a situation
of a certain type, if they were to grasp that situation properly.''36 Con-
sciousness is based upon totality and dialectica! relationship between theory
and practice. As has been stated only the proletariat is capable of grasping
class consciousness, and to assert itself in the class struggle the pro-
letariat must grasp its self-knowledge.

Writes Lukacs, "Now class consciousness consists in the fact of the
appropriate and rational reactions ‘lapyted . .to a particular typical
posLtion in the process of production."' Consciousness is not, then,
reducible to sums, averages, or psychological states of given individuals.
The proletariat needs a holisgic, practical analysis of the historical
function of class in society.

One might ask the question: How does false consciousness occur? It
may occur either in objectives chosen or in terms of action. Lukacs relates
the example of the petty bourgoisie. He indicates that this class is exposed
to the influence of capitalism and yet is unaffected by the fact of class
conflict between the proletariat and the bourgoisie. Lukacs quotes Marx
Stating that as a transition class, ghe petty bourgods will "imagine itself
to be above all class antagoni sms." Thus, the petty bourgois will not
take an active part in any class struggle, neither will the peasants unless
there is some type of external upheaval. The bourgoisie cannot accept
consciousness because it would spell ruination for the entire group. False
consciousness arises out of self-interest (as in the case of the bourgoisie),
selective perception (as with the petty bourgoisie), or through the lack
of organization (as with disparate sectors of peasants). Thus, the only
class capable of class consciousness is the proletariat, and the only
appropriate time for such an occurrence is during a capitalistic period.

Consciousness is enpresags "as the irreconcilable antagonism between
ideology and economic base.' To make a reconciliation or perhaps "to

cover up’', the bourgoisie developed a coherent theory of economics, politics
and society. It is the job of a class conscious proletariat to uncover the
contradictions through an understanding of history. Thus, consciousness is
an awareness of the historical role of class.

Lukacs stated that ''Man is subject in a world of objects, and everything
that attests to his creative power is a step toward that complete

Communication Theory ,

Today, the objective possibility of class consciousness remains. The
question arises how this concept may fit into contemporary communication
theory. Let us look at some of the systems through which we may arrive at
conclusions. In Western society these have been three. They are induction,
deduction and the dialectic. While we know that each of these methods has
its place, the dialectical system has been used only sparingly. 4 Scholars
of communication theory need to develop a clearer understanding of the history
and role of the dialectical method in the analysis of invention.

Only through this first clarification can we begin to develop some
answers to the problems of dehumanization, alienation and reification
prevalent in Western culture today. There are some promising signals in
this direction. Sennet and Cobb's The Hidden Injuries of Class illustrates
some of the fundamental psycho-sociological injury inherent in our class
system. +5 Key's Subliminal Seduction opens our awareness of technology
as a factor in dehumanizing our communication abilities. Works such as
these and those of Ernest Becker bring us to a clearer understanding of the
natural-technological untorecurrgats that affect our social systems and
ultimately humanity in general.

Lukacs' conceptualization of reification and class consciousness may
also fit with other contemporary analyses of communicative behavior. The
symbolic interactionist, for instance, may find the: concept of reificat) gp
helpful in explaining behavior from this perspective. As Hubert Blumer
notes, ''The first premise /of symbolic interaction/ is that human beings 4
act toward things on the basis of the meanings that things have for them.'' 9
More importantly, however, is the second premise whereby ''the meaning of
such things Is derived from, gr arises out of, the social interaction that
one has with one's fellows.’ Taking the second premise as an extension.
of the first we can make a case whereby reification, as defined here,
offers an interpretation of that action and class consciousness an explan-
ation of how man derives the ''meaning of such things.'' Since meanings
are the product of social interactions, Lukacs' contribution to the
symbolic interactionist would further speci fy how the antecedents (i.e.,
the dialectic) and the degree of reification and class consciousness
affect the behavior of the individual.

Closely aligned to this reinterpretation of contemporary approaches
to canmunication theory via Lukacs would be role theory. In this case
one might predict that the strain toward consistency or adjustment within
the role is a function of reification and, further, that role expectations
are governed by the individual's understanding (grasp) of class conscious-
ness. From such an interpretatia@ one might predict that given the
individual's totality (social consciousness), his understanding of reification

7

and awareness cf class consciousness the individual will adopt certain
specific role expectations and interactions will be governed from those
expectations.

Other approaches to communication similarily can be reinterpreted from
Lukacs' view. Given that we can specify the relation of subject and object
within the history of the interaction, the relation between "'things'' emerging
from the dialectic and grasp the class consciousness held by the individuals
within the interaction, we can predict the communicative behaviors of each.
What Lukacs offers us that other historical methods do not in studying
communication is the ability to predict future behavior and events. Based
on the dialectic we can specify the antecedent conditions necessary for the
behavior to take place and, by specification of the development of reification
and the degree of class consciousness, predict differing outcomes dependent
upon these three conditions.

Conclusion

In this paper we have attempted to explicate the communication theory
of Georg Lukacs. From a reinterpretation of his writings we have of fered
"building blocks'' with which one might develop a method for predicting and
analyzing human communication. This method, we believe, offers a potential
alternative explanation to the analysis of human communicat! ve behavior
predicated upon the dialectical nature of man and his social interactions.
