Any biographer of Schopenhauer must find Wilhelm von
Gwinner’s standard work, Schopenhauers Leben, indis-
pensable. A friend and disciple of the philosopher, Gwinner
was given special access to most of the valuable material.
The present volume has also drawn upon the additional
material furnished by Frauenstadt, Grisebach and other
friends and students of Schopenhauer and has profited by
the letters, posthumous papers, and Grisebach’s edition of
Schopenhauer’s works.

The author wishes to express his indebtedness to Alice I.
Bryan and Houston Peterson for much help and criticism
in the preparation of this book.

feiaGhk AY GUIDE OF A CENTURY

N Frankfurt am Main in the Friedhof Cemetery a black

granite gravestone is to be seen engraved with the name
of Arthur Schopenhauer, with no date, nor epitaph nor any
other word. Arthur Schopenhauer. There was no need for
another syllable. “They will find me,” the great pessimist
had said. “The time will come when anyone who does not
know my doctrines will be accounted an ignoramus.” This
proud prediction, made at a time when Schopenhauer was
surrounded with a silence and neglect so deep and ominous
as to shatter the confidence of most men, is now all but
true. For if there is one modern philosopher who is known
and even read by every cultured person, it is Schopenhauer.
He is read by philosophers, by scientists, artists, musicians,
and writers of all kinds; he has become the private passion
of merchants and students and priests, his dark thoughts
proving a mercy, in time of need, to minds of the greatest
diversity, and there are few great men who have not felt at
some time or other in their career, the weight of his influence.

The glorious acrid old man who taught that the world is
vanity and nothingness has a greater following in the world

than any of his contemporaries who praised it. In Dantzig,

~ Schopenhauer’s birthplace, a cult has sprung up in his honor
which since 1912 has published yearbooks celebrating his
character and works with contributions from admirers all
over Europe. His writings, of course, have been translated
into many languages with commentaries and criticisms and
general appreciation. In this country no philosopher sells so
well, unless it is his disciple, Nietzsche, and the manifold
force of his various influence, affecting as it does such a wide
circle of readers, it would be difficult to overestimate.
Schopenhauer himself, though he did not live to see the
full effect of his teaching, never once doubted its value or its
ultimate success. The truth will out. He dedicated his great
work, The World as Will and Idea, to humanity in full con-
fidence that sooner or later it would be accepted as the final
completion of Plato and Kant. Yet for thirty-five years after
the publication of this book (1819) he was doomed to suffer
the neglect of his contemporaries, to wait in bitter anxiety
for recognition and sympathy which never came, to see his
masterpiece ignored while the spurious wisdom of his rivals,
the university professors, sounded about him in brazen tri-
umph. Schopenhauer bided his time. He knew that in the
end the public would tire of the “empty bombast ” of “that
charlatan Hegel” as of the vain confusions of Fichte and
Schelling. In the meantime it came hard even to the arch-
pessimist to bear with such injustice, and the reward of
posterity must have seemed at times a poor return and a
hollow comfort. As conscious as he was of his own genius
and the sacred truth of his philosophy carrying blessedness
and hope to humanity, he could not be content with him-

self alone, and though he expressed, as the years went by,
always greater contempt for the public, he longed for its
applause and concurrence.

With his faithful dog beside him he brooded in the lonely
mornings and scanned the papers intently for some mention
of his name or works. Not a line was to be found. He read
many papers and the years went by, withdrawing him more
and more from the amiable exchange and sympathy of his
fellows, increasing his rancor and exasperation. At times his
suspicions rose to a sharp crescendo. He saw dark forms be-
hind his sorrow. It was the university professors who were
plotting against him, jealous of his overriding genius, fear-
ful of the truth he had proclaimed. It was they who had
walled him up in isolation by their “ conspiracy of silence,”
defeating by a concerted policy of neglect a true philosophy
they were fearful of meeting in open attack. This strange
idea became a settled conviction with Schopenhauer. In the
midst of his philosophical discussions we find brilliant bits
of invective, slashing attacks on his famous contemporaries,
which must have appeared at the time quite mad and as-
tounding. But even these failed to break the silence or draw
the professors forth. Schopenhauer saw it clearly. These pro-
fessional philosophers were paid by the state, dutiful to the
church, obliged to the students, and subservient to many
other interests besides. Philosophy had become a business in
Germany and the guild had ousted him. The more he con-
sidered the mercenary character of German philosophers,
their pomposity and complicated stupidity, in particular their
pretended ignorance of him, the more horrified he became.

The university professors, he concluded, like the Sophists,
for whom Socrates expressed so much contempt, were bread
and butter philosophers, who prostituted high reason and
cherished falsity and favor.

A result of the long years of neglect was an increasing
loneliness, suspicion and rancor, with nightmares and hor-
rors as peaks to his unhappiness. At times his anxiety cul-
minated in the fear that his great work, The World as Will
and Idea, might be lost altogether and never come to light.
His thirty-five years of desperate anxiety on this account
with all the attendant evils constitute the tragedy in Scho-
penhauer’s life. His staunch, strong-hearted endurance of
this tragedy over so long a period shows us “the granite
man ” of passionate strength and courage whom we admire:
not the classic, godlike perfection of Socrates, but the Gothic
_ or Faustian man, with a blend of the comic and the sublime.
Schopenhauer, too, though less dramatically and not nearly
so high-mindedly, drank his hemlock. 1 ihe

After thirty years of horrible stillness and obscurity, wait-
ing vainly for the aegis and renown of his genius, living
variously with foresight and caution his precious days, al-
ternating between the sage, the saint, the sensualist, and the
prudent man of the world, Schopenhauer saw that at last a
change had occurred and that his star, so long obscured, was
rising high. In these few years Germany had changed. In
Schopenhauer’s youth, while the World Will was focused
for the time in the dangerous soaring discontent of Napo-
leon, Germany retired haughtily from the barbarian inter-
ruption, to live inwardly, cherishing her high culture and

cultivating her cult of genius. Here again culture rose to a
peak of splendor. German poetry and philosophy culminated
in an explosion of sudden fruition. As in Periclean Athens
and Renaissance Italy, great men hobnobbed and conversed,
spun their masterpieces boldly, wastefully, and geniuses were
as then absurdly plentiful. Struck with the wonder of all.
this Germany guarded her good time. She became intensely
conscious of her geniuses. They became intensely conscious
of themselves. For Hegel, whose high idealism had begun —
to dominate the scene, the earth became again the center of
the universe, and Germany, the center of the earth. Hegel’s
philosophy appeared likewise, as Aristotle’s had, as the phi-
losophy of philosophies, the last icy pinnacle of wisdom, the
culmination not only of human reason but of the world
reason, in its manifold intricate development. Hegel sat
alone with his thought and the world arose and displayed
itself, logically, necessarily, in time and space, in the draw-
ing room and the kitchen. Reality appeared with all its in-
finite determinations. The logical method employed in this
system is called dialectic and is noted for its rigor, breadth,
and power. In its integrating, systematic procedure, it forms
a decided contrast to the brilliant intuitive method of
Schopenhauer.

Hegel, following the lead of the deepest doctrines of
Plato, Plotinus, Porphyry, etc., had developed one of the
most astounding and ingenious systems in history, concern-
ing which opinions now differ profoundly. In his own day,
however, (1810-30) Hegel’s mad glorious celebration of rea-
son made him the finest expression of that inwardness and

6 SCHOPENHAUER

- grandeur which Germany felt. More than anyone, he intel-
lectualized the mood of the Kulturmensch, gave him a world
built by reason; defended the truth of human institutions
—the church, the state—and placed their wisdom high
above that of individual men, such as romanticists and other
disturbers of the peace. And above all he placed the mean-
ingfulness, the rationality and protection of the Spirit of
World History. While Napoleon conquered Europe with
his tall marshals and famous cannon, Hegel, a proud man,
too, built the world out of reason. This made him the first
philosopher of his times and the bitterest obstacle to Scho-
penhauer’s fame.

As early as 1830, however, the reign of Hegel approached
its end. University students immersed in science and in-
clined to materialism were losing their confidence in pure
reason. Still Schopenhauer received no hearing. He con-
tinued to brood and meditate like his Hindu models, de-
testing the world and its vanities which the light of his in-
tellect enabled him to pierce at every turn, but also travelling
about in it, ever delighting in beauty and the forms of cul-
ture. At times his strict system of life was disturbed by petty
irregularities of the day at which he lamented uncommonly,
at times, by robust outbreaks of the natural man. As the
years went by he read prodigiously in the seven languages
which he had mastered, and wrote, on occasions, additions
to his youthful work of genius.

In 1850 he prepared his last work, Parerga und Paralt-
pomena, his “ last child” as he called it, but so low was his
reputation as a writer that it was only with the greatest

difficulty that he found a publisher. The sixty-two-year-old
philosopher had borne nothing but taunts and neglect. His
Fourfold Root of the Principle of Sufficient Reason had sug-
gested to his mother and others nothing but an apothecary’s
shop, while his treatise on Vision and the Colors had been
judged to be wholly without color. His great system itself
had been branded as “ warmed-up Fichteanism,” an epithet
all the more painful in that, of all philosophers, it was Fichte
he hated most. Now, the publishers hesitated and turned
pale at the thought of “risking” his last precious volume.
Oddly enough it was this work, so inauspiciously launched,
which won him his first recognition. Perhaps the lightest of
all his writings, it appeared at a time of political reaction
and unrest most favorable to its reception. Gradually articles
began to appear and flattering notices which charmed the
much-tired heart of Schopenhauer, in spite of his loftiness
and contempt for the world. Each morsel, however petty,
was a dear solace to an old craving which nothing else could
silence.

After a delay of thirty-five years, the rugged old man with
his hard decisive features overflowed with joy like a child
with its toys and could not contain himself. At times, to be
sure, his triumph seemed a hollow one and then his wrath
and bitterness spoke again. Why could it not have come
before? Why need the public have kept him waiting till one
foot was in the grave? At times it seemed to him that, by
demonic arrangement, the world only waited for his death
to celebrate his teachings in good earnest. This was a spe-
cial vexation. Schopenhauer now craved ample time and

leisure to enjoy his reputation carefully. He was, accord-
ingly, delighted to find one day that the sacred Vedas set the
life span of man at one hundred years, for the great pessimist
now dreamed of a long life, a sun-swept old age, seeing the
glory of his teachings in their progress about the world. So
happy and grateful was he amid the rising applause and ap-
preciation that he almost forgot to curse the fate of being; the
wheel of birth and death, women, even the university pro-
fessors escaped the measure of his abuse. The dangerous old
lion of Frankfurt began to enjoy his life uncommonly, while
his tension and sad vindictiveness unwound. With what exal-
tation he announces his victory! For forty years he had been
walled up by the envy of the university professors, and se-
creted so carefully that no sound could betray his existence to
the world. But “ now he has escaped and is running about the
world; ... In plain language, the misfortune they feared
more than anything has come to pass after all. In spite of
their having done their best to prevent it for more than a
generation by acting with united force, with rare constancy,
secreting and ignoring to a degree that is without example,
my books are beginning and henceforth will continue to
be read.”

Schopenhauer’s first disciple was a strange old councillor
of justice at Magdeburg, Dorguth, who sat up nights, and
kept his daughters up, to read them Schopenhauer, whom
he boldly announced as the first real, systematic thinker in
the whole history of literature. This old man, simple and
loyal, the first apostle of Pessimism and the Will, the master
called his trumpet, while the philosopher Frauenstadt, the

PROLOGUE 9

“arch-evangelist,” he called his trombone. Between them
the creed was propounded. “The trumpet” announced the
approach of the philosopher, while “the trombone,” open-
ing the master’s works, played therefrom the sad music of
the Will to Life, and Denial, in a way to suggest too much,
it is feared, the comic base melody of Mozart’s Don Juan.
Frauenstadt, like Dorguth, was not a great man nor a worthy
apostle and Schopenhauer’s confidence in him and his over-
estimate show that the shrewd misanthrope could prefer
at times a commonplace flattery to a skillful but critical
appreciation.

It was from England, to Schopenhauer’s great joy, that
he received his first real acknowledgment. In that land of
snobbery and genius and good sense, in which he had nar-
rowly escaped being born, and to which he had almost al-
ways felt drawn, as to “the most intelligent country of
Europe,” an article suddenly appeared in The Westminster
Review on Iconoclasm in German Philosophy in which his
own works were discovered to the world and praised most
gratifyingly. The English reviewer, John Oxenford, is ap-
parently quite astonished, and lost between a horror and
fascination — horror of this “gifted, bold, eccentric, this
terrible writer” with his sweeping destructiveness, and fas-
cination with his genius, his style and spirit. With Schopen-
hauer’s attack on university philosophy, Oxenford is in
complete agreement, and he sees an end to these grandiose
systems in the slashing satires of their opponent, but he
regrets that Schopenhauer should have devoted his great
talents to such gloomy and non-utilitarian ends. It was not

long before this article appeared in translation in a German
periodical, where it aroused great interest and curiosity. Who
was this bold original thinker? Where did he live? Gradu-
ally he was discovered. Plaudits and ovations poured in
from all sides. Great men, scientists, artists, literary men
sent him testimony and glory. Even the university profes-
sors had been won over. They wrote him acknowledgments
and sought his acquaintance, in which endeavor, however,
they were not always successful. Before Schopenhauer died _
in 1860, there had been a pilgrimage to his home in Frank-
furt, as to a shrine, great men acknowledging their master,
and toward the end a sort of Schopenhauer cult was
flourishing.

Even the Christian Church honored the old heretic, praised
his genius and courage, and at the last, in honoring the man,
neglected his open slurs and insults, his irony, his addiction
to Buddhism, his rejection of faith. At his burial service a
clergyman appeared in the name of the Evangelical Church,
regretting the waywardness of a brilliant man, but doing
full justice to his character and greatness. Our philosopher
thus departed with every benediction. Death itself had been
gracious and merciful, coming upon him like a sudden,
gentle sleep, just as he had always wished it should be. Such
a peaceful death has often been thought unbecoming to the
greatest of pessimists and many shake their heads at his
evident enjoyment of worldly fame, though why, for that
matter, a pessimist shouldn’t enjoy the few pleasures which
offer themselves in this world, it is difficult to say. Rather
it would seem that the happier the lot of the pessimist the

more disinterested and trustworthy his perception of the
evil of the world should appear.

Though Schopenhauer is known, above all else, as a pessi-
mist who taught that only through the denial of the Will is
blessedness achieved, many have insisted on taking him in
quite a different sense. Nietzsche, who claimed to have “un-
derstood him as though he had written for me,” asserts that
“ Schopenhauer has a second characteristic in common with
Montaigne, besides honesty; a joy that really makes others
joyful.” This true joyfulness, he adds, is only to be found in
victory; not a cheap, romantic joyfulness, therefore, but a
grim triumph with all the monsters of the soul and all the
deep cruelty of nature in view. Schopenhauer for Nietzsche
was always a conqueror, however dark and oppressed his
mood; even in his deep disclosure of evil and wickedness
there is joyfulness, the laughter of the gods. While reading
him, Nietzsche felt himself alone before tall trees, environed
by strength and sanity. It was from Schopenhauer that he
learned — what later saved him in his great affliction —to
walk erect, forbearing to whine. In this parodoxical reading
of the great pessimist many have followed Nietzsche’s lead
and the doctrine of the Superman, which has proved so
powerful a force in modern literature and modern life, has
its roots in Schopenhauer as well as in his brilliant disciple.
The courage and boldness he derived from Schopenhauer
has been a solace and quickening to many other minds.
Schopenhauer had taught the nothingness of the world and
evil of the Will with such glorious energy and fearlessness
that men were inspired by a very unexpected spirit, were

12 SCHOPENHAUER

tempted in fact to new summits of striving, to greater asser-
tion of the Will, which according to the master’s own doc-
trines must always prove vain and wicked.

Schopenhauer could set fire to volcanoes like Nietzsche,
Wagner, and Strindberg, but he could also inspire a milder
sort of man. In recent years a book has appeared called The
Optimism of Schopenhauer in which the author claims the
master to be an optimist in the sense that he rouses us to a
discernment of the tragedy of life, preparing us at the same
time, however, to meet all pain with courage and dignity.
Men who cannot be content with a fool’s paradise, which
may blow up any moment with grief and disillusion, wel-
come the stern lessons of pessimism and, learning the neces-
sity of evil, prepare themselves prudently for the worst in

bP)

this “worst of all possible worlds,” and manage in conse-
quence to live on quite comfortably. It is chiefly youth that
makes this adjustment and at this stage the philosophy of
Schopenhauer is irresistible. Sensitive, fearful, greatly dis-
mayed at the coldness and dearth of the world, which he
cannot but regard as a betrayal and a swindle, the young
man turns with comfort to the grim old pessimist and learns
to universalize his despair, and once more he is attuned to
nature and society. His despair has become the Will of the
World and, seeing there is no escape from his misery, he
becomes quite happy again. It is not, of course, the immatur-
ity of our philosopher which makes him the favorite of
youth; it is that youth itself is philosophical. As the young
man pauses before life, his childhood a sham behind him
and the future inexplicable ahead, metaphysical reverbera-

tions echo through his perplexities. He is alone before reality
and has not made those adjustments which are later to fit
him into a society which does not need to think. But, now,
Schopenhauer gives high company to his loneliness and
remedies his despair, for it is not suffering that hurts the
strong heart, but the meaninglessness of it. That, indeed, is
a personal injury which no free man can bear.

Many have found requitement and peace in Schopen-
hauer; some, like Nietzsche and Shaw, an inspiration for a
higher assertion of the Will. But most of his followers have
taken him at his word. Nietzsche and Shaw, rising above
pity and suffering, affirm the Superman. Tolstoy detests
him. In War and Peace, perhaps the greatest novel in the
modern era, he paints a black picture of Napoleon. We see
him grunting in his bath. He is fleshly, an impostor, a mon-
ster with not a trace of human feeling. We see him on the
battle-field. He is not a genius, but a charlatan, a cruel,
despotic adventurer. The night before Austerlitz the old
Russian general, Kutesoff, goes to bed with the rest of hu-
manity. He knows that battles are not won by strategy and
smiles wearily at the scheming generals. It is chance or the
fate of the world which will decide the issue next morning,
nor can Napoleon nor any hand stay the old necessity.
Tolstoy hates Napoleon as the principle of pride and de-
struction and his opinion of all great egoists is the same.
Like Schopenhauer, he is filled with the wonder and pity
of human lives. In his skillful portrayal of an elaborate
society scattered upon the vast dreary plains of Russia, along
which the rain walks endlessly, and death comes, and

14 SCHOPENHAUER

suffering and birth, we see how individual lives, awakened
from the deep unconsciousness of nature, peep out wistfully
at the world around them, live for a period their brief joys
and sorrows, with mystery about them, with hope and death
ahead, only to sink back at last, like millions before, into
the quietness of nature. Tolstoy’s world-pity, his view of the
pettiness of human striving and the wickedness of egoism,
stand in the closest relation to Schopenhauer’s pessimism.
When Tolstoy discovered Schopenhauer, he was forty years
old and, like Nietzsche, he could not have done with read-
ing him. Here is a philosopher, he wrote to a friend, who
speaks the truth honestly and scorns the dull inconsequences
and quibbles so common among modern philosophers. As
a white-haired old man of eighty-two, having left wife,
