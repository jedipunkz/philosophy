ABSTRACT

♢

This paper undertakes an extended comparative character analysis of two figures who, on
the surface, belong to entirely unrelated intellectual traditions: Arthur Schopenhauer (1788–
1860), the German philosopher of metaphysical pessimism, and Ahmad Musa Jibril (b. 1972), a
Palestinian-American Salafi preacher based in Dearborn, Michigan, whose online sermons have
been the subject of sustained academic attention within radicalization and extremism studies.
Rather than treating either figure devotionally, this paper isolates structural and dispositional
parallels in how each constructs a worldview of suffering, authority, and resistance to comfort
— while holding firmly to the position that resemblance in rhetorical or psychological structure
does not imply moral or intellectual equivalence.

A methodological caveat governs the whole exercise: Schopenhauer is a canonical philosopher
whose system has been debated for two centuries in open academic discourse; Jibril is not a
philosopher in any formal or credentialed sense, but a religious preacher whom researchers at
institutions such as George Washington University’s Program on Extremism have identified as
a significant node in the ideological ecosystem that has motivated Western foreign fighters.
That asymmetry is treated as data throughout, not relegated to a footnote.

The paper proceeds in six movements: an account of Schopenhauer’s philosophical system and
psychobiography; an account of Jibril’s public persona and documented rhetorical function,
bracketed from doctrinal theology; an expanded comparative framework organized across
eleven dimensions; a deep-dive treatment of convergences; a deep-dive treatment of diver-
gences, which the paper argues are analytically more important than the convergences; a
theoretical excursus drawing on Weberian charisma and significance-quest theory to explain
why a shared pessimistic anthropology can terminate in incompatible ethical outcomes; and a
concluding methodological reflection on the risks and value of this kind of comparison.

Keywords: Arthur Schopenhauer; Ahmad Musa Jibril; comparative character analysis; philo-
sophical pessimism; radicalization studies; charismatic authority; significance-quest theory;
Weberian charisma; ethics of suffering; Salafi rhetoric.

INTRODUCTION

1.1
COMPARATIVE CHARACTER ANALYSIS AS A METHOD

Comparative character analysis is a recognized mode of intellectual history and the sociology
of belief. Rather than asking whether two figures influenced one another causally, it asks
whether they occupy a similar position in the psychological and rhetorical architecture of
meaning-making — a similar diagnosis of the human condition, a similar self-conception
relative to the surrounding society, a similar rhetorical grammar — even where their historical
contexts, credentials, and consequences are wholly distinct. The method is used productively
in the study of charismatic authority (for instance, comparing religious reformers to political
demagogues, or mystics to revolutionary theorists) precisely because it isolates form from
content, allowing the analyst to ask what makes certain diagnoses of suffering and complacency
rhetorically powerful regardless of the metaphysical or political system in which they are
embedded.
This paper deliberately brackets theological content, per the terms of the inquiry: it does
not evaluate Islamic jurisprudence, Schopenhauer’s engagement with Buddhist and Vedantic
thought as doctrine, or the truth-value of either figure’s religious claims. Instead, it treats
both men as personalities constructing meaning under a perceived condition of suffering, and
asks how the architecture of that meaning-making compares — structurally, rhetorically, and
psychologically.

1.2
WHY THIS PAIRING IS ANALYTICALLY DEFENSIBLE

At first glance, pairing a nineteenth-century academic philosopher with a twenty-first-century
online preacher invites the objection that the comparison is merely provocative. The defense
offered here is that both figures are, independently of their subject matter, instances of a
recognizable psychological type: the self-styled clear-sighted outsider, who diagnoses the
majority of humanity as living inside a comfortable illusion, and who derives personal and
rhetorical authority from the claim to have seen through that illusion. This type recurs across
otherwise incompatible traditions — ascetic philosophy, prophetic religion, revolutionary
politics, countercultural art — and studying its recurrence is a legitimate object of comparative
inquiry, provided the analysis does not collapse the very different ethical payloads the type
can carry.

SOURCES AND LIMITS

Claims about Schopenhauer draw on standard readings of The World as Will and Representation
(1818/1844), On the Basis of Morality (1840), and Parerga and Paralipomena (1851), along with the
substantial secondary literature on his biography and reception. Claims about Ahmad Musa
Jibril draw on published research from the Program on Extremism at George Washington
University, the International Centre for the Study of Radicalisation (ICSR) at King’s College

troit Free Press, Newsweek, CBS News, BBC). No sermon, tweet, or lecture by Jibril is reproduced
at length in this paper; his positions are described in paraphrase and sourced to researchers
who have studied his rhetoric directly. This asymmetry in sourcing — two centuries of open
philosophical debate versus a decade of terrorism-studies literature — is itself part of what the
comparison reveals.

SCHOPENHAUER: THE WILL,
SUFFERING, AND WITHDRAWAL

2.1
THE METAPHYSICS OF THE WILL

Schopenhauer’s central claim is that the inner essence of reality is a blind, purposeless, insa-
tiable striving he calls the Will — a force underlying everything from the pull of gravity to
the growth of a plant to human desire and sexual attraction. Representation (the phenomenal
world as we perceive it, structured by space, time, and causality) is merely the Will’s out-
ward appearance; the “thing-in-itself” beneath appearance is this ceaseless, objectless striving.
Because the Will has no final goal — it strives simply to strive — existence is structured
around lack: desire, momentary satisfaction, boredom, and renewed desire. Schopenhauer’s
famous image is the pendulum swinging between pain (unsatisfied desire) and boredom (sat-
isfied desire with no new object to strive for); genuine, stable contentment is, on this account,
structurally unavailable.

2.2
SUFFERING AS THE DEFAULT CONDITION

For Schopenhauer, suffering is not an unfortunate accident of an otherwise good life but life’s
baseline state; pleasure is merely the temporary absence of pain rather than a positive good
in its own right. This inverts the commonsense assumption that happiness is the norm and
suffering the deviation. He extends this pessimism to all sentient life, not merely humans — a
point that becomes important in the comparative section below, since it grounds an ethics of
compassion that is explicitly cross-species.

2.3
AESTHETIC CONTEMPLATION AND THE GENIUS

Schopenhauer’s system is not purely bleak: he identifies two partial escapes from the tyranny
of the Will. The first is aesthetic contemplation — in moments of absorbed appreciation
of art, especially music (which he regarded as a direct copy of the Will itself, unmediated
by representation), the individual temporarily ceases to be a striving, desiring subject and
becomes a pure, will-less “subject of knowing.” The genius — the artist, the philosopher — is
someone unusually capable of sustaining this will-less contemplation and translating it into

work that lets others glimpse it too. This is the closest Schopenhauer comes to valorizing an
exceptional individual standing apart from the deluded masses, and it is worth flagging as
the philosophical seed of the “elect few who truly see” trope that recurs, in a very different
register, in the comparative discussion of Jibril below.

2.4
ASCETICISM, COMPASSION, AND DENIAL OF THE WILL

The second and more complete escape is ethical: denial of the will, achieved through com-
passion (Mitleid) and ascetic renunciation. Schopenhauer’s ethics, laid out in On the Basis
of Morality, ground moral worth in the capacity to recognize that the suffering of another
being is not fundamentally different from one’s own — that the boundary between self and
other is, at a metaphysical level, illusory. This produces a strikingly universalist compassion:
Schopenhauer was one of the most forceful early modern philosophical critics of cruelty to
animals, and his ascetic ideal (modeled partly on Christian saints and partly on Hindu and
Buddhist renunciates, whose texts he read closely and admired) is one of withdrawal, chastity,
and voluntary poverty — not conquest, and certainly not violence against others. The suffering
saint, in Schopenhauer’s system, turns inward and away from the world’s strife rather than
outward toward a cause.

2.5
PSYCHOBIOGRAPHY AND TEMPERAMENT

Schopenhauer’s biography is often invoked, not unreasonably, as corroborating evidence
for his philosophy’s tone. He was notoriously irascible, litigious, and misanthropic; he was
estranged from his mother, the writer Johanna Schopenhauer, whose salon success he resented;
he was famously dismissive of the academic philosophy establishment of his day, particularly
Hegel, whose popularity he regarded as a symptom of the very intellectual conformity his
philosophy diagnosed. He never held a stable academic position of significance, lived largely
alone with a series of pet poodles (to which he was reportedly devoted, consistent with his
cross-species compassion ethics), and died largely without the recognition his work later
achieved. This alienation functioned, in his own self-understanding, as evidence for his thesis
— the price of clear sight — rather than as a tactical posture adopted to build a following. It is
worth noting he had no following to build in any meaningful sense during most of his life; his
fame was largely posthumous.

2.6
RECEPTION AND LEGACY

Schopenhauer’s influence extends across Nietzsche (who began as an admirer before reacting
against the “denial of the will” in favor of amor fati and self-overcoming), Wagner, Freud
(whose concept of unconscious drives owes an acknowledged debt to the Will), Wittgenstein,
and the broader existentialist tradition. This reception history matters for the comparison:

open intellectual exchange, in which his conclusions — including his notorious and widely

JIBRIL: GRIEVANCE, AUTHORITY, AND

THE ARCHITECTURE OF MEANING
THROUGH STRUGGLE

3.1
BIOGRAPHICAL AND INSTITUTIONAL BACKGROUND

Ahmad Musa Jibril is a U.S.-born cleric of Palestinian descent, raised partly in Medina, Saudi
Arabia, where his father studied at the Islamic University of Medina and where Jibril reportedly
completed memorization of the Qur’an as a child. He later pursued legal training in the
United States. In 2004 he was convicted on federal charges including fraud, a matter of
public record that predates his rise to prominence as an online preacher. Since the mid-2000s,
and particularly during the Syrian civil war (2011 onward), he built a substantial following
through YouTube lectures, Twitter, and Facebook, becoming — in the assessment of multiple
independent research bodies — one of the most influential English-language Salafi preachers
among Western audiences sympathetic to jihadist movements.

3.2
DOCUMENTED IDEOLOGICAL ROLE

Independent researchers, including the Program on Extremism at George Washington Univer-
sity and the International Centre for the Study of Radicalisation, have documented that Jibril’s
rhetoric has been disproportionately popular among Western individuals who subsequently
joined jihadist factions in Syria and Iraq, including groups such as al-Nusra and the Islamic
State, and that his name has surfaced in connection with individuals later involved in terrorist
attacks in the West, including the 2017 London Bridge attack. Researchers characterize his
communicative style as operating in a legally careful register — one that stops short of explicit,
prosecutable incitement to violence — while nonetheless functioning, in their assessment, as a
significant ideological on-ramp toward militancy, in part because his legal literacy (a product
of his own training) allows him to frame his message just inside constitutionally protected
speech. This is a matter of documented scholarly record, not a characterization advanced
independently by this paper; it is stated here because any honest analysis of Jibril’s “personal
beliefs,” bracketed from theology, still has to account for what those beliefs have been shown
to do in the world — a constraint that does not apply symmetrically to Schopenhauer.

3.3
STRUCTURAL, NON-DOCTRINAL THEMES

With that caveat established, several structural features of Jibril’s public persona are relevant
to this comparison, described here in non-theological terms:

• A totalizing grievance narrative. Jibril’s rhetoric consistently frames existing political and
social orders — particularly Western state power — as fundamentally and irreconcilably
hostile, casting ordinary civic participation (voting, military service, assimilation into
secular liberal norms) as a form of moral betrayal rather than a neutral act of citizenship.

• Suffering and imprisonment as proof of authenticity. Incarceration, persecution, and
hardship are recurring motifs in his rhetorical world, functioning less as conditions to be
alleviated than as evidence of righteousness and of belonging to a legitimate, embattled
struggle. His own history of travel restrictions and social-media scrutiny by U.S. authorities
has been folded into this narrative, reinforcing a self-conception of persecuted truth-telling.

• Contempt for comfortable conformity. Structurally, this mirrors Schopenhauer’s disdain
for the complacent majority — but where Schopenhauer’s target is metaphysical ignorance
of suffering’s universality, Jibril’s target is political and communal assimilation into a
system he regards as corrupt and illegitimate.

• Resolution through collective identification and struggle, not renunciation. Where
Schopenhauer’s answer to suffering is withdrawal of the will, Jibril’s rhetorical reso-
lution is activation: identification with an in-group and a cause, and — as documented
by researchers — the valorization of struggle, sacrifice, and martyrdom as the highest
expression of meaning and belonging.

• A persona of embattled individual conscience. Jibril presents himself, and is received
by his audience, as a lone truth-teller persecuted by a hostile establishment, a self-image
reinforced by the genuinely documented legal and surveillance scrutiny he has faced.

• Rhetorical intimacy and parasocial charisma. Researchers studying his digital strategy
have noted his use of an informal, emotionally direct register — addressing followers
as “brothers” and “sisters,” using colloquial English rather than formal homiletic style —
which produces a sense of personal relationship uncommon in more traditionally creden-
tialed religious authorities, and which is understood in the extremism-studies literature as
contributing significantly to his reach and influence, particularly among young, Western,
English-speaking audiences.

3.4
THE LEGAL-RHETORICAL EDGE

A notable structural feature, distinct from anything in Schopenhauer’s case, is the degree
to which Jibril’s public rhetoric appears calibrated to a legal boundary. Researchers have
described his output as hovering near, and at points arguably approaching, the threshold of

prosecutable incitement without crossing it outright — a strategic positioning made possible by
his own legal training. This is a feature with no meaningful analogue in Schopenhauer’s case:
Schopenhauer wrote as an academic philosopher without any comparable need to calibrate
his prose against a body of anti-incitement law, and nothing in his ethical system required
him to approach such a line in the first place, since his prescribed response to suffering was
compassion and renunciation rather than mobilization toward conflict.

CONVERGENCES, EXAMINED IN DEPTH

5.1
A SHARED PESSIMISTIC ANTHROPOLOGY

Both figures begin from the premise that the visible surface of social life — contentment, confor-
mity, participation in ordinary institutions — is a kind of false consciousness. For Schopenhauer
this falseness is metaphysical: people mistake the phenomenal world of individuated objects
and goals for ultimate reality, failing to grasp the single, suffering Will underlying all appear-
ance. For Jibril, as documented in his public rhetoric, the falseness is political and moral: people
mistake assimilation into secular, state-sanctioned life for legitimate participation, failing to
recognize what his rhetoric frames as the illegitimacy of that order. In both cases, the ordinary
contentment of the majority is reframed not as evidence of a good life but as evidence of a
failure to see clearly.

5.2
EMBATTLED INDIVIDUALITY AS A SOURCE OF AUTHORITY

Both figures derive rhetorical and personal authority substantially from their distance from
mainstream approval. Schopenhauer’s decades of professional obscurity and personal isolation
became, in his own retrospective self-narration and in later biographical treatment, proof of
the unfashionable truth of his ideas — he was not popular because he was right, on this
reading, not despite it. Jibril’s documented history of legal scrutiny, travel restriction, and
social-media monitoring by U.S. authorities functions similarly within his own rhetorical
self-presentation: persecution becomes evidence of the very truth being persecuted. This is a
widely studied pattern in the sociology of charismatic authority — sociologist Max Weber’s
account of charisma as authority grounded in perceived exceptional, often persecuted, insight
rather than in traditional office or rational-legal procedure applies structurally to both cases,
despite their otherwise total incompatibility of content.

5.3
SUFFERING AS EPISTEMICALLY PRIVILEGED

In both systems, suffering is not merely something that happens to people; it is treated as a kind
of privileged access to truth. Schopenhauer regarded the capacity to fully register suffering —
one’s own and others’ — as a precondition for ethical and metaphysical insight; those who
deny or minimize suffering through distraction are, on his account, epistemically as well as

morally deficient. In Jibril’s rhetorical world, per the documented analysis of researchers,
hardship and persecution function analogously as a kind of proof-of-work: those who have
suffered for the cause have privileged standing to speak about it, and those who have not are
implicitly suspect in their commitment.

DIVERGENCES, EXAMINED IN DEPTH —

AND WHY THEY ARE THE POINT

The convergences above are real, but the entire analytical payoff of this comparison lies in
what happens after the shared diagnosis — in what each figure’s system asks a follower to do
about the suffering it identifies.

6.1
UNIVERSAL COMPASSION VERSUS PARTICULARIST LOYALTY

Schopenhauer’s ethics, however severe in tone, terminate in a compassion he explicitly extends
across the boundary of species, let alone the boundaries of nation, religion, or political faction.
There is no in-group in Schopenhauer’s ethical system whose suffering counts more than
another’s; the metaphysical claim that individuation is illusory works, for him, precisely
to dissolve such boundaries. Jibril’s documented rhetoric works in the opposite direction:
it sharpens communal boundaries, framing suffering as something that binds an in-group
together against an external, illegitimate order. Where Schopenhauer’s pessimism dissolves
the self/other distinction, Jibril’s rhetoric, per researchers’ analysis, hardens an us/them
distinction.

6.2
QUIETISM VERSUS MOBILIZATION

Schopenhauer’s prescribed response to the suffering he diagnoses is withdrawal — the quieting
of desire, aesthetic contemplation, ascetic renunciation. Nothing in his system calls a reader to
organize, recruit, or act collectively against an external enemy; if anything, his system counsels
disengagement from worldly striving of every kind, including political striving. Jibril’s rhetoric,
as documented, moves in precisely the opposite direction: it identifies an external enemy
(a hostile political and civilizational order) and calls for engagement, identification, and
— per researchers who have studied his influence on foreign fighters — has functioned as
encouragement toward armed struggle, even where explicit incitement is avoided. This is not
a minor stylistic difference; it is the entire ethical hinge on which any comparison must turn.

6.3
NON-VIOLENCE VERSUS THE VALORIZATION OF MARTYRDOM

Schopenhauer has no doctrine that valorizes dying for a cause; his own relationship to death is
philosophical rather than heroic, framed as release from the Will’s ceaseless striving rather than
as a supreme act of meaning to be sought out. Jibril’s documented rhetoric, by contrast, includes
the elevation of martyrdom as a pedagogical and aspirational ideal — a recurring theme
researchers have identified across his lectures. This divergence is arguably the single most
important one in the entire comparison, since it marks the point at which a shared pessimistic
anthropology produces, in one case, a philosophy of withdrawal from worldly conflict, and in
the other, per independent researchers, a documented on-ramp toward participation in it.

6.4
CREDENTIALED, CONTESTED DISCOURSE VERSUS LEGALLY CALI-

BRATED PERSUASION

Schopenhauer’s claims have been subject to two centuries of open academic contestation,
translation, critique, and revision; his misogyny, for instance, is now near-universally treated
by scholars as a serious flaw rather than a doctrine to be defended. Jibril’s discourse, by
contrast, operates largely outside equivalent institutional contestation — it circulates through
informal online channels, is calibrated (per researchers) to the edge of legal permissibility
rather than to persuasive engagement with counter-argument, and has been studied primarily
by security and extremism researchers rather than engaged as philosophy. This is not a value
judgment so much as a structural observation: the two men’s ideas have moved through
radically different institutional environments, with radically different correctives available.

THEORETICAL EXCURSUS: WHY THE
SAME DIAGNOSIS PRODUCES OPPOSITE
PRESCRIPTIONS

The divergence documented above is not idiosyncratic to these two figures; it reflects a more
general pattern studied in both the sociology of religion and the psychology of radicalization.
Arie Kruglanski’s “significance quest” theory of radicalization argues that a felt loss of personal
significance — often triggered by humiliation, marginalization, or grievance — creates a
psychological vacuum that can be filled by an ideology offering a path to significance through
either withdrawal (asceticism, quietism) or heroic sacrifice (martyrdom, violence), depending
on which narrative is culturally and socially available to the aggrieved individual.

Schopenhauer, writing from a position of personal disappointment and professional marginal-
ization but embedded in a stable, non-violent academic and literary culture, had available to
him a narrative of significance-through-renunciation. Jibril’s audience — often young, West-
ern, and experiencing a comparable sense of grievance or marginalization, but embedded in
a post-9/11 landscape of foreign conflict, transnational jihadist recruitment networks, and
social-media accessible combat narratives — had available a narrative of significance-through-
martyrdom.
On this reading, the difference between the two figures’ prescriptions is not reducible to
individual moral character alone; it also reflects which narrative resources were culturally and
historically available to convert a shared pessimistic diagnosis into a livable answer. This does
not excuse or relativize the documented harms associated with Jibril’s rhetoric; it explains, at
a structural level, why a psychologically similar starting point can terminate in an ethically
incompatible endpoint.

METHODOLOGICAL CAUTION AND THE
LIMITS OF THE COMPARISON

Comparative character analysis of this kind carries a specific risk: the aesthetic pleasure of a
clever structural parallel can smuggle in a false equivalence between a historically vindicated
philosophical pessimist and a living figure whose rhetoric has been linked, by researchers
who have studied it closely, to real-world radicalization and violence. This paper’s position is
that the structural parallel — pessimistic anthropology, contempt for complacency, embattled
individuality, suffering as epistemically privileged — is real and worth naming precisely
because naming it clarifies where the two figures diverge, not because it elevates one to the
intellectual or moral stature of the other.
Schopenhauer’s legacy is contested within academic philosophy on its own terms: his
misogyny, his temperament, and the coherence of his metaphysics are all subjects of ongoing
scholarly debate, conducted through peer review, translation, and two centuries of intellectual
accountability. Jibril’s public role is contested on terms that involve documented, present-
tense harm, assessed by researchers whose explicit professional purpose is understanding
and preventing radicalization. These are different categories of controversy, and collapsing
them into a single frame of “two misunderstood outsiders” or “two clear-sighted iconoclasts”
would misrepresent both — flattering a documented radicalization figure by association with
a canonical philosopher, and trivializing the philosopher by association with a live security
concern. The value of the comparison lies entirely in holding the structural parallel and the
ethical divergence in view simultaneously, rather than resolving the tension in either direction.

CONCLUSION

Placed side by side, Schopenhauer and Jibril illustrate how a shared psychological architecture
— suffering as the central fact of existence, contempt for comfortable conformity, and a self-
image of clear-sighted, embattled isolation — can generate entirely opposite ethical trajectories
depending on the philosophical, communal, and historical commitments that govern the
response to that diagnosis. Schopenhauer’s road runs toward universal compassion and
withdrawal from worldly striving; Jibril’s, as documented by those who study his rhetoric
professionally, runs toward grievance, in-group mobilization, and, per independent researchers,
has functioned as an ideological on-ramp toward armed conflict.

The comparison earns its keep only insofar as it resists the temptation to treat structural re-
semblance as endorsement of either figure, and instead uses the parallel to sharpen a genuinely
useful analytical question: what, exactly, turns a pessimistic diagnosis of the human condition
into quietism in one case, and into documented real-world mobilization toward violence in the
other? The answer offered here — that the presence or absence of a universalist compassion
ethic, and the availability of culturally supplied narratives of significance, does much of the
work — is offered as a hypothesis for further comparative study, not a settled conclusion.
