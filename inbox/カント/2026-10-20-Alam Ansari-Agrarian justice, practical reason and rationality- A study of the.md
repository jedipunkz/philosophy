‘Agrarian Justice’ was first coined in the year of 1793 in a pamphlet under the same title!. It
was the earliest proposal of redistribution of a created national fund among the agriculturists, and all
these in the name of agrarian justice. The pamphlet put forth a critique of the so-called ‘Agrarian
Law’ — a law that was supposedly protecting the concentration of land in a few hands. It attempted
to advance a reasoned out expression to a chief demand - justice in the countryside — under the

banner of the social movement of ‘agrarianism’ in America’.

Thomas Paine (1793) proposed a plan to deal with the problem of poverty by providing for the
taxation of accumulated property. It was opined that the tax revenue collected must permit the
machineries of the state to give each man and woman, who was reaching the age of twenty-one, the
sum of fifteen pounds, and every person of fifty years of age or over, ten pounds per year. In other
words, the state was made responsible to bring the justice to the poor through fiscal measures. In the
name of agrarian justice, the demand was to lay down a system of ‘social insurance’, and it called

for the system of ground rents and graduated ‘inheritance taxes’.

American agrarianism acknowledged and advocated the constitutional provisions to ensure a
pension and insurance fund for the peasantry. Such advocacy was framed as a matter of rights of
men. It was not a matter of concern as to how affluent some persons were, as long as none was
miserable in consequence of affluence of some (Paine, 1797, pp. 9-15). Henry George (1885) took
up the mantle, expressing similar views and ideas, again in America during nineteenth century.

Retrospectively speaking, both Paineian and Georgian proposals skirted away from the public

European agrarianism that flourished in Russia in the mid-nineteenth century was a harbinger of

On the other hand, it has often been claimed that the agrarian changes and land reforms has been
geared towards bringing an equal distribution of assets to the members of rural society, and thereby,
income distribution in rural society (Dore, 1959; Warriner, 1957; Ladejinsky, 1964). In the course

All around the world those who have championed the cause of modern agricultural social and
applied ethics have been hoisting the ethical issues regarding management of earth’s resources for
the production of food and prescribe ways of resolution of such issues that are prudent, fair and
humane. Contemporaneously, agrarian justice agenda is therefore related with not only an entire set
of problems of the distribution of land ownership and water uses but also the distribution of food
security in the countryside — broadly speaking, livelihood. There is more over the need to find a
resolution of the senses of pervasive injustices regarding distribution of individual human
productive capacity in the third world countries in general and South Asia in particular. Such

injustices warrant resolution.

There have been attempts to inform and impact upon the public policy-making by articulating the
ethical dimensions related with the negative implications of intensive use of resources for
production on sustainability of global environment, bearing of modern agricultural biotechnologies
on the human and animal welfare, impact of multinational agribusiness on rural income and
employment, and mismatch between global food supplies and human nutritional needs. All these
have been instrumental in the resurgence of agrarianism in the deontological tradition of rights,

duties and obligations.

Within the domain of agricultural and social applied ethics, the socio-economic strand of
agricultural ethics has been attempting to deal rather directly with the issue of distributive justice -

the problem of mismatch between food supplies and human nutritional needs.

South Asia

Our specific concern in this research work is with the populace and space of the
sub-continental region of South Asia. It is considered a geographical region and a political
regional entity within Asia. Bounded by the borders of the countries of India, Pakistan,

Bangladesh, Nepal, Sri Lanka, Bhutan and Maldives, the region gives shelter to almost 22

26|Page

percent of the aggregate human population of the globe. Being spread over 412.09 million
hectares of the surface area (approximately 2. 4 percent of the land surface area of the
world), it is today the home of almost 1.3 billion human populace. The man-land ratio is
3.16 persons per hectare in the region. It is certainly a highly populous region, characterised

by the phenomenon of scarcity of land for majority of populace.

South Asia has been a countrified region for a long time. While there are wide discrepancies in the
rate of urbanization across countries of the region, it is not yet heavily urbanized space of the world.
In 2000, it was only just about 28.2 percent of total population of the region that resided in the

urban space (it was not even one-third of the aggregate populace of the region).

While continuing to be a countrified space, South Asia is also a region that is home to the largest
number of economically poor populace. Over the years, the persistence of poverty and the growth in
inequality has derived from the inherited unjust attire of the social order, which has effectively
excluded the resource poor populace from equitable opportunities for earning a decent livelihood

and participating in the development process.

It has long been believed in the parlance of policy making in all countries of the region that the fast

South Asia continues to be a multi-ethnic society harbouring huge populace divided along
lines of race, caste and religion. Social transformations have taken place but still social
insecurities and lack of social protection and insurance looms large in all countries of south
Asia. The phenomenon of social exclusion is still persistent. The discrimination along the
caste and community lines has not been properly eliminated. The literacy is low and health-

Objectives of Study

Our study puts forth an exposition of the idea and agenda of agrarian (transitional)
(distributive) justice and the description of an alternative governance framework of the
policies of justice and social protection, security and insurance under the aegis of a welfare

state regime in the context of South Asian countryside society and economy.

There are three principal purposes of the thesis. The major purpose is to explore the
rationale of a concern for a floor level of surplus generating capacity and a concern for
equity and fairness with regard to the distribution of such a capacity as opportunity freedom
and benefit among producing households in the countryside. The classical political
economy pioneered the ‘surplus’ paradigm of analysis. It is required that it is brought back,

human rationality and morality of practical reasons of human agency.

To provide a critique of the existing public concerns for equity, which are variously sought

stricken Monsoon-rain-based, tropical-vegetation-oriented south Asian countryside so as to

Methods of Study

The methodology of our thesis is compound and hybrid in nature. The thesis is an
exercise in neither an explanation of the prevalent phenomena of justice and injustice nor a
prediction of the future state of justice and injustice. It is about description and prescription

of a norm of ought-abidance of justice and ought-avoidance of injustice.

Our composite and hybrid methodology is one that describes the idea of justice and the
attendant ideas related to justice on the one hand, and analyses the circumstances of
agrarian justice and injustice in south Asia on the other. It is befitting to the normative
study and prescriptive framework of justice in the agrarian setting. The mixed methods of
description, analysis and prescription are therefore adopted, and the tools and techniques of
analysis and description are used to facilitate the laying of three foundations: conceptual

design, analytical-empirical framework and policy prescription structure.

Our method of study of agrarian justice is descriptive and analytical; it is not at all
explanatory and predictive in any sense. The method is not at all oriented towards
explaining any observed phenomenon and instance of justice and predicting anything about,
and related with, the magnitude and trend or otherwise of injustices and justice. It is not our

concern to make any positive scientific analysis of the object under study.

Our central focus is on human agency, normative prescription of goods in conjunction with

linking these to the idea of justice and fairness in this project are explicable in the empirical

It is within our scope of study to revisit agrarianism, classical political economy and

accommodated but only with modifications and improvements in the scope of their
application. These are integrated into the theme of agrarian social egalitarian surplus

So far as our concern with justice in the project is confined to justice to rural citizens in the
society, the present work is limited in scope, and therefore, it does not address the issue of
universalization in conceptualizing justice for all. It is confined to agrarian distributive
transitional justice for rural citizens of south Asia only. The scope of study is thus limited

area.

Our work is again limited to exploration about merely distributive justice. In other words,
our project does not address the restorative and retributive justice (though conceptually, a

commutative, restorative and distributive justice). Our concern is moreover confined to
addressing the issues of institutional and macro-justice. It does not much address the micro-

In agriculture, a peasant household is both a consumer household as well as a producer

stock from the previous cycle of production. This initial stock is created out of the surplus

over and above the necessary labour cost of production. It is the difference of net value

added and labour cost of production.

Moral-theoretic Framework

Agrarian justice as an idea and a category of theoretical conception as well as an
agenda of practical political discourse and policy ought to be grounded in the moral-
theoretic framework. It is neither political philosophy nor political economy (including the
modern discipline of mainstream economics) but moral philosophy which is enriched

enough to deal with this area of investigation.

Morality is a matter of what to do. A moral question is actually a ‘planning question’
involving practical decisions about when and to what extent to feel such characteristically
moral sentiments as compassion, guilt or resentment. Morality involves a decision, and
many facts may inform the decision; but, these facts are not moral facts. These facts are
entities that just count as considerations in the decision about what one ought to do. There
are no moral facts except perhaps in some deflated, quasi-realist sense. Morality is not an
issue of factual enquiry about the social and natural world. It is not regarding addressing the

empirical questions about the social and natural world.

Common-sense morality largely consists in such obligations as most of us believe that there
are certain people to whom we have special obligations. We believe we ought to help these
people in certain ways. We should try to protect them from certain kinds of harm, and
should try to give them certain kinds of benefit. These are the people to whom we stand in
certain relations; the examples are our children, parents, pupils, patients, members of our

trade unions, or those whom we represent.

Minimization of Moral Bads

Agrarian justice theory and practice in hybrid morality structure of goal-right
system ought to focus on minimization of the moral bads (for example, affront, harm,
injury, human incapacity) as well as minimization of violation of the rights to human
capacity. The minimization of moral bads ought to be more urgent than the maximization
of the goods (which is so emphasized in the monist moral discourse and practice of

utilitarianism-consequentialism).

Agrarian justice agenda, political discourse and policy ought to involve three-part
minimizations: minimization of individual un-freedom, coercion, duress, oppression, threat,
vulnerability, harms, damages, outrages and injuries, minimization of social inequality,
unevenness, disparity, asymmetry, imbalances, exclusion, deprivation, exploitation, misery

and resource-less-ness, and minimization of public unfairness, partiality, nepotism, undue

favour, rent-seeking, irresponsibility, un-accountability, violation of rule of law and

A profound question raised by Donald Davidson (2001) in the discipline of moral

promotion of the “goods”, attainment, embracing and entertaining the “rights” and both

always evaluated subsequent to completion of action in terms of the consequences and
outcome. The human agency and moral personae and functionary pursue the goals of either
the promotion of the goods or the protection of the rights or both in the interactive setting
of society and economy. These two methods of appraising the worth of an action has been
the standard formulation in ethics, moral and political philosophy. It is also accepted in the

discipline of economics.

Means-rationality’ involves the formal properties of decision-making. ‘Ends-rationality’

All actions based on genuinely held beliefs — subjective and objective — are rational in
economics. ‘Substantive rationality’ refers to ‘attributes of the equilibrium configuration of
the system’. It is to be contrasted with “procedural rationality’ which refers to ‘attributes of

part of the real world and thus it is a physical phenomenon.

first priority is to institute a system of guarantee of a floor level (a threshold level) of the
agential capability or ability for the worst-off rural actors (prioritarianism) to do something
to produce a surplus or to be a surplus producer in the countryside; second, this level of the
agential capability (capacity) to do or to be is guaranteed as a matter of basic and

fundamental right and entitlement for all producing human actors rather equally.

Public Policy of Government

Agrarian egalitarian capacity-distribution transitional justice as agential actions
towards minimization of affront of human productive incapacity and equalization of human
productive capacity in matter of generation of material surplus in the countryside ought to
be a principal feature of obligatory actions of public organizations and policy of
government. Two significant questions are: to what extent may a government succeed in
instituting justice in a social setup pervaded by private individual irrationalities and
practical unreason? What role has the government to play in instituting equity/equality-

centred agrarian surplus-capacity-justice through social policies of a benign welfare state?

The notion of constitutional welfare states in south Asia that pursues ‘growth with justice’
is our crucial point of departure. A government in a democracy ought to reflect the ethical
demands of the masses which in turn is conducive to the evolution of policies leading to a
just and humane social union and order. The mediation of an inclusive developmental
welfare state regime is of significance. In the cases of moral deficits and failures of
otherwise rational and autonomous private persons in interaction framework and break-
down of cooperative contractual ventures in civil society, the second-best option is attempt
at attainment of agrarian distributive transitional justice under the aegis of proactive,

inclusive and universal social policy of an altruistic public bureaucratic agency.

Throughout twentieth century, there had been a clamour for the establishment of proactive,
inclusive and universal social public policy framework of welfare states. In the concrete
setting of malnutrition-afflicted and poverty-stricken Monsoon-rain-based, tropical-
vegetation-oriented south Asian countryside, agrarian bureaucracy ought to be at the

driving seat of instituting agrarian distributive transitional justice in south Asia.

Good Governance

Agrarian egalitarian capacity-distribution transitional justice as political agenda,
government policy and public action programme ought to be implemented in the spirit of
good governance. Good governance is a new paradigm of private-public participation in
monitoring and assessing the outcome of policy initiatives of the governments in south
Asia. In matter of agrarian justice in south Asia, there are two important questions: how is
to ensure the effectiveness of the governments in realising the goal-right system of
distributive justice in the countryside? How is to execute, monitor and assess the
consequences in terms of redistribution of human surplus productive capacity and delivery

of the ‘good’ and the ‘right’ to such human capacity?

A structure and processes of good and effective governance is a necessary and sufficient
condition for generation of fruitful outcomes of agrarian distributive transitional justice as

rational, reasonable and moral public action. The welfare governments in south Asia which

pursue the public goal of making individual and collective human life go well ought to
ensure two things: a minimum benchmark of a surplus-producing capacity for each person,
and an equal distribution of such a capacity among all persons in a closed agrarian society,
and this is possible only in the case of effective government with good governance. Since
the human capacity is not a visible entity, the government has to choose an indirect index,
set a benchmark and attempt to equalize this index. This index may be a composite one.

Agential (Surplus Productive) Capacity

A human capacity is a set of attributes, features and characteristics of an individual
being that is conducive, instrumental and helpful in doing or becoming something. It is a
set of faculty to do something (ability to act on something) and also a set of competence to
be something (become something). A productive capacity of a human individual is in the
nature of bare ownership, and bare ownership does not confer full usufruct (usufruct
denotes the right to use, right to benefit or to receive the rent). In matter of human
capacities to do or to become, the only remaining possible type of external rights is the

“rent-rights”’.

Productive capacity of human actors is not just geared to production of consumables but
essentially to reproduction of investable surplus. Paul Baran (1969) differentiates three
variants of economic surplus from the production perspective in terms of comparative

statics: actual surplus, potential surplus, and planned surplus.

The capacity of a human rational and reasonable agent to deliberate on considerations, to
reflect on the dispositions and to act in accordance with dispositions on the deliberated
considerations is a rational capacity that is required to agree at the consideration of capacity
to produce a surplus being a vital capacity of an individual in matter of biological and

social survival and living a good life.

There is a prescription of a threshold level of the capacity of an agrarian productive unit
(peasantry) to produce/reproduce a surplus for a rural household and farm; it is to be
guaranteed at least at its minimum across the landscape of south Asia. This threshold level
of human capacity is the economic capacity of human agency that is in need to be

guaranteed for all on an equal basis as a matter of the moral-need-right.

A goal-right system of principles and actions motivated by and culminating in capacity of
basic-needs-meeting as part of the capability set that is conducive to capacity-creation may
be justified on grounds of failures of principal institutions in the society. In the goal-right
perspective, surplus producing capacity of basic-needs-meeting of a rational and reasonable
peasant actor in the countryside is to be accorded the moral status of an overriding goal of
human person and agency, a fundamental right of individual citizen in a constitutional
democratic welfare society, and most importantly, a basic need of the vulnerable human

beings in adversity.

Under the framework of classical liberalism that advocates and confers the process—
freedom to be assigned to individuals, the notion of individual liberty implies the right to
the allocation of use-rights and benefit-rights in human capacities. There is a third
component of human capacity - the rent-rights of capacity, and this is not conferred under
the process-freedom. The rent depends on the quality of the capacity (such as its
productivity for a productive capacity) of an individual, and the quality of human capacity

reflected in the labour productivity is a function of genetic luck, health-care, education and

training, and disposition to work. A fraction of the rent-right ought to be distributed on the

principles of equality for justice in society.

Agential (Surplus Productive Capacity) Egalitarian Distribution

Egalitarianism is the ideology of valuing equality. Equality is fair and inequality is
unfair. Equality is constitutive of fairness and justice, and therefore contrarily, inequality is
constitutive of injustice and wrong-doing. Over the last three decades, the normative
principles of distributive justice designed to allocate the ‘goods’ in limited supply relative
to demand, for example, the income, wealth and opportunity, are a matter of intense debate

in the Anglo-American world.

In matter of fairness motivation of people and choice of principles in preference ordering,
Morton Deutsch (1985) lists three principles of equality, equity and need. David Miller
(1976) also lists principles of desert, need and rights. John Scott, et al. (2001) have as well
noted three principles of equality, equity/merit, efficiency and need, though Walzer (1983)
lists the principles of need, desert and free exchange. John Elster (1992) lists efficiency,
basic needs and desert in lexicographic order. In other words, there are at least three
distribution principles that constitute justice in distribution in the literature: equity, equality,

and needs-fulfilment.

One of the basic tenets of medieval Semitic religions of Christianity and Islam has been
some form of equality among faith-abiding individuals. The Marxists have championed the
cause of egalitarian concerns since nineteenth century. As late as in 1971, equality
continued to play a central role in the theory and principles of justice, proposed by John
Rawls (1971). Egalitarianism does play a central role in the ideas of justice of Amartya Sen
(1999) and Ronald Dworkin (1978) as well. Whatever the ‘rights’ are and whatever way the
‘rights’ ought to get priority over or trump upon or include the ‘goods’, there must be
equality/equity in the allocation, division and distribution of claims, rights and entitlements

of individual persons.

67|Page

In the modern formulations, justice as equality of original positions has implied equality of
opportunities for individuals, and it has of late implied justice as equality of basic,
fundamental and natural rights. Equality in access to opportunities is a chance to do or to
obtain something with a course of action within one’s power and control, and also be able
to take that course of action and ensure the fructification of doing or obtaining that
something — equality in human capability. Demand for equality of original positions,
construed as equality of proportions, had been the most primitive and also the oldest notion,
dating from the writings of Aristotle and Greek philosophy. Equality in original position
through the provision of opportunities is contrasted with equality as end-state or outcomes.

The notion of equality of outcomes got associated with the idea of justice much later.

Theoretical and empirical analysis of justice addresses the specific concern regarding
determination of what is that something which people need or deserve. Agrarian egalitarian
distribution justice requires resolution of the questions: equality of what or equality with
regard to what thing? What ought to be the equalisandum, considered both as the good and
the right in the goal-right system? Agrarian egalitarian transitional distributive justice ought
to focus on and be concerned with the idea and conceptual discourse on the capacity of

human agency - the egalitarian distribution of human productive and reproductive capacity.

Agrarian justice as hybrid moral action of minimization of affronts and harms ought to be
characterised by a distinct and specific concern for instituting an egalitarian distributive
system in rural society. Agrarian distributive justice in the goal-right system of hybrid
morality framework requires an equitable distribution — equity or equality or needs-
fulfilment. In contrast to commodity (including resources) and income (reflecting the level
of welfare in terms of access to utility), it is the human capacity that ought to be equalized

for the sake of egalitarian distribution.

The recognition of need for achieving equality in the distribution of material surplus
producing capability and concomitant relative opportunity aspect of positive freedom to
enjoy the fruits of it by all peasantries and rural labourers in an equal manner is what ought
to be explored as the singular pedestal of a deliberated scheme of enhancing agrarian

distributive transitional justice. It is only recently that Amartya Sen (1999), Martha

68|Page

Nussbaum (1992;2006) and their followers have favoured a conception of justice that

insists on equality in human capability.

The argument is worth defending that agrarian egalitarian distributive justice ought to be
grounded in the contemporaneous empirical facts and historically-inherited conditions of
cooperation and endemic conflicts in the allocation, exchange and distribution of not only
material resources and commodities but also human capacities (substantive capacities to
produce a reproductive surplus) among actors in the countryside. The classical political
discourse on the restoration of the rights of citizens through the rule of law, subsequent to
harm/damage/Vviolation, is in need to be jointed with the modern socio-economic discourse
on equality/equity through distribution and redistribution of rights to resources, commodity

and income.

The methodology of the overall thesis is a compound one, as it is grounded on a
triangular foundation of three frameworks: theoretical account of the conception of agrarian
justice, empirical analysis of social and economic conditions in south Asia, and prescription
of an alternative agrarian social policy and design of governance. There are issues of
hitherto prevailing public welfare and redistribution policy. The prevalent social policy has
been ad-hoc in orientation and residual in nature. It is the deficits and failures of the
existing public policy of welfare and redistribution and growth-with-justice in south Asia
that is the issue. There has been a pervasive governance failure in a matter of execution and
implementation. In the alternative framework that is developed towards the end chapter of
the thesis on the basis of methodology followed, it is argued that the governance of agrarian

justice and social policy is required to be improved.

The chapter is divided into three principal sections and a brief concluding section at the
last.

In the first section, the concept of morality is visited. The role of interpersonal morality in human
action is also dealt with. Moral and morality has been a debatable and a matter of conflict of
opinions in moral philosophy.

In the second section, the morality of the good and the moral philosophy of utilitarianism are
discussed. The evaluative criterion of allocative efficiency of neoclassical economic approach
variant is described. It is the criterion of evaluation of human actions that has been focused at the
maximization of the ‘goods’ in society under the influence of utilitarianism. Such a criterion is
narrow, and therefore, discarded in our project.

Third section deals with the morality of the rights and deontology. The morality of the goods and
the morality of the rights are contrasted in historical perspective. The perspective of human rights
and human needs are positioned to highlight another popular criterion. The ‘right to equity/equality
in productive capacity’ is argued to be superior, realistic and worthwhile, and arguments are
borrowed from the literature on deontology and the classical political economy.

We conclude with the discussion on the concepts of hybrid morality. A discussion on the features
and arguments of rationalist morality thesis is also put forward. While a part of the first hypothesis
is tested, a part of the first objective of the thesis is also addressed here in this chapter.

In a doctrine of agrarianism, it is a matter of substantive meaning as well as of
analytical significance to accommodate a conception of hybrid and mixed morality — a
morality of the goal-right system. A conception of hybrid morality is one that
simultaneously creates an ought-abidance imperative for pursuit of the goods of something
as well as seeking of the rights to something. This is required by the circumstances of
justice in concrete societies which are not fully democratic and liberal. What is happening
in the world and how the human lives are going in the world requires consequence-
sensitivity in preference, choice and actions of individuals. The centrality of human lives in

reasons assessment of the world in which we live is to be appreciated.

One of the influential advocates of a similar position has been Amartya Sen (1995), who
has rejected the notion of preference-independent, consequence-detached perspective of
rights of men/women, and rather favored a system of consequentialist-procedural approach.
While ‘no direct and basic importance’ is attached to ‘rights and liberties’ in the evaluation
of states of affairs in the utilitarian framework of analysis, the consequential analysis can

still be employed in inverse form to determine which rights to protect.

To go back to the history of literature on the moral discourse, the most prominent paradigm
of a hybrid moral theory, that is, a goal-based ethical theory of rights, is the utilitarianism-
consequentialism. The social welfare theory employs a hybrid moral theory — emphasizing
the goodness of utility and the property and exchange rights. The ‘neoclassical’ social
welfare theory under the framework of utilitarianism-consequentialism emphasizing the
goods is a hybrid moral theory. It is a hybrid of utilitarianism, libertarianism and liberalism
that emphasize, in addition to subjective aims and goals of individuals, the concerns for
welfare rights, property rights and rights to liberty, and constitutional public choice (Roth,
2002, pp.14-7).

In ethics, there are two mainstream and dominant theories of the “good” and of the “right”.
The moral theories of consequentialism and deontology have framed and formulated the
specific standards to appraise the action of a human agency. There is a tradition in
philosophy in which the ‘the goods’ are specified independent of ‘the rights’ and also a

tradition which asserts the priority of ‘the rights’ (an agent-relative entity) over ‘the goods’

79|Page

(an agent-neutral entity). There is however a possibility of synthesizing the two systems.
Hybridization in conceptualization exercises to accommodate the plurality of views on
morality and justice is further reinforced by the facts of fluidity characterising the

categories of deliberation, intentionality, disposition and action in moral philosophy.

Utilitarianism has been a hybrid moral theory but it has principally been a goal-based
theory in which there has been no duty to respect rights and no duty to promote just
institutions. It is not a genuine goal-right system of evaluation of human behavior, conduct
and action. Pareto-optimal or efficient outcomes cannot accommodate the lexical priority of
rights. The fluid conception of public good and institutional fluidity, that is, instituionless
and value-free normative enterprise of utilitarianism has been conducive to \suggesting no

institutional imperative to promote justice as a procedural notion.

So far as utilitarianism has been a rights-based moral theory, it has been able to assign
merely an instrumental role to unattenuated property and exchange rights of individuals. It
is a theory grounded in consequentialist, procedurally-detached and intendedly value-free
theorisation about welfare, accommodates the moral force of the goods (though the ‘ends’,
‘final aims’ or goods are subjective in nature) as well as the rights, and fails to
accommodate any plausible understanding of justice as mutual advantage and justice as
impartiality because it fails to reconcile with the moral force of rights and their correlative

duties (Roth, 2002).

“comprehensive outcome” (rather than merely “culmination outcome’) to be considered as
representing the process as well as the end-state together. In the alternative hybrid goal-

Morality of the Goods

In ancient Greek philosophy, goodness has been the property of the objects of the
world. The “idea of the good” (i.e., the form of the good) is the ultimate object of

Morality of the Rights

In the alternative tradition of morality of the rights, ‘rights’ are the claims,
privileges, immunities and power of individual agency. In political theory, analytical
jurisprudence theory and normative theory, rights are not only considered the immunity and
power but also the liberty. It is often affirmed that the rights control our sense of freedom
and autonomy. Whenever we talk of right, we need to talk about freedom and coercion as

well. It is also affirmed that the rights protect our interests and desires.

In the history of rights as these have originated in Rome and European countries, most

popular rights have been the property rights and welfare rights. Property right includes the

93|Page

right to exclusive use of one’s property and the right that others not trespass on it; it also
includes the liberty to use the property as one chooses. Welfare rights might be described as
rights which create an unconditional duty on part of others to contribute. Social contract
and natural rights theories were propounded in eighteenth century. In 1789, the Declaration
of the Rights of Man and of Citizens was declared in France. In 1791, Thomas Paine wrote
the Rights of Man. Greater attention was drawn towards human rights after Second World

War during Nuremberg trial in 1945.

The Greeks had the laws of custom and convention. The language of rights and the
intuitions that provided the structure of such rights however developed in a demanding
culture of ancient Rome. The right of lex talions, a term coined by Cicero in Rome, was the
right of retaliation or revenge. It evolved into a series of laws, rules and codes covering
punishment, exchange for damage to the injured party and property rights. The natural
rights theories developed as early as the high Middle Ages in Europe. In the twelfth
century, cannon lawgivers began to ask whether a pauper could licitly take something from
the superfluities of a rich man, in case he/she would perish without it. The accepted dictum
was: a pauper in case of extreme necessity could legitimately take whatever he/she needed
to survive. Necessity has no law. It became a standard doctrine in theology as well as civil

law. Such doctrine continued unchallenged till the second half of the seventeenth century.

There has also been a tradition of championing the cause of ‘divine rights’. The divine
rights are conferred upon individuals by God. They are self-evident. Nowadays, it is
popular anymore. The natural right is a non-descriptive normative concept. It is constant
and immutable right attached to the human nature, and therefore, the natural right is neither
conventional nor divine in nature. A person possesses such rights in virtue of his nature or
humanity. The natural right is the right to acquire or retain something rather than of the
right to do or to refrain from doing something. Some natural rights are universal and some
are non-universal in nature. The natural rights theory slowly lapsed and lost the authority

later.

moral or natural rights. They are inalienable. They are essential part of who we are as

human beings and require no proof since there are known by intuition. All human beings
have certain natural rights and certain moral rights. Such rights are not created by the legal
and social systems. The idea of the ‘rights’ is an agent-relative concept. There are civil,
legal and political rights; there are social rights; there are contractual and promissory rights;
and all these are conventional rights. These are implied rights which are of course
derivative and secondary rights (Hoekema, 1986). Legal, constitutional and contractual

rights are secondary and implied rights.

The conventional rights are created, conferred, granted and conceded to individuals by
governments, states, legal systems and societies. The “rights” are resources deriving from
norms, at the disposal of persons to deploy at their discretion in the pursuit of their own
projects, subject to overriding reasons which may arise in particular cases. A right is used
in dual senses of being correct and being the claim, privilege, immunity and power. A
“right” is a thing that is antonym and contradiction of “wrong”. It is anything that is

opposite to the harm, mistake, and incorrect. Such rights are considered correct.

A right is always held by a person with respect to other person. All persons have certain
rights simply as persons. A “right” is a claim of one on another, and therefore, it is the
correlative of obligation, responsibility and duty. With rights come responsibilities and
liabilities. The rights of someone entail a duty of non-interference with the rights of others
and also a duty of contributing to the securing of rights of someone. Wherever there are
rights, there are both positive and negative duties, responsibilities and obligations (Hohfeld,

1917; 1974).

There are correlative duties. “To do good’ is a positive duty. Not to do harm is a negative
duty. Charity of someone makes one just a beneficiary of the goods. Kindness and charity
is however different sort of duty; it is not moral duty. Benevolence and charity are “non-
directed” duty. Right-holding is more than being merely a beneficiary of a directed duty.
Not all duties are directed. A talk of duties owed to the “public” is merely shorthand of

talking about duties owed to each and every citizen.

There are ‘rights in something’ and there are ‘rights to something’. Rights in something are mostly

the possession and acquisition, that is, entitlement to use and enjoy fruits. The 1974 Robert

95|Page

Nozick’s holdings entitlement theory of justice and rights is an example. Rights to something are
promises and contracts, that is, due shares and obligations in leisure income as compensation that
gives rise to the notions of compensatory justice and distributive justice. Nonetheless, the
stipulative meaning of the rights is different from the descriptive usage meanings of the rights. The
ways we use this word called “right” is ambiguous, incoherent, and loose and carry vague
meanings. There is in actuality no common identical core of meaning attached to the terminology of
“right”, and it is difficult to define the term in its most generic sense. A conceptual hygiene exercise
is often needed. The stipulative definition of rights should be derived from the linguistic intuitions

about rights.

There are classic works on “rights” by Wesley Hohfeld, Stanley Benn, HLA Hart, Hillel Steiner and
Feinberg. In contemporaneous political philosophy, Robert Nozick and Ronald Dworkin have
prioritized the issues of rights in the literature. In the classic work, Wesley Newcomb Hohfeld
(1917) (1974) has talked in terms of “correlatives” and “contradictories” (opposites) to clarify the
meaning of rights. Rights arise from claim, consent, recognition and what supervenes upon human

nature.

The claim-right is correlative to duty, and a liberty is the contradictory of a duty. The right
as power is correlative to liability, and the right as immunity is the contradictory of
liability. A right is but primarily a moral concept. The character of moral rights differs in
several respects from that of legal rights. Moral rights in the proper sense create
corresponding duties, which may be either duties of noninterference or duties of
contribution. Rights are directed and it entails a duty owing to someone and duty regarding
someone. Someone having a duty means that some action or omission of a person is
normatively required. This is the idea that whenever one is under a duty, other things being

equal, one is required to do or omit some action.

2.3. Egoism, Utilitarianism and Deontology

In addition to the two broad classifications of schools of thought on morality,
political and philosophical ideologies have developed variously over the last few centuries.
In a survey of literature, it is evident that egoism is a doctrine of means that says that
people ought to promote one’s own good. There is a sense in which egoism is also
universalistic, impersonal, and egalitarian. An egoist is claiming that the only unconditional
obligation anyone has, regardless of who he may happen to be, is ‘to act compatibly with
the promotion of his own good’ (Ramon Lemos, 1986, pp. 15-28). Egoism is the theory that
all human behaviour is (psychological egoism) or ought to be (ethical egoism) motivated
entirely by self-interest. Interest “in” us are interests that one’s own self have certain things,
held to be good by the agent, which can be identified and possessed independently of the

goods of others.

The desire to feel pleasure is the most widely held candidate for the status of fundamental
motivator of all action, and pleasure is subjective, in the ‘soul’ or psyche of the agent.
Interests “of” ourselves simply refer to whose interests they are, but not at all to what the
interests are taken in- what they are aimed at affecting , either in or outside of ourselves.
The so-called theory that we always act on motives of ours may therefore be called

‘tautological psychological egoism”. It is surely obvious that we are very interested in at

98|Page

least some others, both positively (as with love) and negatively (as with hatred) (Narveson,

1998, pp. 15-6).

Egoism comes in two forms: teleological egoism and deontological egoism. Both
teleological as well as deontological egoist may admit that certain things, such as pleasure
or the satisfaction of desire, are intrinsically good, regardless of whose pleasure it is or
whose desires are satisfied. The teleological egoist attempts to derive this obligation from
an intrinsic good, whereas the deontological egoist does not. For the deontological egoist
this obligation is ultimate and un-derived. In the literature, egoism is expressed in two
variants: extreme version of egoism and moderate version of egoism. The extreme
teleological egoist maintains that its source is that its fulfilment is either necessary for or
else contributes to the attainment of one’s own goods. Neoclassicism in economics is a
hybrid of ethics of egoism, utilitarianism, libertarianism and liberalism that champions the
values of individual liberty from undue interference, unfettered property rights of

individual, aggregate human welfare, and constitutional public choice (Roth, 1974).

The moderate teleological egoist denies that the obligation in question is necessarily
derivative from the fact that its fulfilment is necessary for or contributes to the attainment
of one’s own good. The moderate deontological egoist admits that there are certain non-
teleological and non-egoistic prima facie obligations that do not issue from the fact that
their fulfillment is necessary for or contributes to the fulfilment of one’s unconditional
obligation to promote one’s own good. The extreme deontological egoist maintains that all
non-teleological and putative non-egoistic prima facie obligations are in fact obligatory in
particular situations if and only if an attempt to fulfil them is necessary for or contributes to
the fulfilment of one’s unconditional obligation to promote one’s own good. The problem
is that the claim that the only unconditional obligation anyone has is to act compatibly with

the attainment of one’s own good is inadmissible and contestable.

Axiological hedonism is a theory of intrinsic value, with two core components: first, all
pleasure is intrinsically good; and, second, nothing but pleasure is intrinsically good.
Ethical hedonism is the view that the moral rightness or wrongness of an act is a function of

the pleasure the act produces. This view is logically distinct from axiological hedonism.

99|Page

Genetic hedonism is the view that all of our present desires arose originally from our desire
for pleasure. Psychological hedonism is the view that everyone is actuated, ultimately, by
nothing but self-interest. Whenever a person acts, her ultimate aim is only to promote her
own survival, her own pleasure, her own self-esteem, and so on. Epicurus, Bentham and
Mill held the theory of hedonism. Axiological hedonism is not a theory about rightness or
wrongness of actions though it figures prominently in theories of Jeremy Bentham (1789)

and John Stuart Mill (1871).

Vilfredo Pareto (1971) remarked that the study of political economy is separate from that of
morality, but it is not to assert that the former matters more than the latter. Political
economy cannot be accused of not taking morality into account. It is a gross mistake to
accuse a person who studies ‘economic actions — or homo oeconomicus — of neglecting , or
even of scorning moral, religious, etc., actions — that is the homo ethicus, the homo

religious, etc.’ (Pareto, 1971, p.13).

A teleological conception is one in which the ‘good’ is specified independently of the
‘right’, though the ‘rights’ are distributed subsequently so as to maximize the ‘good’.
Teleology is thus an approach to ethics that evaluates actions on basis of the consequences
of those acts, and utilitarianism is one kind of teleological ethics. In a goods-centric view in
normative philosophy and economics, the goods are both means to an end (having
instrumental value) and the ends-in-themselves (having intrinsic value). Consequentialism
begins with a theory of the good and insists that the good ought to be promoted (Pettit,
1993, p. 231).

Questions of morality are largely questions of results. The dictum “ends justify the means”
is an expression of this perspective on morality. Maximize benefits and minimize harms is
the goal in teleological utilitarianism. A theory of ethics is a philosophical structure which
together with some degree of empirical fact yields a decision procedure for moral reasoning
(Williams, 1985), and consequentialism is one decision procedure. The ‘felicific calculus’

is the archetype of the decision theory that is accepted in economics.

Rational-desire teleology is the teleological conception of practical reason together with
moral rationalism. The rational-desire teleology is centered at a view that the deontic status
of an action is determined by the agent’s reasons for and against preferring its outcome to
those of the available alternatives. In the case that an agent is morally required to perform
an action, then ‘she has most reason to desire that its outcome obtains’ (Portmore, 2011,
p.119). A rational-desire teleology contrasts with personal-value teleology as well as

impersonal-value teleology (both are consequentialism).

Let us bring the matter in historical perspective! To revisit, Jeremy Bentham (1789) held
that a person has a right whenever he/she stands to benefit from the performance of a duty.
There are two points at which the individual rights and human agency figure importantly in
Bentham’s theory. There was insistence that ‘the means to utility are privately produced’,
and therefore, the role of the government is to create an environment in which people can
enjoy the fruits of labour by ‘protecting those fruits through the rule of law’. The political
party, Bentham established eventually became known as that of the “intellectual” or
“philosophical” radicals - the party of “bourgeois doctrinaires”. Second, Bentham embraced
the idea that ‘science allows us to understand and shape our destinies’ in much better ways
than those ‘based on religion, superstition, natural law, or blind impulse’. Bentham
embeds the Enlightenment aspiration to achieve freedom in ‘a thoroughly determinist

science’ (Shapiro, 2004, p. 36).

Classical utilitarianism as advanced by Jeremy Bentham was first and foremost a standard
for judging the public action. The utilitarian ethic was pitted against the philosophy of
natural rights and natural law. Utilitarianism had a lineage in the ideas and advocacy of
British political and moral philosophy of the eighteenth century. Though the basic idea of
moral theory of utilitarianism and the ethics of the “goods’ was pioneered by Francis
Hutcheson (1725) and David Hume (1777) much earlier, this corpus of moral philosophy
was eulogized and formalized by Jeremy Bentham (1789), John Stuart Mill (1871) and
Henry Sidgwick (1874).

A distinction between the ‘act-utilitarianism’ and the ‘rule-utilitarianism’ was developed

later in the writings of Richard Hare (1976; 1981) and Jack Harsanyi (1982; 1986). In

recent times, basic tenets of utilitarianism has been summarized by R F Harrod (1936), J C
Smart and Bernard Williams (1973) and Amartya Sen and Bernard Willaims (1982). In the

jurisprudence (Charles Rowley, 1993, pp. 38-9).

Utilitarianism is committed to treating the good as an aggregative commodity, something
that comes in greater or lesser amounts. The utilitarian conception of the public good
embodies a definition of “good” in application to the whole community of persons rather
than to individual members. It is a supra-individual ‘good’ to which is attributed the status
of an independent existence and to which a goal is assigned that must be promoted. Most
versions of utilitarianism have been welfarist, consequentialist, aggregative and
maximizing. Epicurus, Jeremy Bentham and John Stuart Mill held the theory of hedonism.
Axiological hedonism is not a theory about rightness or wrongness of actions though it

figures prominently in theories of Bentham and Mill.

In the public sphere of the bureaucracy of the government, the foundation of utilitarian
public policy analysis is based on the measurement of welfare. The utilitarian conception of
the public good embodies a definition of “good” in application to the whole community of
persons rather than to individual members. It is a supra-individual ‘good’ to which is
attributed an independent existence and which must be promoted. In twentieth century, the
utilitarian social welfare theory was advanced. Central to the social welfare theoretic

enterprise is the distinction between efficiency in the sense of Pareto optimality and equity.

The social welfare theory has been based on two mathematised theorems about achieving
Pareto optimality or efficiency in perfect and imperfect markets of the economy. The first
theorem of the utilitarian social welfare theory is about the Pareto efficiency in a perfectly
competitive market. The second theorem is about enhancing the Pareto efficiency in the
imperfect market and the environment of externalities and public goods through lump-sum
taxes and subsidies. Presently, the social welfare theory that is informed by a combination
of utilitarianism and consequentialism deploys only one standard of moral appraisal of
human action and working of social institutions, and that standard is called ‘efficiency’ —

better called the ‘Pareto optimality’.

Economics borrowed the concept of efficiency to refer to the competence and good

the waste and technological cumbersomeness are avoided. In general, asking for efficiency
is to inquire about the process of making the best use of limited resources of time, effort

divided by input, giving the maximum output of goods and services at minimum cost of
time, effort and money. In general, efficiency characterizes the quality and attribute of the
processes of activities of production, consumption and exchange in economics. While
ethical equilibrium and political efficiency goes together, the competitive market

agents and persons, but efficiency is an agent-neutral concept. It is applicable to human

actions, and not the human agency — the performers of actions.

Exchange efficiency refers to the production of goods that the consumer wants at the right

With domination of utilitarianism, the neoclassical political economy tradition of

In the school of moral thought of utilitarianism, the cause of the ‘goods’ of the aggregate

was championed as the supreme morality. In the beginning, the emphasis was on promotion
of a priori the ‘good’ in the lives of free and autonomous individual beings. Subsequently,

individuals. Utility, satisfaction happiness, pleasure and _ want-fulfillment (all
interchangeable terminologies to signify a single thing) is the criterion of appraisal of

The philosophical version of utilitarianism insists on at least three premises: one, the
individual wellbeing ought to be the end of moral action; two, each individual is to count
for one and no more than one; three, the object of social action ought to be maximization of
general utility. The third premise is represented by Bentham’s famous phrase: to promote
the greatest happiness of the greatest number (Welch, 1987, p.770). However, the moral
principle version of utilitarianism consists of three propositions: welfarism, sum-ranking,
and consequentialism. This is the architecture of classical utilitarianism which consists of a

conjunction of three theses: welfarism, maximalism, and consequentialism (Almond, 1993).

According to the thesis of maximalism, it is always wrong to fail to do the best thing
available. The idea is that what is ‘good’ is the pleasure, or happiness, or utility, or
‘welfare’ only. The goodness of a state of affairs is a function of utility information alone
regarding that state of affairs only. The thesis of sum-ranking requires that the utility
information regarding any state of affair must be assessed in terms of the sum total of all
utilities of that state of affair (Amartya Sen, 1987). Since welfare is the only valued ‘good’,

utilitarianism advocates a duty to promote the ‘good consequences’ (Almond, 1993).

Consequentialism is then a thesis that a person should act with a purpose and a goal to
achieve, and an individual action is to be judged ‘right’ or ‘wrong’ solely according to the
value of its intended consequences. It requires that every choice of individual should be
determined ultimately by the goodness of the consequent state of affairs (Amartya Sen,
1987, p. 39). According to the ideology of consequentialism combined with the theses of
welfarism and maximalism, the comparative goodness of any option depends exclusively
on the comparative goodness of the consequences of choosing that option over others.
Principles of human action are weighed against consequence, and therefore, the utilitarian
morality is justified by beneficial effects of goal-directed human actions. The consequence
of all human actions is to be computed in terms of utility yield that in turn is to be measured
in the units of chief human goods called ‘pleasure’ or ‘satisfaction’ of preference or in

short, ‘welfare’.

Justice was simply conceived simply as consistent with the maximal efficiency in attaining

Ethics of Deontology

Deontology is a philosophical and ethical approach that asserts that matters of right
and wrong ought to be assessed through reference to standards other than the results that
are produced by certain decisions and actions. Treating person with respect is one
deontological standard. The principle of integrity is another set of deontological concerns.
Deontology requires that the ‘rights’ are to be distributed independently of the ‘goods’.
The idea of the ‘rights’ is prioritized over the idea of ‘goods. In other words, deontology
stands in contrast to consequentialism. The conception of the right is the moral law that is
given by pure (as opposed to empirical) practical reason. Specification of what ends is

permissible and what social arrangements are right and just is possible in the light of this

106|Page

conception. The conception of the good ought not be given independently of the right
(Rawls, 1999, p. 509).

Morality in deontology approach is not a matter of producing right results, but of meeting
obligations that relate to features of acts themselves rather than their results. The
deontological approach advocating the equal distribution of the ‘rights’ having an intrinsic
value in its own right trumps the consequentialist approach championing the instrumental

value of the equal distribution of the ‘goods’.

A consistent deontological ethics was propounded by Immanuel Kant, based on the
fundamental principle of Categorical Imperative. It expressed the idea that action should be
chosen for their intrinsic features - especially the feature of being part of a policy for action
that could be followed on every occasion by an agent who is rational in a special Kantian
sense. Emmanuel Kant propounded the rule-deontological ethics. It defended the priority of
rights over goods of action. The ethics of rule-deontology is the rights-based ethics and an
ethics of categorical imperative - what one ought to do is what is obligatory. The maxim is:
“always act in such a manner that your actions can be taken as a universal maxim” or

“always act according to a maxim that is at the same time valid as a universal moral law”’.

In its original formulation, Kantian ethics of the categorical imperative is: “act according to
the maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law”,
and ‘act as though the maxim of your action were by your will to become a universal law of
nature” (Kant, 1785, p. 422). This is the maxim of universality and reversibility. The
maxim, or the reason behind our action, should be one that we wish to be practiced
universally by others (universality), and one which we could reasonably wish to be on the
receiving end of (reversibility). In another formulation, Kant says that: “act in such a way
that you always treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other,

never simply as a means, but always at the same time as an end” (Kant, 1785, p. 429).

In deontological criterion of assessing the worth of an action through some definite
righteousness features of the act itself, all human persons have certain rights simply by

virtue of being and as persons and such rights that have intrinsic worth for human persons

107|Page

qua persons are to be distributed equally. The capacity to produce a surplus (material and
biological) as part of the capability set is a need that is to be accorded the status of a right
and distributed rather equally among population of human persons. Persons have rights to
resources or commodities or incomes that are consequentially good but rights to biological-
and-economic-surplus-producing capacity that is intrinsically a claim and entitlement in its

own right is much more significant.

3.2. Liberal Construct of Ideal Justice

A philosopher attempts to conceptualise an absolute and transcendental conception
of perfect, optimal and maximal justice with a belief in a sort of vicious universalism. It is
as if a single understanding and conception of justice with a fixed constitution applies to all
people in all contexts of all cultures everywhere in an immutable way. Such a notion of
perfect, optimal and maximal justice pervades the writings in liberal and libertarian

traditions as well as in the tradition of absolute egalitarianism.

An absolute and transcendental rendering of perfect, optimal and maximal justice are
however certainly not the facts of actual states of affairs and the actual procedures and
processes of doing and becoming in the world. Justice to such an extreme level is not as yet
achieved anywhere. It is a heuristic construct for analytical purpose that is used in
evaluation of the ideal of a liberal and well-ordered society. The idea of heuristic construct
of liberal perfect optimal and maximal justice is premised on absolute conceptions of
categories of liberty and freedom; it requires “liberty” (negative and /or positive liberty)
including the political freedom along with individual responsibility of each person. Such a
conception of justice is built on an ideal conception of perfect individual liberty and
