## Abstract

Abstract This article seeks to demonstrate that Kant throughout the Critique of Pure Reason employs a type of analogical reasoning that belongs to the methodical backbone of the work. More specifically, I take him to draw on disciplines such as mathematics, general logic, and empirical psychology to expose the a priori features of human cognition at stake in his analyses. After examining Kant’s own comments on the use of analogies in the Critique of Pure Reason, the Prolegomena, the Critique of Judgment and relevant lecture transcripts, I outline the predicative type of analogical reasoning I take to be employed in the Critique of Pure Reason itself. Turning from theory to practice, I analyze Kant’s use of (1) metaphors, (2) analogical inferences intended to clarify concepts such as transcendental illusion, and (3) analogical inferences that inform key arguments, namely, the metaphysical deduction and the so-called subjective deduction contained in the 1781 edition of the Critique. In each case, I argue, Kant’s sustained attempt to illuminate the a priori dimension of the human mind by means of analogical inferences is akin to the use of analogical inferences in former natural theology to obtain cognitions of God.

1. Introduction
In the Critique of Pure Reason, Kant abstracts from past elaborations of metaphysics in order to raise the question as to whether this discipline is possible at all (A3–4). This problem cannot be solved, on his account, unless philosophy engages in a thoroughgoing analysis of the human mind.
However, Kant does not explain how this peculiar and rarely attempted analysis (A65/B90)
can actually be carried out: it is unclear how philosophy can actually shed light on pure sensibility, the pure understanding, and pure reason in view of their elements and the cognitive acts these faculties engage in. Given Kant’s premises, this surely cannot be done by tracing them to actual non-scientific and scientific practices, as he took Locke and Hume to have done (B127–128). Yet the Critique of Pure Reason contains many discussions of cognitive elements and acts that rely on other disciplines, including the logical forms of judgment treated in the metaphysical deduction
(A70–76/B95–101) and the threefold act of apprehension, reproduction, and recognition treated in the A-Deduction (A97). Kant’s repeated appeal to empirical and mathematical concepts also

does not sit well with his assertion in the Prolegomena that the Critique of Pure Reason develops
“cognition from its original seeds without relying on any fact whatsoever.”1
In what follows, I tackle this apparent incongruity by examining conspicuous and inconspicuous types of analogical reasoning enacted in the Critique of Pure Reason.2 Kant’s frequent use of analogies in this work to clarify concepts and problems has been the subject of various studies.3 While I do elaborate on this aspect, my main aim is to demonstrate that Kant throughout the Critique employs analogical inferences that belong to the methodical backbone of the work. More specifically, I take him to draw on disciplines such as mathematics, general logic, and empirical psychology in order to expose non-phenomenal features of human cognition that, as such, cannot be accessed in any direct manner. By proceeding analogically in this methodological sense, I argue, Kant seeks to bring into focus the a priori concepts, cognitions, and cognitive activities at stake in his “completely new science” (Prol, AA 04:262) without letting these results infringe upon the purely intellectual nature of the analyses themselves.4

2. Kant on Analogical Reasoning
Simply put, drawing analogies is a type of cognitive act that consists in transferring features of something known to something treated as unknown, in a particular respect, so as to generate a more determinate conception of the latter. The purpose and epistemological status assigned to this type of reasoning varies widely and depends on the disciplinary context. The sought-for x can further be treated as the subject of predicates or as member of a relation. I will refer to these two types as predicative and proportional analogies, respectively.6 Both types were discussed from at least Aristotle onward, and Kant’s account follows the tradition in many respects.7
Whereas Kant’s own discussions focus on proportional analogies, I hold that the distinction between the two types is a relative one and carries little weight in other contexts than Kant’s account of natural theology. In what follows, I discuss his main examples and relevant comments in tandem, starting with those that concern mathematics and the natural sciences.
In the Transcendental Analytic of the Critique of Pure Reason, Kant introduces the concept of an analogy of experience by first considering the better-known case of mathematical analogies.

The Critique of Judgment (1790) addresses the use of analogical inferences in natural theology from a different angle (AA 05:464–465). Kant here charges the discipline not so much with assuming that analogical inferences yield cognitions of God as with ignoring the constraints on any such inferences. He is particularly concerned with the tendency of natural theology to erroneously transfer aspects of human cognition to God that involve sensibility, something which the mere use of proportional analogies does not preclude.21
In the Critique of Judgment, Kant prepares his criticism in this regard by discussing a proportional analogy intended to clarify the activities carried out by beavers, of which the nonphenomenal cause is treated as sought-for x. In this case, the two relations at hand are ‘human intelligence : buildings’ and ‘x : beaver dams.’ These causal relations are construed by positing
‘artisanal activity’ as their common denominator, i.e., as a rule instantiated by the two relations.

In sum, the Prolegomena and the Critique of Judgment vindicate the use of analogies in natural theology on the condition that they are proportional and do not transfer concepts tainted by sensibility. Kant deems this use purposive in that it allows us to conceive of God in a determinate way – e.g., as creator or benefactor – without laying claim to objective cognitions of God. This vindication is relevant to Kant, in turn, in that natural theology understood in this way can provide humans with conceptual tools to interpret the world as a sphere that, at least in principle, allows for the increasing actualization of the highest good.25
Whereas Kant’s broader intentions are irrelevant to the present article, I have elaborated on his account of the use of analogical reasoning in natural theology because of my interest in the question as to how transcendental philosophy, for its part, can transfer known features of the human mind to a type of thought treated as sought-for x so as to produce a more determinate concept of the latter. In Sections 4 to 6, I argue that the Critique of Pure Reason proceeds along these lines in order to shed light on the a priori dimension of human cognition. By way of transition from Kant’s theory to my own analysis of what he did in practice, the next section reflects on the results obtained so far.

3. Analogical reasoning as a three-step procedure
In this section, I draw five conclusions from Kant’s account of analogical reasoning, in particular as regards his examples, that are not explicitly drawn by him but that are relevant to my analysis of the analogical inferences employed in the Critique of Pure Reason.
(1) As is shown by the example of the earth and the moon in Kant’s lectures, we cannot infer from his published discussions and qualified defense of proportional analogies that he objected to the use of predicative analogies in all contexts.
(2) Kant held that non-mathematical proportional analogies proceed by comparing two causal relations (cf. A179/B222, KU, AA 05:464 n), which means that natural theologians who use these analogies must treat the sought-for divine attributes “as if” God existed independently of the human mind.
(3) I believe we can infer from the examples treated above that any type of analogical reasoning consists in three steps, namely, [1] the ascent from a known instance to its ground, [2]
the conception of this ground as the common denominator of a known and unknown instance, and
[3] the descent from this common denominator to the instance treated as sought-for x. Both [1]
and [2] prepare the actual transferal of the feature at hand carried out in [3]. In the case of the predicative analogies used in pre-Kantian natural theology, these steps consist in [1] the ascent from a feature of human rationality to a feature of rationality per se, [2] the conception of this feature of rationality per se as the common denominator of a known and unknown instance, and
[3] the descent from this common denominator to its divine instantiation. In the case of proportional analogies, conversely, we [1] ascent from a known relation a : b to its ground, i.e., a rule, [2] conceive of this rule as one that governs a known and unknown relation, and [3] descent from the rule per se to a relation treated as unknown, i.e., c : x. Even though actual analogical inferences may not always carry out steps [1], [2], and [3] explicitly or in this sequence, I take them to be essentially constituted by these steps.
(4) As can be seen from the beaver example, the common denominator that makes it possible to transfer features from the known to the unknown instance need not be a genus in the accepted sense of the term. It suffices to posit either a relation or one or more features as common denominator of two different instances.
(5) The common denominator of analogies can be established in various ways: whereas analogical inferences in the natural sciences rely on inductive generalizations, analogical inferences in natural theology rely on an introspective examination of the human mind.
Given (1 and (2), we cannot expect transcendental philosophy to use proportional analogies in its attempt to produce a determinate concept of the a priori cognitive activities carried out by the human mind, for the latter cannot be conceived as cause. Given (5), the only way in which transcendental philosophy can establish the required common denominator is by setting out from features of human cognition that can be identified by observing the activities carried out by the
11

human mind. In the remainder of this article, I will argue that Kant indeed employs predicative types of analogical reasoning in the Critique of Pure Reason that proceed by means of the three steps outlined in (3).
4. Analogical inferences that serve a clarificatory purpose
As I see it, Kant’s use of analogical reasoning in the Critique of Pure Reason can roughly be distinguished in three types, namely, the use of (1) metaphors and analogies intended to clarify philosophical ideas, (2) analogical inferences intended to clarify concepts that concern aspects of transcendental philosophy and therefore could not be assumed to be known, and (3) analogical inferences that are intrinsic to Kant’s actual arguments. In all cases, I submit, the analogical inferences seek to provide the reader with a more determinate conception of a sought-for x to which we lack direct access, namely, either the human mind qua source of priori cognitive acts and cognitions or features of transcendental philosophy itself. After briefly discussing the first type, the current section focuses on the analogies Kant draws to clarify the concept of transcendental illusion and the concept of a transcendental schema.26
4.1 Kant’s classical use of analogies
Kant’s frequent use of metaphors to clarify concepts such as metaphysics or critique follows the example of many classical and modern authors within and without philosophy.27 His comparison of transcendent metaphysics with a “broad and stormy ocean” (A235–236/B295, cf. A395–396), for instance, recalls Locke’s use of the same image in the introduction to An Essay Concerning
Human Understanding (I.i.7). Even if Kant’s comparison of the history of metaphysics to a battlefield in the first Preface (Aviii, cf. Bxv) is more original, it can be understood independently of the framework established by the Critique of Pure Reason.28

4.2 Transcendental illusion
Apart from his use of classical analogies, Kant also draws analogical inferences to clarify concepts such as ‘transcendental illusion.’ These inferences are bound to the framework of the
Critique of Pure Reason and less easy to identify. In this case, I hope to show, the known relatum is not an arbitrarily chosen empirical concept, but a better-known instance of the concept that functions as the common denominator of both relata. Since these concepts are produced as Kant goes along, the method he employs in this regard resembles that of empirical concept formation.29
As is well known, Kant conceives of the transcendental dialectic as a whole as a “logic of illusion” (A131/B170) since it investigates the illusory judgments about the soul, the world as such, and God generated in classical metaphysics. According to Kant, any metaphysics that seeks to turn these ideas of reason into objects of cognition rests on a single misguided assumption called “transcendental illusion” (A295/B352). But how can this concept become meaningful to the non-initiated reader? I contend that Kant proceeds by drawing on a reflection on the concepts of empirical and logical illusion, i.e., types of illusion with which his readers could be assumed to be familiar. By drawing more or less explicit analogies between these known instances of illusion and transcendental illusion, the relevant features of the latter can be exhibited.

4.3 Transcendental schemata
Kant employs a similar procedure to clarify a priori elements of cognition such as intuitions, concepts, and schemata. In the case of transcendental schemata, to which my discussion will be limited, he proceeds by reflecting on the relevant features of both mathematical and empirical schemata. Even if he could not expect his readers to be familiar with them, they are easier to explain and, thus, serve as complementary stepping stones in the clarification of the sought-for x.
Kant begins his account by comparing mathematical and transcendental schemata, both of which are pure, i.e., produced independently of empirical input. By considering what it means to represent the concept of a triangle in thought, it emerges that what is represented in this case is not an image but “a rule of the synthesis of the imagination with regard to pure shapes in space,”
namely, the act of combining three straight lines in a particular way. The representation of such a rule is called a schema (A140–141/B179–180, cf. A105). The schema of a triangle thus (a)
represents the rule contained in the concept of a triangle in a sensible manner. This representation
(b) requires the imagination and (c) allows us to produce a particular determination of space, namely, (d) by unifying a manifold of representations (A140–141/B179–180).
