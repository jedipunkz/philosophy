Abstract:

Museum studies, or museology, has evolved into a recognized academic discipline, especially
through the influence of American and German models. Rooted in both practical applications and
theoretical inquiry, the development of museum studies reflects broader intellectual, cultural, and scientific
paradigms. This paper explores the institutionalization of museum studies as a scientific discipline,
highlighting its growth in the United States and Germany and examining philosophical contributions that
have shaped its epistemological foundation.

The development of museum studies—also referred to as museology—as a scientific and academic
discipline is a significant chapter in the evolution of modern cultural and educational institutions. This paper
examines the historical emergence of museum studies as a science, with particular emphasis on the German
and American models, while also integrating philosophical perspectives that have influenced the theoretical
foundations of the field. The German tradition contributed a structured, theory-oriented approach grounded
in aesthetics, pedagogy, and the philosophy of knowledge, drawing from Neo-Kantian and
phenomenological frameworks. Thinkers such as Immanuel Kant, Hans-Georg Gadamer, and Otto Neurath
provided critical insights into the nature of knowledge, interpretation, and communication—concepts
central to museum theory and practice.

In contrast, the American model emphasized professionalization, pragmatism, and public service,
inspired by the educational theories of John Dewey and the democratic values underlying cultural access.
American museology evolved through practical museum training programs and the institutional efforts of
bodies like the American Association of Museums, focusing on exhibition design, visitor experience, and
museum management.

Introduction.

The transformation of museum work into a field of scholarly inquiry emerged in the late
19th and early 20th centuries. As museums evolved from private collections into public
educational institutions, a need arose for systematic approaches to collection management,
exhibition design, and public engagement. The academic recognition of museology—or museum
studies—stemmed from this transformation. The American and German models of museum
studies played a central role in shaping its development as a scientific field.

Methodology
This study employs a qualitative, interdisciplinary research methodology grounded in
historical analysis, comparative evaluation, and philosophical inquiry. The purpose is to explore
the formation of museum studies as a scientific discipline through the lens of two influential
national models—Germany and the United States—and to analyze how philosophical thought
contributed to its theoretical foundation.

1. Historical-Comparative Method

A historical-comparative approach was applied to trace the evolution of museum studies in both
Germany and the United States from the late 19th century to the late 20th century. Archival
materials, foundational texts in museology, and institutional histories were reviewed to identify
milestones, key figures, and the progression of ideas in each country. The comparative element
involved juxtaposing the development paths, educational frameworks, and museological

philosophies of both nations to identify convergences and divergences.

Early Foundations of Museum Studies
The roots of museology can be traced to Enlightenment thinking, which emphasized the
categorization and systematization of knowledge. Museums became a locus for the classification
of human and natural history, echoing the empirical methodologies advocated by philosophers like
Immanuel Kant and Auguste Comte. Kant emphasized the role of structured knowledge in
understanding phenomena, a principle echoed in the systematic cataloging practices of early

museums (Kant, 1781/1998).

In the 19th century, the rise of the public museum coincided with industrialization and nation-
building. Museums in this period were seen as tools for civic education, moral improvement, and
the reinforcement of national identity (Bennett, 1995).

Historical Background of the Creation of Museum Studies

The origins of museum studies—also known as museology—are deeply rooted in the evolution of
museums themselves. While the practice of collecting and displaying objects dates back to
antiquity, the formalization of museum studies as a scholarly discipline is a relatively recent
development that emerged alongside modern academic and cultural institutions in the 19th and
20th centuries.

From Cabinets of Curiosity to Public Institutions

In the Renaissance and early modern periods, private collections—known as cabinets of curiosity
(Wunderkammern)—were curated by aristocrats and scholars who sought to display natural
specimens, antiquities, and artifacts as representations of wealth and intellectual prestige. These
collections were rarely accessible to the public and lacked any systematic methodology for
interpretation or education (Impey & MacGregor, 1985).

The Enlightenment brought about a shift in thinking about knowledge, classification, and public
access to education. Philosophers such as Denis Diderot and Jean-Jacques Rousseau emphasized
public enlightenment through institutions of knowledge, including museums. This led to the
transformation of elite collections into public museums, exemplified by the opening of the British
Museum in 1759 and the Louvre in 1793 after the French Revolution.

19th-Century Developments and the Professionalization of Museum Work

The 19th century witnessed the proliferation of public museums across Europe and North America,
reflecting national ambitions, scientific progress, and the emergence of public education. Museums
were increasingly seen as vehicles for civic moral instruction, nationalism, and scientific
dissemination (Bennett, 1995). However, museum work remained largely unstandardized, and
there was no formal academic training for curators, conservators, or educators.

During this period, Germany emerged as a center for intellectual engagement with the museum as
an institution. German scholars and museum professionals began to articulate principles for
museum management, object classification, and display. Figures like Gustav Waagen and later
Hugo von Tschudi helped define the museum's role in society, linking it to art history,

archaeology, and pedagogy.

The Emergence of Museology as a Science in Germany

By the early 20th century, German-speaking countries began using the term Museologie to refer
to the systematic study of museums. This reflected a growing interest in developing a theoretical
framework for museum practice, informed by philosophy, education theory, and scientific
classification. The German model emphasized theoretical rigor, influenced by the Neo-Kantian
philosophy prevalent in Central European universities, which stressed the importance of
categorization, phenomenology, and historical consciousness in interpreting material culture
(Schubert, 2009).

Notably, Otto Neurath’s concept of the “museum of the future” introduced a radical rethinking
of the museum’s communicative function, proposing simplified, visual languages for universal
understanding. Neurath’s ideas marked a departure from traditional object-centered museums
toward interpretation and social relevance (Neurath, 1933).

The American Contribution: Pragmatism and Professionalization

In contrast to the theoretical emphasis in Germany, the American development of museum studies
followed a more pragmatic and professional path. The United States saw the rapid expansion of
museums in the late 19th and early 20th centuries, aligned with the ideals of democracy,
progressivism, and public education. Influenced by the educational philosophy of John Dewey,
American museums began to integrate visitor-centered approaches, interactive exhibitions, and
school outreach programs (Hein, 1998).

The creation of professional organizations such as the American Association of Museums
(AAM) in 1906 and the establishment of the first formal museum training programs in the 1920s,
such as the University of Chicago’s program and later the Cooperstown Graduate Program,
signaled the institutionalization of museum studies as an academic and vocational field. These
programs emphasized museum management, education, exhibition design, and public service—
marking a shift from traditional scholarly curation to public engagement and administration
Global Institutionalization and Academic Recognition

By the mid-20th century, museum studies began to emerge as an independent field of academic
research and teaching. The establishment of the International Committee for Museology
(ICOFOM) under the International Council of Museums (ICOM) in 1977 further signaled the
field’s growing international legitimacy. Museology programs began appearing in universities

across Europe, North America, and later in Asia and Africa.

care to include questions of interpretation, audience engagement, representation, and institutional

The German Model: Theoretical and Systematic Museology

Germany is often credited with laying the theoretical foundation for museum studies. In the early
20th century, museum professionals in Germany began advocating for a structured, scientific
approach to museum work. The term Museologie gained currency, reflecting the effort to
systematize museum-related knowledge.

A central figure in this development was Hugo von Tschudi, who emphasized the museum as an
educational institution. German thinkers like Alexander Dorner and Otto Neurath proposed that
museums should not only preserve objects but also communicate ideas and narratives. Neurath’s
"museum of the future" focused on visual education and the use of didactic methods (Neurath,
1933).

By mid-century, German museology was characterized by its theoretical rigor, influenced by Neo-
Kantian and phenomenological traditions. Theodor Lipps and later Hans-Georg Gadamer
contributed indirectly to museology by advancing theories of aesthetics, perception, and
interpretation, which informed curatorial practices (Gadamer, 1960/1989).

The American Model: Pragmatic and Professionalized Museum Studies

In contrast, the American model of museum studies developed with a more pragmatic and
professional orientation. American museums were influenced by John Dewey’s educational
philosophy, which emphasized experiential learning and the social function of education (Dewey,
1934). Dewey’s ideas inspired innovations in exhibition design, such as interactive displays and
narrative-driven curation.

In the 1920s and 1930s, institutions like the American Association of Museums (now the AAM)
began to standardize museum practices. The emergence of formal training programs—such as
those at the University of Chicago and later the Cooperstown Graduate Program—marked the
institutionalization of museum studies in the U.S.

A significant contribution to the American model came from Stephen Weil, who argued that

museums must balance their roles as both educational and cultural institutions while maintaining

Philosophical Perspectives and the Scientific Status of Museum Studies
Philosophers have long debated whether museology qualifies as a "science." Karl Popper’s notion
of falsifiability as a demarcation criterion for science would seemingly exclude museum studies,
as it lacks predictive theories. However, others, like Thomas Kuhn, have argued for broader
conceptions of scientific paradigms that accommodate disciplines rooted in humanistic inquiry
(Kuhn, 1962).
Museology intersects with epistemology, aesthetics, hermeneutics, and ethics—domains often
addressed by philosophers such as Michel Foucault, who examined how institutions like museums
shape knowledge and power (Foucault, 1972). Foucault’s archaeology of knowledge offers a
critical lens through which to understand how museums construct narratives and authority.
Additionally, Eilean Hooper-Greenhill, influenced by post-structuralist thought, challenged the
traditional positivist models in museology, advocating for interpretive and visitor-centered
approaches (Hooper-Greenhill, 2000). Her work contributed to the field’s theoretical depth and
opened new pathways for research in museum education and semiotics.
Conclusion
Museum studies has evolved from a set of professional practices into a multidisciplinary academic
field. The German model, with its theoretical and philosophical orientation, and the American
model, emphasizing pragmatism and professionalization, have both significantly influenced this
transformation. Philosophers—from Kant to Foucault—have enriched the field by offering
insights into the nature of knowledge, perception, and institutional authority. Today, museum
studies stand as a hybrid discipline that draws from the sciences, humanities, and social sciences
to address complex questions about culture, memory, and meaning.
