The argument is exact and it can be stated at the outset. The militant
atheist is the purest instance of the vanguardist, because he does not merely
attack a particular creed that serves a particular order; he attacks the whole
apparatus of belief, on the theory that religion is a fraud — a thing the
priests, in league with the kings, laid over the people to keep them docile —
and that reason, applied with sufficient force, can peel the fraud away and
let the free, rational human being stand up underneath. This is the doctrine
of false consciousness before Karl Marx gave it a name, and it fails for the
two reasons the whole of this project has been assembling. The first is the
one The Man Underneath established: there is no rational man beneath the
believer, because the faith is not a veil over him but a load-bearing part of

him — his community, his consolation, the frame through which a hard life

It cannot, because managed belief is not an accident of history that the right
revolution could abolish; it is what a large society does with the mind it has.
The most the war achieves is to change which belief, and to change it, almost
always, for a worse one: unchecked where the old was plural, held by force
where the old had grown cheap, and murderous in the changing.

Two disciplines must be stated before the cases, because without them
the argument would collapse into the thing it is not. The first is that this
paper does not adjudicate whether any religion is true. It brackets that
question entirely, as the whole method does, and asks only how belief func-

of any creed’s claims about God or the soul; it is an account of a policy and
its results. The second is that the revolutionaries were very often right about
the thing they were attacking. The Church of the French Ancien Régime,
the Orthodox Church of the Tsars, the Buddhist establishment of feudal
Mongolia — these were, in the framework’s own terms, frequently robes

The Enlightenment did not invent the imposture theory, but it gave it its
sharpest modern edge. Its rawest form was the clandestine Treatise of the
Three Impostors, a book that circulated in secret manuscript through
eighteenth-century Europe and named Moses, Jesus, and Muhammad as
three deceivers who had each fabricated a religion to master a credulous
people — the founders of the great faiths recast as con men, and faith itself
as the long shadow of their con. In the atheist priest Jean Meslier the theory
found a voice of extraordinary bitterness: his posthumous Testament, the
first sustained written defence of atheism in French, denounced religion as a
fraud propping up the powerful, and gave rise to the phrase, adapted and
made famous by Diderot in a couplet and often misattributed to Voltaire,
that humanity would not be free until the last king was strangled with the
entrails — the guts — of the last priest. The line’s savagery is the imposture
theory’s emotional core laid bare: king and priest are one apparatus, throne
and altar a single machine of deceit, and the killing of the one is continuous
with the killing of the other. In the Baron d’Holbach, whose System of
Nature of 1770 was the first openly atheist book published in France, the
theory acquired a full materialist metaphysics: no God, no soul, no purpose
in nature, and religion an invention of fear exploited by priestcraft. And in
the Marquis de Condorcet, writing his Sketch for a Historical Picture of the
Progress of the Human Mind while in hiding from the Terror that his own
party had loosed, it acquired its optimism — the confidence that
superstition would recede as reason advanced, that the arc of history bent

away from the priest and toward the philosopher.

But the figure who fixed the premise in the modern mind was Voltaire,
and it is worth dwelling on him, because the tell that undoes the whole
project is already visible in the most famous atheist-adjacent writer of the
age — who was not, in fact, an atheist at all. Voltaire’s war-cry, repeated
across his letters from around 1759 and sometimes used as a signature, was
écrasez linféame — “crush the infamous thing” — and the infamous thing
was the Church, or superstition, or fanaticism, the exact referent shifting
but the target always the apparatus of organised religion and the intolerance
he saw it breed. To Frederick the Great he wrote that a wise prince “with
money, troops, and laws, can quite well govern men without the aid of
religion, which was made only to deceive them” — the imposture theory in
a single clause, religion as deception, pure and unadorned. And yet in the
same breath Voltaire drew the line that gives the whole game away. He
wished the infamous thing extirpated, he told Frederick, “I do not say
among the rabble, who are not worthy of being enlightened, and who are
apt for every yoke; I say among honest people, among men who think.” The
fraud, in other words, was to be swept from the minds of the educated elite
— but the common people were to keep it, because Voltaire understood, as
every governor understands, that a population without a sacred order is a
harder population to hold. “I want my lawyer, my tailor, and my wife to
believe in God,” he has a character say, “so, I imagine, I shall be less robbed
and less deceived.” And in the line that has outlived all his others: / God did
not exist, it would be necessary to invent him.

Hold that admission against the theory it accompanies, because the
contradiction is the seed of everything this paper will trace. Voltaire says in
one breath that religion is a fraud made only to deceive, and in the next that
the fraud must be kept in place for the tailor and the wife, because without
it they cannot be trusted and society cannot be held. But a fraud you must
keep because society collapses without it is not merely a fraud; it is infra-

structure. It is doing work — holding the population’s honesty, softening

dupe; it is grasped by a sufferer to bear the unbearable. And from this Marx
drew the conclusion that separates his branch from the older imposture
theory: because religion is a symptom of real conditions, “the struggle
against religion is indirectly the struggle against that world whose spiritual
aroma is religion.” You do not abolish the halo by tearing it off; you abolish
the vale of tears it crowns, and the halo fades of itself. Religion, on Marx’s
account, would wither — dissolve as the material misery that produced it
dissolved — and required no war, because a war on the symptom while the
disease raged on was worse than useless.

This is the withering branch, and it is worth marking that, whatever else
is wrong with it, it lands on the framework’s side of a crucial line. To say
that belief is grown by conditions and formation, and changes only when
conditions and formation change, is precisely the framework’s account of
how the benefit dial is set — installed early, by a person’s whole world, and
re-set only by study, by conversion, by the widened horizon, almost never by
force applied to the belief itself. The withering thesis is wrong in its
confidence that religion is merely a symptom of material misery and would
vanish once misery did; the persistence of faith among the comfortable and
the free has refuted that timetable many times over. But it is right that you
do not change what a man holds sacred by attacking the sacred thing; you
change it, if at all, by changing the man’s world so that he comes to hold

something else. The withering wing, in other words, would never have

The other branch runs through Lenin, and it takes the symptom and
hardens it back into a swindle. Lenin kept Marx’s sociology but discarded
his patience. In “Socialism and Religion,” published in a Bolshevik paper in
December 1905, he wrote that “religion is one of the forms of spiritual
oppression which everywhere weighs down heavily upon the masses of the
people,” and then, invoking Marx’s line and twisting its temperature:
“Religion is opium for the people. Religion is a sort of spiritual booze, in
which the slaves of capital drown their human image, their demand for a life
more or less worthy of man.” The shift from of to for — opium of the
people, the thing they reach for, becoming opium for the people, the thing
administered to them — is the whole distance from Marx to Lenin. For
Lenin the party could not be neutral; atheism was a duty, the propagation of
the materialist worldview a task of the revolutionary, and religion an enemy
to be combated, not merely a symptom to be outgrown. He tempered the
tactics — warning against giving the clergy the gift of martyrdom, insisting
the struggle be subordinated to the class struggle — but the direction was
set. In “On the Significance of Militant Materialism,” written in 1922 with
the Bolsheviks now in power, he called for tireless atheist propaganda and
the exposure of religion by every means. The symptom had become the
swindle again; the withering had become the war.

It was the Leninist branch — the imposture theory revived, married to
the machinery of a seized state — that the twentieth century put into
practice, and the marriage was consummated in a particular place. As the
project’s study of the Paris apparatus records, the French Communist Party
of the early and middle twentieth century served as the institutional
incubator where a Jacobin-Marxist synthesis was transmitted to a
generation of revolutionaries, colonial students among them, who absorbed

not only a theory but a whole repertoire of practice — and the “cultural-

The altar was not emptied. It was restocked.

And then came the recoil — and it came not from the Revolution’s
enemies but from its own leadership, which is what makes it such powerful
evidence. Maximilien Robespierre, the Revolution’s dominant figure by the
winter of 1793-94, looked at the Cult of Reason and recognised a danger.
He was no friend of the Catholic Church, but he was a disciple of Rousseau
and a believer in a Supreme Being, and he regarded the atheism of Hébert
and Chaumette as both false and, more to the point, politically ruinous — a
thing that would sever the Revolution from a people who believed, and
hand its enemies a weapon. His formulation is one of the most revealing
sentences the whole episode produced: “Atheism is aristocratic; the idea of a
great Being that watches over oppressed innocence and punishes

triumphant crime is altogether popular.” Read what that says. It says that
