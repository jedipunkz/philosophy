TRANSLATOR'S INTRODUCTION

THE ALGEBRA OF THE SPIRIT

WE BEGIN, aS Kierkegaard does, with the story, in the Gospel of John, of
Jesus raising Lazarus from the dead:

Now a certain man was sick, named Lazarus, of Bethany, the town
of Mary and her sister Martha. (It was that Mary which anointed the
Lord with ointment, and wiped his feet with her hair, whose brother
Lazarus was sick.) Therefore his sisters sent unto him, saying,
“Lord, behold, he whom thou lovest is sick.” When Jesus heard that,
he said, “This sickness is not unto death, but for the glory of God,
that the Son of God might be glorified thereby” (John 11:1-4,
Authorized [“King James”] Version).

Thus Lazarus was indeed sick, but his sickness was not “unto death,”
for as Jesus explains, “our friend Lazarus sleepeth,” to which the disciples,
relieved, reply, “Lord, if he sleep, he shall do well” (vv. 11-12). But the
disciples misunderstood Jesus, and the gospel narrative therefore informs
the reader that “Jesus spake of his death: but they thought that he had
spoken of taking of rest in sleep” (v. 13). The error had to be remedied:
“Then said Jesus unto them plainly, ‘Lazarus is dead’ ” (v. 14). Thereafter
Jesus and his disciples went to Lazarus’s grave to see for themselves, and

“when Jesus came, he found that he had lain in the grave four days already
(v. 17).

Then said Martha unto Jesus, “Lord, if thou hadst been here, my
brother had not died. But I know, that even now, whatso-ever thou
wilt ask of God, God will give it thee.” Jesus saith unto her, “Thy
brother shall rise again.” Martha saith unto him, “I know that he
shall rise again in the resurrection at the last day.” Jesus said unto
her, “I am the resurrection, and the life: he that believeth in me,
though he were dead, yet shall he live: And whosoever liveth and
believeth in me shall never die. Believest thou this?” She saith unto
him, “Yea, Lord: I believe that thou art the Christ, the Son of God,
which should come into the world” (vv. 21—27).

And so Jesus, accompanied by Mary, Martha, several of his disciples, and
other spectators, went to the grave where Lazarus lay:

Jesus said, “Take ye away the stone.” Martha, the sister of him that
was dead, saith unto him, “Lord, by this time he stinketh: for he hath
been dead four days.” Jesus saith unto her, “Said I not unto thee,
that, if thou wouldest believe, thou shouldest see the glory of God?”
Then they took away the stone from the place where the dead was
laid. And Jesus lifted up his eyes, and said, “Father, I thank thee that
thou hast heard me. And I knew that thou hearest me always: but
because of the people which stand by I said it, that they may believe
that thou hast sent me.” And when he thus had spoken, he cried with
a loud voice, “Lazarus, come forth.” And he that was dead came
forth, bound hand and foot with graveclothes: and his face was
bound about with a napkin. Jesus saith unto them, “Loose him, and
let him go” (vv. 39-44).

Though he appeared to be dead, Lazarus was not dead: his sickness had
not been “unto death,” but was a mere physical illness that served to
demonstrate “the glory of God.” What, then, is a sickness that is “unto
death,” if not death itself? This question constitutes Kierkegaard’s point of

departure in his “Christian psychological exposition for edification and
awakening.”

To answer this question, we must first look at the genesis of The Sickness
unto Death, and at Sgren Kierkegaard’s understanding of its place in his
works. In many ways, Kierkegaard’s Fear and Trembling, first published in
1843, and The Sickness unto Death, first published in 1849, can be viewed
as a pair of bookends. The six years that separate the two works might seem
a relatively brief interval, but Kierkegaard’s short life—he died at forty-two
—was characterized by a literary productivity that is almost without
parallel. Indeed, in forty-two years, he published no fewer than forty-two
works.

In Fear and Trembling, Kierkegaard’s pseudonym Johannes de silentio
marshaled an assortment of literary and historical figures in presenting the
case for a third dimension that is neither self- and sense-centered (the
“aesthetic”) nor other- and society-centered (the “ethical”), but is
transcendent and God-centered (faith, the “religious”’). Thus, in 1843,
Kierkegaard had Johannes de silentio adumbrate the boundaries of the
religious, but Fear and Trembling was not an overtly Christian work. The
other bookend would be supplied years later, when Kierkegaard’s
pseudonym Anti-Climacus—originally named “Johannes Anticlimacus,”
perhaps to emphasize the relation to and contrast with the other Johannes,
who wrote Fear and Trembling—published The Sickness unto Death, a far
more compact and tightly argued work, which presents the case that,
properly understood, this religious sphere is in fact specifically Christian.

Early in 1848, Kierkegaard wrote in his journal that “a new book is to
be written called: “Thoughts That Heal from the Ground Up, Christian
Healing.’ The doctrine of the atonement will be treated here. First it will
show where the root of the sickness is found: sin. . . . 1) [The book] will be
about the consciousness of sin. ‘The Sickness unto Death.’ ”! Somewhat
later, probably in early May, amid the aftershocks of revolutionary uprisings
in Denmark and in Europe generally, Kierkegaard wrote in his journal that
“even in these days, I’ve worked on a new book on the sickness unto

death.”! Not long after, on May 13, 1848, Kierkegaard wrote a detailed
“Report on The Sickness unto Death,” in which he noted:

The book has a difficulty: it is too dialectical and harsh to make
proper use of the rhetorical, the engaging, the enthralling. The title
itself seems to indicate that it ought to contain discourses; the title is
lyrical. ...

What must be understood by the expression, “sickness unto
death.”

No. 1. Its hiddenness. .. . No. 2. Its universality .. . every other
kind of sickness is limited in one way or another; by climate, age,
etc... . No. 3. Its sustained character throughout all ages—
eternally. ... No. 4. Where does it reside? In the self. despairing
ignorance about having a self; knowledge of having a self,
despairingly not to want to be oneself, or despairingly to want to be
oneself. But the fact of the matter is that the project is far too large
for a rhetorical format, because each individual figure must be
sketched poetically. Dialectical algebra does it better.'

Although he abandoned the notion of employing a “rhetorical format” to
present his “exposition” about sin, and instead opted for the more compact
and technical language of “dialectical algebra,” Kierkegaard nonetheless
decided “it would be best to use the title The Sickness unto Death; it is so
rhetorical.”" It’s likely that most of the material for the book had already
been completed by May 13 or 14, 1848, when he made these remarks, but
Kierkegaard remained uncertain as to whether he wanted to include The
Sickness unto Death as part of a larger work or publish it on its own. A year
passed before Kierkegaard decided to publish it as a separate work, though
he would go on to reuse the name of its pseudonymous author, Anti-
Climacus, in connection with related material that he published in 1850,
under the title Practice in Christianity.

The manuscript of The Sickness unto Death was delivered to the printer
on June 29, 1849, at which point Kierkegaard noted in his journal: “At any
rate The Sickness unto Death is now coming out, but pseudonymously with
me as editor. .. . The pseudonym is named Johannes Anticlimacus, in

contrast to Climacus, who claimed not to be Christian; Anticlimacus stands
at the opposite extreme: a Christian to an extraordinary degree—I myself
push it only to the point of being a perfectly simple Christian.”! Shortly
thereafter, Kierkegaard reiterates this point, explaining that “I positioned
myself above J[ohannes] C[limacus], below Anti-C[limacus].”!!

The printing was finished on July 27, 1849, and The Sickness unto
Death was advertised for sale three days later.

Now to the book itself: what is the sickness unto death? Kierkegaard’s
pseudonym Anti-Climacus informs us that the sickness that is unto death is
a sickness not of the body but of the spirit, and therefore, of “the self.” If
the self is not the body, what is it? Here, near the outset of the book, Anti-
Climacus presents the reader with his compact definition of the self, a
definition indeed so compact that it can benefit from some unpacking.

A human being is spirit. But what is spirit? Spirit is the self. But
what is the self? The self is a relation that relates to itself, or it is
that in the relation which is the relation’s relating to itself. The self
is not the relation, but is that the relation relates to itself. A human
being is a synthesis of the infinite and the finite, of the temporal and
the eternal, of freedom and necessity, in short, a synthesis. A
synthesis is a relation between two terms. Considered in this way, a
human being is not yet a self.

In a relation between two, the relation is the third as a negative
unity, and the two relate to the relation, and in the relation to the
relation; thus, under the aspect of psyche, the relation between
psyche and body is a relation. On the other hand, if the relation
relates to itself, then this relation is the positive third, and this is the
self.

Such a relation that relates to itself, a self, must either have
established itself or have been established by another.

If the relation that relates to itself has been established by
another, then the relation is surely the third, but this relation, the
third, is in turn nonetheless a relation, relating to that which has
established the entire relation... .

This, then, is the formula that describes the state of the self when
despair is entirely eradicated: in relating to itself, and in willing to
be itself, the self is grounded transparently in the power that
established it.!

This is a famously difficult passage, difficult in Danish, and difficult as well
to translate, because the content itself is difficult to grasp. Kierkegaard
might argue that grasping God’s creation of the world and of human beings
is at least equally difficult to grasp as humanity’s plunge into the dizziness
of despair, or, as he explains it in Christian categories, as sin. Earlier, in The
Concept of Anxiety, which appeared in June 1844, only eight months after
the publication of Fear and Trembling, Kierkegaard had discussed a related
dizziness. But five years later—an eternity on Kierkegaard’s authorial time
line—in The Sickness unto Death, the question of sin was to receive a more
systematic and specifically Christian treatment. It is helpful to see the
present work as a sort of parallel to the beginning of the book of Genesis,
where without explanation or further ado, everything comes out of nothing.
Creation, of course, is soon followed by the Fall, in which a failure to heed
God’s command is responsible for situating humanity in its continuing
unhappy state. In The Sickness unto Death, Kierkegaard provides, as a sort
of anti-Genesis, a schematic description of the human self and of despair,
which is the omnipresent possibility of disorder in the self. In the book of
Genesis we begin with the world and with no explanation of how it came to
be other than that God said so and made it such. Immediately thereafter, we
have the Fall, once again without any “explanation.” Kierkegaard’s parallel
to the Genesis narrative is to present the “self” as a “synthesis,” placed here
(or “posited”) by God, a synthesis whose proper task and destiny is to
“sround” itself “transparently [i.e., self-consciously] in the power that
established it [i.e., God].” The self, then, is a possibility whose task it is to
realize (i.e., consciously embrace) its true origin, nature, and destiny.

Specifically, the self is a synthesis of three sets of contrasting
characteristics: finite/infinite, temporal/eternal, freedom/necessity. The
human self, in other words, is a synthesis of a finite, temporal, and
necessary aspect as well as an infinite, eternal, and free aspect of one and
the same being. But that is not all. Because this synthesis is capable of
“relating to” itself, it is thus a self-relating synthesis of these contrasting
aspects—that is, the self is a “relation.” Furthermore, the self-as-self-
relating-synthesis is not an entity but a capacity: the self is “that the relation
relates to itself.” As a capacity, a possibility, a potential, the self is perhaps
better understood as a verb than as a noun. But that is still not all, because,
as Anti-Climacus informs us, there is one additional, and indispensable,
level to the self, and this additional level involves an obligation: as a part of
its relating to itself, i.e., its consciousness of itself, the self must recognize
that it has been “established by another,” that is, by what we call God, and
that, in order to be healthy and complete, the self must “relate to itself” and
“will to be itself” in such a manner that, in so doing, the self is “grounded
transparently in the power that established it,” that the self consciously rests
in God.

The self, then, is an activity and an obligation, that is, a task, and, at
various points in the book, Anti-Climacus specifically refers to the self as
such:

The self is the conscious synthesis of infinitude and finitude that
relates to itself, whose task is to become itself, which can only be
done in the relation to God, [and thus become] entirely present and
simultaneous . . . with itself in that small portion of the task that can
be accomplished right now, so that in being infinitized, it in the
strictest sense comes back to itself, so that in being farthest away
from itself (when it is most infinitized in purpose and decision) it is
at that same instant nearest itself in accomplishing that infinitely
small portion of the work which can be accomplished even today,
even at this hour, even at this instant.!

If a self fails to fulfill its obligation to become itself, this is owing to
“despair.” This despair can be either that of “weakness” or that of
“defiance.” There are varying levels of “weak” despair, which Anti-

Climacus presents to us in ascending order as the level of consciousness
increases. When the despairing self becomes conscious of its existence
“before God,” as happens halfway through Anti-Climacus’s schematic
description of despair, the self—and the depiction of despair—undergoes a
qualitative transformation, entering into the realm of specifically Christian
definitions, and despair is now defined as “sin.”

In this way, Kierkegaard’s pseudonym presents us with an anthropology
in which a human being’s defining characteristic is “spirit”—not body, or
mind (“psyche”). The spirit is a capacity and therefore always a potential
activity, while the mind and the body are things, givens. “Spirit”—which is
the defining element of the “self”—is also a “given,” but in a quite different
sense: it is a God-given capacity that can be either accepted or stifled, either
by being ignored (i.e., in “weakness”) or by being rejected (i.e., in
“defiance”). The Sickness unto Death, then, is not only a dense, laconic,
schematic—Anti-Climacus calls it “algebraic”—argument but also a
dramatic “medical” history of the course of a sickness, a continuing
crescendo culminating, as all medical histories do, in a “crisis,” a turning
point, at which the patient, the self, opts for salvation or extinction.

On the title page, The Sickness unto Death is described as “A Christian
Psychological Exposition for Edification and Awakening.” The book, then,
is “psychological,” and to that extent, a neutral, objective exposition, but it
is also “for edification and awakening,” and its tenor and intention are
consequently by no means neutral or objective, but are instead intended to
“awaken,” and thus, to alarm and strengthen (“edify”) the reader as a
subject. Accordingly, Anti-Climacus does not shy away from using
straightforwardly Christian terminology in explaining that a person’s failure
to accept and become the full possibility of the self—whether through
weakness or defiance—constitutes sin. This is also why Anti-Climacus
repeatedly emphasizes that in Christianity, unlike the pagan world of
classical antiquity, “the opposite of sin is not virtue, but faith.”!
Furthermore, as Anti-Climacus insists:

The opposition: sin—faith is the Christian one which refashions all
ethical concepts in a Christian manner, providing them with an
additional extension of their range. This opposition rests upon the
decisive Christian stipulation “before God,” a determinant which in

turn includes the decisive Christian criterion: the absurd, the
paradox, the possibility of offense. ... Where, then, is the
possibility of offense in this? It is that a human being should have
this reality: the reality of existing, as an individual human being,
directly before God, and therefore, in turn, what follows from this:
that a human being’s sin should be of concern to God.

Thus, as he approaches the climax of his exposition, Anti-Climacus
repeatedly points out that the greatest danger confronting the individual—
who has made his way through the levels of sickness charted in his
“algebraic” schema, who has arrived at the decisive turning point, and who
is thus in critical condition—is the risk or possibility of “offense.” That the
infinite, almighty God should take such an interest in an individual human
being seems, to a human being, to make no sense at all; it seems absurd, a
paradox, a mockery, something at which one might take offense. Therefore,
as Anti-Climacus explains, if indeed God is truly to approach a person,
“there is an infinite qualitative difference between God and human beings
[that] constitutes the possibility of offense, which cannot be taken away.”
Human beings are always in danger of being “offended” because of the
absurd and “paradoxical” fact that “out of love, God becomes a human
being [and] assumes the form of a lowly servant,” who then says to us:

“Look here, .. . and learn with certainty what it is to be a human
being; ah, but take care, for I am also God—blessed the person who
is not offended in me... . 1 am this particular, lowly human being,
poor, forsaken, delivered into the hands of human beings—blessed
the person who is not offended in me. I, this lowly human being, am
the one who makes the deaf hear, the blind see, the lame walk, who
cleanses lepers, who causes the dead to arise—blessed the person
who is not offended in me.”!

Thus, “the possibility of offense” is always present when God, in love,
attempts to approach a human being on the human level. And as we have
seen, this possibility “cannot be taken away.” There is a kind of symmetry
here: when God approaches a human being on the human level, the
difference between God and humanity is so great that there is always a

possibility that a person can feel that they are being made a fool of, and for
that reason can abandon and denounce Christianity “modo ponendo”
(definitely, positively). This is “the sin against the Holy Spirit,” the apex of
despair, the end of the line, the point at which “the sickness unto death”
definitively ends with the death of the spirit. The alternative, according to
Anti-Climacus, is simply “faith,” an acceptance that God is so loving and
merciful that he can and has become a human being.

Kierkegaard’s argument here makes it possible for the reader to grasp
the point he made in his journal about a year after the publication of The
Sickness unto Death: “Truly, the difficulty is not, when absolutely feeling
one’s wretchedness, to grasp the consolation of Christianity. . . . No, the
difficulty is to become so miserable as to want to risk discovering one’s
own wretchedness. To be made healthy by the aid of Christianity is not the
difficulty; the difficulty is to become truly and thoroughly sick.”

The Sickness unto Death is Anti-Climacus’s compact, systematic
—“algebraic”—treatise on the subjects of sin and faith, but it is also a book
by Sgren Kierkegaard, and like everything (or almost everything) he wrote,
it should also be understood in the context of his time and place, mid-
nineteenth-century Europe, and Denmark in particular. It does not require a
great deal of digging for a reader to discover in The Sickness unto Death a
continuation of Kierkegaard’s polemic, begun in Fear and Trembling,
against what he perceived as the shallow self-satisfaction of his times. In
the eighteenth century, Denmark, like much of Western Europe, had
experienced significant economic growth and a corresponding growth of
confidence in human reason, a development known as the Enlightenment, a
label that the age, not insignificantly, applied to itself. It stuck, and the
nineteenth century, with its extraordinary progress in science, engineering,
and—as many thought—in social and political arrangements, was widely
regarded as a continuation of the Enlightenment. In the course of a few
decades, enormous advances in such scientific disciplines as chemistry,
physics, mathematics, astronomy, and medicine were paralleled by what
seemed like an instantaneous transformation of everyday life: railroads and

steamships radically altered previous notions of distance; the telegraph
made communication, which previously had taken days or weeks, virtually
immediate; and it was a widely held belief that this sort of “progress” also
applied to the social and political world of human beings—we were getting
better and better.

Kierkegaard was more than merely skeptical about the appellation
“Enlightenment.” He made this clear in a journal entry from late in 1854, in
which he mocks his “enlightened” age for the hypocritical manner in which
it trivializes religion:

The Enlightened Nineteenth Century

Fundamentally, this is, after all, awfully pitiful: For most people
Christianity is really mythology, fable, poetry, they don’t consider it
to be anything else—and then, in addition, it is their religion! That is
to say that they don’t in fact have the courage to renounce it, that
with respect to the most important matter, they don’t have enough
energy to break with it in order either to go without religion
altogether or to look for another.

This is the state of affairs in the enlightened nineteenth century
—because the number of those who are in one sense honest, the
declared freethinkers, is in no way greater in our time than in an
earlier period.

The enlightened nineteenth century—well, yes, gas lighting is in
fact also an invention of our time, and it is doubtless from this that
the century gets its name. For this business with Christianity is
really the most pitiful possible. Not the grossest superstition, if it at
least fulfills these people’s lives and is what they believe, not the
most obdurate unbelief, if it at least has character, is anything in
comparison to this pitifulness: that something a person himself
regards as fable is also that person’s religion. They have their
children baptized, instructed in Christianity, confirmed; when they
themselves are to die they have the priest called, want to be buried
as Christians—and, in addition to this, they regard Christianity,
fundamentally, as fable.!

Clearly, then, in addition to being a tightly argued and systematically
presented “exposition” concerning the structure of the self and the nature
and origin of sin, The Sickness unto Death is also a critique of
Kierkegaard’s modern, “enlightened” times and of self-satisfied “Christian
society”—or, as Anti-Climacus frequently calls it, “Christendom,” which he
contrasts with pre-Christian classical civilization, or “paganism.” Indeed,
Anti-Climacus contends, from a spiritual point of view, modern
Christendom—that is, “the paganism within Christendom”—is even further
from “being spirit” (i.e., from affirmatively embracing the possibility of
being the self a person has been given by God) than was classical paganism,
inasmuch as “paganism indeed lacks spirit, but nonetheless is oriented in
the direction of spirit, whereas paganism within Christendom lacks spirit in
the direction away from spirit, or by having fallen away from it, and is
therefore spiritlessness in the strictest sense.”! This is a point to which Anti-
Climacus returns repeatedly in his treatise, as Kierkegaard does in his
published writings and very frequently in his journals.

In discussing the social forms in which “despair’—i.e., the sickness of
sin—presents, Anti-Climacus explains that despair stems either from an
absence of necessity, so that one becomes enmeshed in fantasy, and thus
everything is possible and “nothing becomes actual”!—or from an absence
of possibility, so that a person becomes a determinist or fatalist, for whom
“everything has become necessity,” and thus nothing is possible. These
two examples could be said to represent unusual, “strong” forms of despair,
and are useful extremes in the explanatory model Anti-Climacus constructs.
But the most common form of despair was typified in a figure quite
recognizable in Kierkegaard’s Copenhagen, the “bourgeois philistine”:! a
cautious, complacent, generally self-satisfied person, who avoids taking
risks by going to the extreme of neither the fantast nor the fatalist, and does
his best to remain optimistic—though, of course, cautiously so. Unlike the
extreme positions occupied by the others, bourgeois philistinism (which in
Anti-Climacus’s presentation serves as a sort of stand-in or representative
for “enlightened” Danish society, and thus for “Christendom” generally):

believes it has possibility at its disposal, believes that it has fooled
that enormous elasticity into the trap or madhouse of probability,

believes that it holds it captive. It leads possibility about, captive in
the cage of probability, exhibits it, imagining itself to be the master,
not noticing that precisely by doing so it has imprisoned itself as the
slave of spiritlessness and is the most wretched of all. For the person
who has become lost in possibility soars with the daring of despair;
the person for whom everything became necessary overreaches
himself in life, crushed in despair; but bourgeois philistinism,
devoid of spirit, triumphs.'

It is also important to note that The Sickness unto Death was written
under the immediate influence of the popular unrest associated with the
various European revolutions of 1848, and especially, of course, the
relatively peaceful abolition of absolute monarchy in Denmark that took
place in March of that year. In much of Europe, the spring and early
summer of 1848 was characterized by popular uprisings that called for
democratic, constitutional government in place of the various forms of
absolute monarchy then prevalent. Denmark was no exception to this trend,
and the Danish monarchy was further riven by the insistence of political
activists, in the predominantly German-speaking southern duchies of
Schleswig and Holstein, that the duchies be permitted to break away from
Denmark and become part of a nascent confederacy of German states.
Throughout the rest of Denmark, however, Danish nationalist sentiment and
political liberalism held sway, exercising a particularly powerful hold on the
middle and upper-middle classes in the capital city of Copenhagen. And so
it came to pass that on March 21, 1848, nearly twenty thousand people, led
by prominent members of Copenhagen’s bourgeoisie, marched upon the
government palace and presented their demands both that the government
convene an assembly charged with drawing up a constitution based on
popular sovereignty and that the government refuse to give in to the
secessionists in the southern duchies. The king, Frederick VII, who had
succeeded to the throne only two months earlier and was not a particularly
strong-willed man, quickly yielded to the demands of the liberal nationalist
politicians. The result was war with the German-speaking secessionists, and
the promise of a convention to write a democratic constitution for the
monarchy.

Just over a year later, on June 5, 1849, in the midst of continuing war
with various German states and the breakaway provinces, a democratic
Danish constitution was ratified. The June Constitution, as it became
known, established Denmark as a constitutional monarchy governed by a
parliament elected by what then may have been the most inclusive suffrage
in the world. One of the hallmarks of the new constitution was that it
retained an established national church. The state, formerly an absolute
monarchy, was now subject to popular sovereignty and, accordingly, the
established church, which had hitherto been known as “the State Church,”
was now written into the constitution as “the Danish People’s Church” and
was indeed the property of the people, subject to the vagaries of shifting
majorities in the parliament. Under this new constitution, the parliament
established ministries to oversee various state activities. One such ministry
was the “Cultus Ministry,” which was responsible for the affairs of all
manner of cultural institutions, including primary and secondary education,
the University of Copenhagen, the museums, the zoo, the Royal Theater,
and the Danish People’s Church. In a journal entry from October 1849,
Kierkegaard remarks archly that “people have taken exception to the fact
that the theater has been placed under the cultus minister; I think it would
be better if the cultus minister kept control over what has been entrusted to
him—the theater and the Church—but took the title of theater director.”!

So the old State Church that had existed since the Reformation was now
the Danish People’s Church. There was no change in personnel or policy
aside from the fact that the established church was now under the direction
of a cabinet minister who was ultimately responsible to a popularly elected
parliament. Kierkegaard regarded this as the worst possible outcome, a
superficial, cultural Christianity that was part of Danish national identity, a
Christianity that was the possession of the Danish people and ultimately
subject to the will of a parliament elected by what was—for the time—a
broadly democratic franchise. With the Danish People’s Church safely
ensconced in the constitution, the actual day-to-day life of official Danish
Christianity was administered by an academically educated clergy that, in
Kierkegaard’s view, continued to live well, or comfortably, on their
guaranteed positions and to be willing—as they had been under the old

absolutist State Church—to console and condescend to those who had been
entrusted to their care.

In a journal entry from late May 1849, approximately a month before
the manuscript of The Sickness unto Death went to press, Kierkegaard
makes it clear that his particular target is the fact that Christianity has been
transformed into “priestly chatter about a heavenly friend, the gentle
doctrine of truth, the satisfying of deep longings, depth itself, and other
such saccharine stuff, which the silk-clad priests serve up to their silk-clad
listeners.”! Kierkegaard much preferred “freethinkers,” that is, outright
atheists, to the watered-down official religion he saw as characteristic of the
Danish Church. He dwells on this topic at length in a journal entry from the
late summer of 1849:

That the “Christian State” Is Based on an Untruth, Not to Mention
a Lie, Illustrated by the Existence of Bishop Mynster

The “Christian state” is of course one in which all the subjects are
Christians, or at least where the general run of them do not publicly
confess another religion. When this is the case, there naturally
emerges a firm concept of the clergy, who become civil servants just
like secular civil servants, and some of them will become very lofty
and distinguished as well as well-paid.

When this is the case: for if it is not the case—if neither all of
the subjects nor more or less the general run of them are Christian—
then the Christian Church will of course be a small or a minor
society in the state, and being a Christian priest would be neither a
civil service position nor particularly profitable, in short, not an
occupation for ambitious people.

Now take Bishop Mynster. In order to be this distinguished and
to be so qua clergyman, there must be a Christian state; his entire
existence qua distinguished clergyman supports the concept of the
“Christian state” on which it itself rests. But how, in fact, does this
man live? Or what does his life express existentially? How has it
been arranged and edited from its earliest days? What his life
expresses is: For the sake of God in Heaven, let us not involve

ourselves with the mass of people (in the Christian state, where of
course everyone is a Christian); let us live at the greatest possible
remove, and with the greatest possible distinction, from the average
run of people. We preach on Sundays, but of course in the splendid
house of God, secured by all possible distinction: “Out there—no, I
must take care not to preach there.” . ..

He profits from the illusion that this is a Christian state (the
profit is to be bishop, the supreme civil servant, having offices and
other delights within his gift, great revenues), and existentially he
expresses that the great mass of people are as far as possible from
being Christians, but are crude and ignorant rabble.

Look, this is the Christian state. What is true here of Bishop
Mynster is true with respect to more or less every clergyman in the
capital.

There is no doubt that The Sickness unto Death is, in addition to
everything else, very much a political book, conceived by Kierkegaard as a
response both to the general tenor of his times—with his contemporaries’
view of themselves as “enlightened”—and to the specific political and
ecclesiastical upheavals that culminated in 1848-1849. So, although
Kierkegaard does not mention Bishop Jacob Peter Mynster by name in the
text of The Sickness unto Death, there can be no doubt that it is Mynster to
whom Anti-Climacus is referring when he writes:

Ah, when one sees a person who gives assurances that he has
understood completely how Christ went about in the form of a lowly
servant, poor, despised, mocked, as Scripture says: spat upon—
when I then see that same person so painstakingly seek out the place
where, in the worldly sense, it is pleasant to be, and ensconce
himself there in complete security, when I see him so anxiously—as
if it were a matter of life and death—avoiding every unfavorable
gust of wind from the right or left, so happy, so extremely happy, so
madly happy—indeed, to top it off, so madly happy that, profoundly
moved, he even thanks God for it—for being unreservedly honored
and esteemed by everyone, everyone.!

Nonetheless, while he does not shy away from criticizing “Christendom,”
with its hired clergy and its “bourgeois philistine” adherents, Kierkegaard
also targets himself. In his journals from the period preceding and
immediately following the publication of The Sickness unto Death, he
makes it clear that as a spectator and critical analyst of his social and
religious environment, he is nonetheless part of the problem—a “poet,”
rather than someone who takes action. In a journal entry written during the
first half of July 1849, when The Sickness unto Death was in press,
Kierkegaard notes: “At first I thought of adding a postscript [to The
Sickness unto Death] by the editor [i.e., Kierkegaard himself]. But... one
can see that I myself am already present in the book, e.g., the part about the
poet of the religious.”! Several months later, in mid-November 1849,
Kierkegaard again took up the matter in his journal, noting that in The
Sickness unto Death, “perhaps there ought to have been a tiny little
postscript by the editor as originally intended, e.g., Editor ’s Postscript. This
book is as if written by a physician; I, the editor, am not the physician, I am
one of the sick.” (Subsequently, in the period between publication of The
Sickness unto Death and the furious attack on “Christendom” that
Kierkegaard waged from 1854 until his death in the autumn of 1855,
Kierkegaard made what he understood to be a move from writing “poetry”
to taking concrete action.)

As can be seen from its table of contents, The Sickness unto Death,
despite its relative brevity, is divided, subdivided, sub-subdivided, etc. into
a great many sections, carefully arranged in a complex hierarchy and
sequence. The overall architecture of the book is clear: it is divided into two
“Parts,” with the first “Part” arguing or demonstrating that “the sickness
unto death is despair,” and the second “Part” extending the demonstration,
arguing that “despair is sin.” There is therefore a major division between
the First Part and the Second Part, and it is clear to the reader that despite
this discontinuity, there is nonetheless an internal continuity insofar as Anti-
Climacus, the putative author, suddenly displays what looks like a curiously
personal-sounding concern with the problem of an individual who is
plagued by a “thorn in the flesh,” which torments him and which, at the

same time, the individual cannot—or does not want to—be rid of. Of
course, this mysterious, embedded suffering recalls that of St. Paul, but we
also know that such suffering—and this particular term for it—recurs at
many points in Kierkegaard’s writings, both published and unpublished. In
one of the journal entries cited above, Kierkegaard is quite open about
admitting that he himself is “present in the book” in “the part about the poet
of the religious,” which is found at the very beginning of the Second Part.
As we will see, right at the beginning of the Second Part (in which the
reader enters into specifically Christian territory where the category of “sin’
is present), Anti-Climacus launches into a discussion of “the poet of the
religious” in which he grapples with the problem of the thorn in the flesh.
However, it is equally interesting to note that a vigorous discussion of
someone with a “thorn in the flesh” is also found immediately preceding
this passage, at the very end of the First Part (which concerns itself not with
sin, but with the general category of “despair”). Thus the bridge between
the book’s two major divisions is its discussion of a person who clings to
his “thorn in the flesh.” Kierkegaard has identified himself with the latter
portion of this discussion, and it is difficult to see how the former portion is
not to be read in a similarly autobiographical manner.

What follows is an abbreviated excerpt from the concluding portion of
the First Part of The Sickness unto Death:

bd

Perhaps, when it is tentatively orienting itself in its concrete self, an
experimenting self such as this, which in despair wills to be itself,
runs into one or another difficulty, something that the Christian
would call a cross, a fundamental impairment, whatever it might
be... . [I]Jn Promethean fashion, the infinite, negative self feels itself
to be nailed fast to this servitude. . . . [It is a] form of despair that
despairs over the earthly or over something earthly. . . . But it is also
a form of despair to be unwilling to hope in the possibility that an
earthly suffering, a temporal cross, can be taken away. Now, this is
something this despairing person, who in despair wills to be himself,
is unwilling to do. If he has convinced himself that this thorn in the
flesh (whether it is actually present or his passion makes it seem to
him that it is) gnaws so deeply that he is unable to abstract from it,
then he wills, as it were, to make it a part of himself for eternity. So

he is offended by it, or rather, he lets it serve as the occasion to be
offended at the whole of existence: then he wills to be himself in
spite of it, not be himself in spite of it without it (that, of course,
would be to abstract from it, and that he cannot do—or it would be
movement in the direction of resignation): no, in spite of, or in
defiance of, all of existence, he wills to be himself along with it, he
wills to take it along with himself, almost defying his agony.’

This is how the First Part of The Sickness unto Death ends, with its
ultimate development of the concept of despair. Then, immediately
thereafter, comes the Second Part, which makes the case that despair is sin,
thus taking the reader into the specifically Christian part of the
“exposition.” In the Second Part, despite its clear separation from the part
that preceded it, Anti-Climacus begins with a continuation of what seems
clearly to be Kierkegaard’s autobiographical theme, depicting “a poet-
existence tending toward the religious,” a person who refuses to let himself
be healed.

Even though the present part, and least of all section A, is not the
place for psychological sketches (nor is there room for such),
mention may nonetheless be made here of the most dialectical
boundary area between despair and sin—of what could be called a
poet-existence tending toward the religious. ... A poet of this sort
can have a very deep religious craving, and the conception of God is
incorporated in his despair. He loves God above everything, God
who is his only consolation in his secret agony, and yet he loves the
agony—he will not let go of it... . As a person unhappy in love
became a poet blissfully praising the happiness of love, he became
the poet of religiousness. . . . In relation to the religious he is an
unhappy lover; that is, he is not a believer in strict sense; he
possesses only the first part of faith: despair—and in it a fervent
longing for the religious. His conflict is really this: Has he been
called? Does the thorn in the flesh mean that he is to be used for the
extraordinary? Before God, is it entirely in order that he has become
this extraordinary person? Or is the thorn in the flesh something

under which he is to humble himself in order to attain what is
universally human?!

Thus, the autobiographical element quite visibly and deliberately bridges
the divide between the First and Second Parts. Kierkegaard’s argument
concerning the nature and tenacity of the despair that is sin is clearly
intended to apply to everyone, including the author himself, who, as we
have seen, appropriately “positioned [him]self above J[ohannes |
C[limacus], below Anti-C[limacus].”

Kierkegaard’s The Sickness unto Death is tough, systematic, and brilliant in
its rigorous investigation of the human soul. It spares no one, and certainly
not its author. It presents God as love, as what T. S. Eliot terms an
“infinitely gentle, infinitely suffering thing,” yet it is also a love that is
rigorous and demanding, a love that sees through all efforts at deception,
especially—and worst of all—self-deception. The Sickness unto Death
presents a Christianity stripped of all the historical accretions and emotional
trappings that Kierkegaard believed had hobbled it and pressed it into the
service of the “bourgeois philistines” of Danish “Christendom,” and that
have likewise turned it into fodder for the dangerous “evangelical”
hucksterism of present-day political life. And so The Sickness unto Death
speaks to us, even today.

TRANSLATOR'S NOTE

“THE BOOK has a difficulty: it is too dialectical and harsh to make proper use
of the rhetorical, the engaging, the enthralling.”! This is how Kierkegaard
described The Sickness unto Death when he was working on it in the spring
of 1848, and he was right. The reader has to do a bit of searching to find
“engaging” and “enthralling” passages in The Sickness unto Death—unlike,
say, in Fear and Trembling, which Kierkegaard had published six years
earlier. Indeed, by “rhetorical,” Kierkegaard meant precisely the sort of
lyrical writing that characterizes much of Fear and Trembling and his many
collections of edifying discourses. By “dialectical,” on the other hand,
Kierkegaard meant a systematic, organized argument (Kierkegaard’s
pseudonym Anti-Climacus calls it an “exposition”) like that which
comprises The Sickness unto Death—see, for example, the extremely
detailed “Contents” section that in the original Danish edition was placed
directly following Kierkegaard’s “Preface” (in the present translation, that
“Contents” section is on page 7, following the Foreword). Even a brief
inspection of the “Contents” section, with its complex hierarchy of
divisions and subdivisions, lays bare what Kierkegaard termed the
“algebraic” organization and compression that he imposed upon his
presentation. (So complex, in fact, was the plan of Kierkegaard’s argument
that at a few points he seems to lose his otherwise perfect overview of the
whole, and a sharp-eyed reader will detect places where the internal
headings that appear in the body of Anti-Climacus’s argument don’t quite
match up with the wording assigned to them in the Contents.)

As is often the case in Kierkegaard’s writing, it is not unusual to find
sentences that are quite long and that seem convoluted, or even contorted,
in their attempt to present the various points of his argument. This confronts
the translator with a difficult choice: Should such sentences be broken up

into several shorter sentences? Or should the length of such sentences be
preserved while their syntax and structure are adjusted in order to convey
Kierkegaard’s meaning while retaining the overall feel of his exposition? In
translating “rhetorical” works, I have often chosen to break up
Kierkegaard’s extraordinarily long sentences, but in the present case, with a
book as “dialectical” as The Sickness unto Death, I have generally chosen to
allow lengthy sentences to remain lengthy, and have attempted to preserve
their meaning and the flow of Kierkegaard’s exposition by adjusting his
nineteenth-century Danish syntax to the conventions of modern English
usage.

In presenting his case, Kierkegaard makes frequent use of the Danish
noun Bestemmelse (cognate of the German Bestimmung). Along with its
verbal, adjectival, and adverbial forms, Bestemmelse appears in this little
book about a hundred times. Its polysemy presents a unique challenge to the
translator, offering them a welter of possible meanings and translations to
choose from. The term is typically concerned with definition and
categorization, though it can also mean “destination” and quite a number of
other things—for example, “classification,” “specification,” “qualification,”
“determination,” “aspect,” “characterization,” and their various related
forms—and Kierkegaard’s penchant for using the term was perhaps owing
to its very malleability. Another term that appears with great frequency is
Begreb (cognate of the German Begriff), though this term is relatively
unproblematic and in the present volume has most often been translated as
“concept,” while its verb forms have generally been rendered as forms of
“comprehend” and occasionally as forms of “grasp.” Other specialized
terms preferred by Kierkegaard present fewer problems. Unsurprisingly,
“speculation” and “speculative,” which Kierkegaard tends to use in a
negative or pejorative sense, refer to Hegel and Hegel-inspired philosophy
and theology.

FOREWORD

Lhe form of this “exposition” will perhaps strike many people as curious;

it will seem to them too rigorous to be edifying, and too edifying to be
strictly scholarly. With respect to this latter point, I have no opinion. As for
the former point, that is not my view of it, and if it were in fact too rigorous
to be edifying, then, in my view, that would be a flaw. For of course, it is
one thing that it cannot be edifying for everyone, because not everyone
possesses the prerequisites needed in order to be able to follow it; that it has
the character of being edifying is another matter. Indeed, from the Christian
point of view everything, everything ought to serve for edification.'
Scholarliness of the sort that is not, ultimately, edifying is for that very
reason precisely unchristian. In its presentation, everything Christian ought
to be like the physician’s words beside the sickbed; even if they are
understood only by those with medical training, it nonetheless ought never
be forgotten that they are spoken beside the sickbed. This is the relation of
Christianity to life (as opposed to a scholarly remove from life), or this, the
ethical aspect of Christianity, is precisely what is edifying, and the manner
of its presentation, however rigorous it might otherwise be, is entirely
different, qualitatively different, from the sort of scholarliness that is
“indifferent,” the scholarliness whose lofty heroism is, from a Christian
point of view, so far removed from being heroism that, from a Christian
point of view, it is a species of inhuman curiosity. It is Christian heroism—
and indeed, it is perhaps seen quite rarely—to dare to be oneself entirely, an
individual human being, this particular individual human being, alone
before God, alone in that enormous effort and that enormous responsibility;

but it is not Christian heroism to be fooled by the abstract notion of pure
humanity in itself, or to play “Guess Who?” with world history.! However
rigorous its form is in other respects, all Christian knowledge ought to be
concerned; but this concern is precisely what edifies. The concern is the
relation to life, to the actuality of the personality, and is thus, from the
Christian point of view, seriousness; from the Christian point of view, the #
[118]# loftiness of indifferent knowledge is far from being greater
seriousness—it is, from the Christian point of view, jest and vanity. But,
again, seriousness is what edifies.

Therefore, in one sense, this little book is of the sort that could have
been written by a college undergraduate, though in another sense, perhaps
not every professor could write it.

But the fact that the treatise appears in this form is at least evidence of
its having been well thought through, and it is surely correct from a
psychological standpoint as well. There is a more solemn style that is so
solemn that it does not mean very much and which, when a person is
altogether too accustomed to it, easily ends up saying nothing at all.

Just one additional remark, most likely superfluous, but I am
nonetheless willing to take that upon myself: I want to point out, once and
for all, that, as the title of course indicates, throughout this entire work,
despair is understood as the sickness, not as the means of recovery. Indeed,
that is how dialectical despair is. Thus, too, in Christian terminology death
is the expression for the greatest spiritual misery, and yet the cure is
precisely to die, to die away.”

merely human terms, not only is there life, but this life in the greatest of

Thus, as Christianity views it, not even death is “the sickness unto
death”—even less, then, all these things that are called earthly and temporal
suffering: want, sickness, misery, distress, difficulties, suffering, mental
affliction, sorrow, grief. And even if such things were so burdensome and
painful that we human beings, or at any rate, that the suffering person, was
to say, “this is worse than death”—for the Christian understanding, all such
things (which, even though they are not sickness, can be compared with a
sickness) are nonetheless not the sickness unto death.

That is how nobly Christianity has taught the Christian to think of
everything earthly and worldly, death included. It is almost as though the
Christian might feel superior because of this proud elevation above
everything that people otherwise call misfortune, above what people
otherwise call the greatest evil. But then Christianity has in turn discovered
a misery which the human being, as such, does not know exists; this misery
is the sickness unto death. The things which the natural human being
reckons as appalling—when he has gone through them all and knows of no
more he can name: for the Christian, they are like a joke. Such is the
relation of the natural human being to the Christian—it is like the relation
of a child to a man: what the child shrinks from in terror is viewed as
nothing by the man. The child does not know what is horrifying; the man
does know, and he is appalled by it. The child’s imperfection is, first, not
knowing what is horrifying, and then, as a part of this, shrinking from #
[125]# what is not horrifying. And thus, too, with the natural human being:
he is ignorant of what is truly horrifying, and yet he is not thereby freed
from shuddering—no, he shudders at what is not horrifying. It is like the
pagan’s relation to God: he does not know the true God—but as if that
weren’t enough: he worships an idol as God.

Only the Christian knows what is meant by the sickness unto death. As
a Christian, he has been the recipient of a courage that the natural human

being does not know—he acquired this courage by learning to fear what is

even more horrifying. A person always acquires courage in this way: when
one fears a greater danger, a person always has courage to confront a lesser
one—when a person infinitely fears one danger, it is as if the others did not
exist. But the horrifying thing that the Christian has learned to know is “the
sickness unto death.”

The Sickness unto Death Is Despair

#[129]#

A

THAT DESPAIR IS THE SICKNESS UNTO
DEATH

A

Despair is a sickness in the spirit, in the self, and can
thus be threefold: in despair not to be conscious of
having a self (not properly despair); in despair not to
will to be oneself; in despair to will to be oneself

A HUMAN BEING IS spirit. But what is spirit? Spirit is the self. But what is the
self? The self is a relation that relates to itself, or it is that in the relation
which is the relation’s relating to itself. The self is not the relation but is
that the relation relates to itself. A human being is a synthesis of the infinite
and the finite, of the temporal and the eternal, of freedom and necessity, in
short, a synthesis. A synthesis is a relation between two terms. Considered
in this way, a human being is not yet a self.

In a relation between two, the relation is the third as a negative unity,
and the two relate to the relation, and in the relation to the relation; thus,
under the aspect of psyche, the relation between psyche and body is a
relation. On the other hand, if the relation relates to itself, then this relation
is the positive third, and this is the self.

Such a relation that relates to itself, a self, must either have established
itself or have been established! by another.

If the relation that relates to itself has been established by another, then
the relation is surely the third, but this relation, the third, is in turn
nonetheless a relation, relating to that which has established the entire
relation. #[130]#

Such a derived, established relation is the human self, a relation that
relates to itself and, in relating to itself, relates to another. This is the reason
that there can be two forms of genuine despair. If the human self had
established itself, there could be talk of only one form, that of not willing to
be oneself, of willing to be rid of oneself, but there could not be talk of the
form: in despair to will to be oneself. This formula is in fact the expression
for the relation’s (the self’s) total dependence, the expression of the fact that
the self cannot of itself come to or be in equilibrium and rest, but only, in
relating to itself, by relating to that which has established the entire relation.
Indeed, so far is it from being the case that this second form of despair (in
despair to will to be oneself) merely designates a specific form of despair,
that, on the contrary, all despair can in the end be resolved into it and traced
back to it. If someone in despair is (as he believes) attentive to his despair,
he does not speak meaninglessly of it as something that happens to him
(more or less as when, owing to a nervous delusion, a person who suffers
from vertigo speaks of a weight on his head or that it is as if something had
fallen onto him, etc., a weight or pressure which, however, is not something
external, but an inverted reflection of something internal)—and then, on his
own and solely by himself, wants to abolish the despair using all his might:
then he is still in despair and with all his supposed efforts is merely working
himself all the more deeply into a deeper despair. The misrelation of despair
is not a simple misrelation, but a misrelation in a relation that relates to
itself and that is established by an other, so that the misrelation in this for-
itself relation also reflects itself infinitely in the relation to the power that
established it.

This, then, is the formula that describes the state of the self when
despair is entirely eradicated: in relating to itself, and in willing to be itself,
the self is grounded transparently in the power that established it.

B

The Possibility and Actuality of Despair

Is despair an advantage or a defect? From a purely dialectical standpoint, it
is both. If one wanted to grasp the abstract idea of despair without thinking
of any despairing person, one would have to say that it is an enormous #
[131]# advantage. The possibility of this sickness is the human being’s
advantage over the animal, and this advantage distinguishes him in a
manner quite different from the upright gait,* for it indicates the infinite
uprightness or elevation of his being spirit. The possibility of this sickness
is the human being’s advantage over the animal; to be aware of this sickness
is the Christian’s advantage over the natural human being; to be cured of
this sickness is the Christian’s blessedness.

Thus, to be able to despair is an infinite advantage, and yet to be in
despair is not merely the greatest misfortune and misery—no, it is perdition.
The relation between possibility and actuality is not like this in other
situations: if it is an advantage to be able to be something, then it is an even
greater advantage to be it—to be something is like an ascent in relation to
being able to be something. With despair, on the other hand, being in
despair is like a fall in relation to the possibility of being in despair: as
infinite as is the advantage of the possibility of being in despair, the fall of
being in despair is equally deep. Thus, in relation to despair, not being in it
is an ascent. Yet here, once again, this category is ambiguous. Not being in
despair is not like not being lame, blind, or the like. If not being in despair
means neither more nor less than not being in despair, then it means
precisely to be in it. Not being in despair must mean the annihilated
possibility of being able to be in despair; if this is true, then in order for a
person not to be in despair he must annihilate despair at every moment.
This is not how possibility relates to actuality in other circumstances. For it
is true that thinkers say that actuality is annihilated possibility, but that is
not entirely true: it is the fulfilled, the active possibility. In this case, on the
contrary, it is the actuality (not being in despair) which is therefore also a
negation, the impotent, annihilated possibility; in other cases, actuality
relates to possibility as a confirmation—here it is a denial.

Despair is a misrelation in the relation of a synthesis that relates to
itself. But the synthesis is not the misrelation, it is only the possibility—or,
the possibility of the misrelation lies in the synthesis. Were the synthesis
itself the misrelation, then despair would not exist at all, then despair would
be something that was present in human nature as such—that is, then it
would not be despair; it would be something that happened to a person,
something he suffered, like an illness a person falls into, or like death, #
[132]# which is everyone’s lot. No, despairing lies in the person himself,
but were he not a synthesis, he would be absolutely unable to despair; nor
could he despair had the synthesis not originally come from God’s hand in
the right relationship.

Where, then, does despair come from? From the relation in which the
synthesis relates to itself, inasmuch as God, who made the human being
into this relation, lets him, as it were, slip out of his hand—that is, from the
relation’s relating to itself. And in this—in the fact that the relation is spirit,
that it is the self—in this lies the responsibility which rests upon all despair,
responsibility which rests there for every moment it exists, however much
and however ingeniously and deceptively the despairing person deceives
himself and others, speaking of his despair as a misfortune—confusing it
with the aforementioned case of vertigo, with which despair, though
qualitatively different, has much in common, for, in connection with the
category of psyche, vertigo has much in common with what despair is in
connection with the category of spirit, and it is teeming with analogies to
despair.

Then, when the misrelation, despair, has arisen, does it follow as a
matter of course that it continues? No, it does not follow as a matter of
course; if the misrelation continues, it is not a consequence of the
misrelation, but of the relation that relates to itself. This means that every
time the misrelation manifests itself, and at every moment it exists, it must
be followed back to the relation. For instance, it is said that a person brings
a sickness upon himself, e.g., through carelessness. Then the sickness sets
in, and from that moment it asserts itself and is now an actuality whose
origin increasingly becomes something in the past. It would be both terrible
and inhuman if someone were incessantly to say: “At this moment you, the
sick person, are bringing this sickness upon yourself”—that is, if at every
moment one were to dissolve the actuality of the sickness into its

possibility. It is true that he brought the sickness upon himself, but he did
that only once; the continuation of the sickness is a simple consequence of
the fact that at one time he brought it upon himself; its progress cannot at
every moment be referred back to him as its cause; he brought it upon
himself, but one cannot say that he is bringing it upon himself. It is
otherwise with despair: every actual moment of despair can be traced back
to possibility. Every moment he is in despair he is bringing it upon himself:
it is always the present tense; nothing comes about in relation to an actuality
that is past; at every actual moment of despair, the despairing person carries
all #[133]# the past as something present in possibility. This is because to
despair is a qualification of spirit that relates to the eternal in a person. But
he cannot rid himself of the eternal, no, not in all eternity. He cannot cast it
away from himself once and for all—nothing is more impossible. At every
moment that he does not have it, he must have cast it, or be casting it, away
from himself—but it comes back: that is, at every moment he despairs, he
brings despair upon himself. For despair is not a consequence of the
misrelation, but of the relation that relates to itself. And a person cannot be
rid of the relation that relates to itself any more than he can be rid of his
self, which, in fact, is one and the same thing, for the self is of course the
relation to itself.

C

Despair is: “the sickness unto death”

THIS CONCEPT, THE SICKNESS unto death, must, however, be understood in a
special sense. Understood straightforwardly, it designates a sickness of
which the end, the outcome, is death. Despair cannot be called the sickness
unto death in that sense. But from a Christian point of view, death itself is a
transition to life. Thus, in the Christian sense, there is no earthly, bodily
sickness unto death. For, of course, death is the end of the sickness, but
death is not the end. If there is to be talk of a sickness unto death in the

strictest sense, it must be one in which the end is death and in which death
is the end. And precisely this is despair.

Yet in another sense, despair is even more definitely the sickness unto
death. Indeed, it is as far as possible from being the case that, in a
straightforward sense, a person dies of this sickness or that this sickness
ends with a bodily death. On the contrary, the agony of this despair consists
precisely in being unable to die. Thus, it has more in common with the
condition of someone who is mortally ill when he lies struggling with death
and is unable to die. Thus, to be sick unto death is to be unable to die, yet
without any hope of life—no, the hopelessness is that even the last hope,
death, is not there. When death is the greatest danger, one hopes for life, but
when one becomes acquainted with the even more frightful danger, one
hopes for death. #[134]# So when the danger is so great that death has
become one’s hope, then despair is the hopelessness of not even being able
to die.

In this latter sense, then, the sickness unto death is despair, this
agonizing contradiction, this sickness in the self: eternally to die, to die and
yet not to die, to die the death.° For to die means that it is all over, but to die
the death means that we experience what it is to die, and if this can be
experienced for a single moment, it is thereby experienced forever. If a
human being were to die of despair as one dies of an illness, then the eternal
within him, the self, would have to be able to die in the same sense that the
body dies of illness. But this is an impossibility; despair’s dying constantly
transforms itself into life. The despairing person cannot die. “No more than
a dagger can kill thoughts’”* can despair consume the eternal, the self, that is
the basis of despair, whose worm does not die and whose fire is not
quenched.! Yet despair is precisely a self-consumption, but an impotent self-
consumption that is unable to do what it itself wants. What it wants is to
consume itself, which it is unable to do, and this impotence is a new form of
self-consumption in which, however, despair is again incapable of doing
what it wants, of consuming itself—this is an intensification or is the law of
the intensification. This is the incitement or the gangrene? in despair, the
gnawing that constantly goes further and further in, deeper and deeper in
impotent self-consumption. For the person in despair, so far from being a
consolation is the fact that the despair does not consume him, that it is

precisely the opposite; this consolation is precisely the agony, is precisely
what keeps the canker alive and keeps life in the canker, for it is precisely
over this that he has—not despaired—but is despairing: that he cannot
consume himself, cannot be rid of himself, cannot be annihilated. This is
the intensification of the formula for despair, the rising fever in this sickness
of the self.

A despairing person despairs over something. That is how it looks for a
moment, but only for a moment; at that same moment, true despair makes
its appearance, or the despair shows itself in its true form. When he
despaired over something, he really despaired over himself, and now he
wants to be rid of himself. Thus, when the power-crazed person whose
motto is “Either Caesar or nothing at all”® does not become Caesar, he
despairs over it. But it means something else: that precisely because he did
not become Caesar, he now cannot endure being himself. Thus he does not
really despair over the fact that he did not become #[135]# Caesar, but over
himself for not having become Caesar. This self which, had it become
Caesar, would have been his heart’s desire—which in another sense,
however, would have been equally despairing—to him, this self is now the
most intolerable of all. In a deeper sense, what is intolerable for him is not
the fact that he did not become Caesar; rather, what is intolerable to him is
this self that did not become Caesar; or still more accurately, what is
intolerable to him is that he cannot be rid of himself. If he had become
Caesar, he would have been rid of himself in despair, but now he has failed
to become Caesar and cannot be rid of himself in despair. Essentially, he is
equally despairing, for he does not have his self, he is not himself. By
having become Caesar, he would not, however, have become himself, but
would have been rid of himself, and by not having become Caesar he
despairs over not having been able to be rid of himself. Thus, it is a
superficial remark (presumably by someone who has never seen a
despairing person, much less seen himself) when someone says of a
despairing person, as if it were his punishment: “He is consuming himself.”
For that is precisely what he, despairingly, cannot—and is precisely what
he, in his agony, cannot—do: because with the despair, something that
cannot burn or be burned up has been ignited in the self.

Therefore, to despair over something is still not genuine despair. It is the
beginning stage, or as the physician would say of an illness, it has not yet
declared itself. What is next is declared despair, to despair over oneself. A
young girl despairs because of love, thus she despairs over the loss of the
beloved—that he died or was unfaithful to her. This is not declared despair;
no, she despairs over herself. This, her self, which, had she become “his”
beloved, she would have been rid of, or lost, in the most blissful fashion—
this self is now a plague to her if it is to be a self without “him.” This self
which—equally despairing, albeit in a different sense—would have become
her treasure, has become a revolting emptiness to her now that “he” is dead,
or it has become abhorrent to her because it reminds her that she has been
deceived. Now, try it, say to such a girl, “You are consuming yourself,” and
you will hear her reply: “Oh, no, the agony is precisely the fact that I cannot
do that.”

To despair over oneself, in despair to will to be rid of oneself, is the
formula for all despair, so the second form of despair, in despair to will to
be oneself, can therefore be traced back to the first, in despair not to will to
be #[136]# oneself, just as in the foregoing we resolved the form, in despair
not to will to be! oneself, into despair to will to be oneself (see A).’ A
person in despair wills in despair to be himself. But if he wills in despair to
be himself, then of course he does not will to be rid of himself. Yes, so it
seems, but when one looks more closely, one will nonetheless see that the
contradiction is the same. The self he wills in despair to be is a self he is not
(for to will to be the self he truly is, is of course precisely the opposite of
despair); he in fact wants to wrest his self away from the power that
established it. But despite all his despair, this is something he is unable to
do; despite all despair’s efforts, that power is stronger, and it compels him
to be the self he does not want to be. But, nonetheless, this is indeed how he
wants to be rid of himself, rid of the self he is, in order to be the self he
himself has come up with. To be a self in the way he wants (even though, in
another sense, that would be just as despairing) would be his greatest
delight, but to be compelled to be a self in a way he does not want to be,
that is his agony: that he cannot be rid of himself.

Socrates proved the immortality of the soul from the fact that the
sickness of the soul (sin) does not consume it as sickness of the body

consumes the body.® In this way, one can also prove the presence of the
eternal in a human being from the fact that despair cannot consume his self,
that this is precisely what constitutes the agony of contradiction in despair.
Were there nothing eternal in a human being, he would be absolutely unable
to despair, but were despair capable of consuming his self, despair would
not have been present in any case.

This, then, is what despair is: a sickness in the self, the sickness unto
death. The despairing person is mortally ill. In an entirely different sense
than with any sickness, the sickness unto death has attacked the noblest
parts, and yet he cannot die. Death is not the end of this sickness, but death
is incessantly the end. It is impossible to be rescued from this sickness by
death, for the sickness, and its agony—and the death—consist precisely in
being unable to die.

This is the state of being in despair. However much it escapes the notice
of the despairing person, however much the despairing person succeeds in
having entirely lost his self (this must apply especially to the sort of despair
that consists of ignorance of being in despair), and has lost it in sucha
manner that it is not noticed in the least way: eternity will nonetheless make
it manifest that his condition was despair, and it will nail him fast to his
self, so that the agony nonetheless continues to be that he cannot be rid of
his self, #[137]# and it becomes manifest that he was deluded when he
believed that he had succeeded in doing so. And eternity must do this,
because to have a self, to be a self, is the greatest, the infinite, concession
that has been granted a human being, but is also eternity’s requirement of
him.

B

THE UNIVERSALITY OF THIS
SICKNESS (DESPAIR)

J ust as a physician might well say that there perhaps is not one single

human being alive who is entirely healthy, so might one, if one were truly
knowledgeable concerning human beings, say that there is not a single
human being alive who is not, after all, a bit in despair, in whom there does
not, after all, dwell, deep down, an unrest, a discord, a disharmony, an
anxiety for an unknown something or for something with which he does not
even dare become acquainted, an anxiety concerning a possibility in
existence or an anxiety concerning himself—so that, as a physician speaks
of going about with a sickness in the body, this man in fact goes about with
a sickness, goes about carrying a sickness of the spirit, which, owing to and
accompanied by an anxiety that he finds inexplicable, permits him, once in
a while, to take note, fleetingly, of its presence within him. And in any case,
no human being who has ever lived, or who is living, outside of
Christendom—and no one within Christendom, if he is not a true Christian
—is without despair; and to the extent that he is not wholly a Christian, he
is nonetheless to some extent in despair.

To many people, this observation will undoubtedly seem a paradox, an
exaggeration, as well as a dark and depressing view of things. But it is none
of these things. It is not dark; on the contrary, it seeks to shed light on what
people ordinarily allow to remain in a certain obscurity. It is not depressing;
on the contrary, it is uplifting, as it views every person under the aspect of
the loftiest requirement to which he is subject, that of being spirit. Nor is it

a paradox; on the contrary it is a systematically developed foundational
view, and as such neither is it an exaggeration. #[139]#

On the other hand, the generally held view of despair stops at
appearances and is thus a superficial view—that is, it is no view at all. It
assumes that of course every person must himself know best whether or not
he is in despair. So the person who says that he is in despair is regarded as
being in despair, but the person who thinks he is not is regarded as not
being in despair. As a consequence of this, despair becomes a quite rare
phenomenon instead of being quite common. It is not a rare thing for a
person to be in despair; no, it is rare, very rare, that someone truly is not.

But the common view has a very poor understanding of despair. Among
other things, it entirely overlooks (merely to mention that which, properly
understood, includes thousands upon thousands and millions under the
category of despair) that not to be in despair, not to be conscious that one is
in despair, is precisely a form of despair. In a far deeper sense, in its
understanding of despair, the common view is similar to what sometimes
happens in connection with determining whether or not a person is sick—in
a far deeper sense, for the common view understands far less of what spirit
is (and without that, neither can one understand despair) than it understands
of sickness and health. Generally, one assumes that a person is healthy if he
does not himself say that he is sick, and even more so if he himself says he
is well. The physician, on the other hand, takes a different view of sickness.
Why so? Because the physician has a definite and developed notion of what
it is to be well, and in accordance with that, he assesses a person’s
condition. The physician knows that just as there is sickness that is merely
imaginary, so, too, is there health of this sort; therefore, in cases of the latter
type, he first employs methods that will cause the sickness to manifest
itself. Altogether, precisely because he is a physician (someone with
insight), the physician does not have unconditional faith in a person’s report
of the state of his own health. If it were the case that what every person said
concerning the state of his health—about whether he is healthy or sick,
about where his problem lies, etc.—could be unconditionally relied upon,
then being a physician would be a hallucination. For a physician has the
task of not only prescribing medications, but first and foremost of
recognizing the illness, and thus, in turn, first and foremost of ascertaining
whether the supposedly sick person is actually sick, or whether the

supposedly well person is perhaps actually sick. This is also the situation of
the physician of souls #[140]# in relation to despair. He knows what despair
is, he recognizes it, and therefore is satisfied neither with a person’s report
that he is not in despair nor with his report that he is. It must in fact be
noted that, in a certain sense, not even those who say they are in despair are
always in despair. One can in fact affect despair, and one can err and
confuse despair, which is a qualification of spirit, with all sorts of transitory
dejection and unhappiness that pass away without coming to the point of
despair. But the physician of souls indeed also regards these as forms of
despair; he sees very well that they are affectation, but precisely this
affectation is despair; he sees very well that this depression, etc., does not
have much significance, but precisely this—that it does not have and does
not acquire much significance—is despair.

Furthermore, the common view overlooks the fact that, in comparison
with illness, despair is far more dialectical than what people otherwise call a
sickness because it is a sickness of the spirit. And, properly understood, this
dialectical quality in turn brings thousands under the category of despair. If,
at a particular moment, a physician has indeed ascertained that such and
such a person is well, and he then later falls ill, the physician can be correct
in maintaining that at that time this person was well—now, on the other
hand, he is ill. With despair, it is otherwise. As soon as despair manifests
itself, it is manifest that the person was in despair. Thus at no moment can
one decide anything about a person who has not been saved by having been
in despair, for if something takes place that brings him to despair, it
instantly becomes manifest that he has been in despair all his life. On the
other hand, by no means can it be said that when a person has a fever, it
now becomes manifest that he has had a fever all his life. But despair is a
qualification of spirit, it is related to the eternal and therefore has something
of the eternal in its dialectic.

Not only is despair far more dialectical than an illness, but all the
symptoms related to despair are dialectical, and thus the superficial view is
so easily deceived in determining whether or not despair is present. Not to
be in despair can indeed signify precisely to be in despair, and it can signify
being rescued from being in despair. Security and tranquility can signify
being in despair—precisely this security, this tranquility can be the despair
—and it can signify #[141]# having overcome despair and won peace. Not

being in despair is not like not being sick, for not being sick cannot, after
all, be being sick, but not being in despair can mean precisely to be in
despair. The situation with despair is not like that of sickness, in which
feeling unwell constitutes the sickness. Not at all. Here again, feeling
unwell is dialectical. Never to have sensed this feeling of being unwell is
precisely to be in despair.

This means and is rooted in the fact that, regarded as spirit (and if one is
to speak of despair, one must regard human beings under the aspect of
spirit), a human being’s condition is always critical. People speak of a crisis
in relation to sickness, but not in relation to health. And why not? Because
bodily health is an immediate qualification that becomes dialectical only in
a state of sickness, when one can thus speak of a crisis. But spiritually, or
when a human being is regarded as spirit, both health and sickness are
critical; there is no such thing as immediate health of the spirit.

As soon as the human being ceases to be regarded under the aspect of
spirit (and if he is not regarded in that way, neither can there be any talk of
despair), but merely as a psychic-physical synthesis, health becomes an
immediate qualification, and the dialectical qualification arises only when
there is a sickness of the psyche or the body. But despair consists precisely
in a human being’s not being conscious of having been qualified as spirit.
Even that which, humanly speaking, is most beautiful and most lovable of
all, a feminine youthfulness that is sheer peace and harmony and joy: it is
nonetheless despair. This is in fact happiness, but happiness is no
qualification of spirit, and deep, deep within, farthest within, in the most
concealed hiddenness within happiness—there, indeed, dwells also the
anxiety that is despair; it wants so very much to be permitted to remain in
there, for this is despair’s dearest and most exquisite place to dwell: at the
very heart of happiness. Despite illusory security and peace, all immediacy
is anxiety and thus, quite consistently, is most anxious about nothing; one
does not make immediacy as anxious with the most terrifying description of
the most frightful something as with a subtle, almost casual, fragmentary
aside about an indefinite something, tossed off with the most calculated
reflection—indeed, one makes immediacy most anxious by craftily hinting
to it that it of course knows what one is talking about. For immediacy
certainly does not know it, #[142]# but never does reflection entrap with
such certainty as when it fashions its snare out of nothing, and never is

reflection so thoroughly itself as when it is—nothing. An eminent degree of
reflection—or, rather, great faith—is required in order to be able to endure
reflection upon nothingness, that is, infinite reflection. Thus, even that
which is the most beautiful and most lovable of all, a feminine
youthfulness, is nonetheless despair, is happiness. Therefore, neither can
one succeed in slipping through life on the basis of this immediacy. And if
this happiness does succeed in slipping through, well then, it doesn’t help
very much, for it is despair. Despair, precisely because it is entirely
dialectical, is indeed the sickness concerning which it is the greatest
misfortune never to have had it—a true godsend to contract it, although it is
the most dangerous sickness if one does not want to be cured of it. In other
cases, it can be said only that it is good fortune to be cured of a sickness—
the sickness itself is the misfortune.

The common view, which holds that despair is a rarity, is thus as far as
possible from being correct; on the contrary, despair is quite common. The
common view—that everyone who does not think or feel that he is in
despair is not in despair, and that only the person who says he is in despair
is in despair—is as far as possible from being correct. On the contrary, the
person who without affectation says that he is in despair is a little, a
dialectical step, closer to being cured than all those who are not regarded,
and who do not regard themselves, as being in despair. But (and here the
physician of souls will surely grant that I am right) this is precisely the
usual situation: that most people live without being properly aware that they
are destined as spirit—and this is the source of all the so-called security,
satisfaction with life, etc., etc., which is precisely despair. On the other
hand, those who say that they are in despair usually either are those who
possess a nature that is so much deeper that they must necessarily become
conscious of themselves as spirit, or are those who, owing to oppressive
circumstances and dreadful decisions, have been helped to become
conscious of themselves as spirit—it is either the one or the other, for a
person who is truly not in despair is certainly extremely rare.

Ah, so much is said about human want and wretchedness—I seek to
understand it and indeed have understood not a little of it firsthand; so much
is said about wasting a life: but only that person’s life was wasted who lived
in such a fashion that, deceived by life’s joys or by #[143]# its sorrows, he
never became eternally, decisively conscious of himself as spirit, as self, or,

what amounts to the same thing, never became aware and never in the
deepest sense gained the impression that there is a God, and that “he,”
himself, his self, exists before this God—an infinite gain that is never
attained except through despair. Alas, and this wretchedness—the fact that
so many people live like this, defrauded of this most blessed of all thoughts
—this wretchedness, that a person occupies himself (or, with the mass of
humanity, that someone occupies them) with everything else, using them to
furnish the energy for life’s drama, but never reminding them of this
blessedness; that someone heaps them up in groups—and defrauds them
instead of splitting them apart in order that each individual might gain what
is highest, the only thing worth living for, and is enough to live in for an
eternity: it seems to me that I could weep for an eternity over the fact that
this wretchedness exists! Ah, and to my way of thinking, this is one
additional expression of the terror of this most frightful of all sickness and
wretchedness: its hiddenness, not only that the person who suffers from it
may wish to conceal it and may succeed in doing so, that it can dwell in a
person in such a way that no one, no one, discovers it—no, that it can be so
concealed within a person that he himself is not aware of it! Ah, and then,
when the hourglass has run out, the hourglass of temporality; when the
hubbub of worldliness has fallen silent and the restless or futile busyness
comes to an end; when everything around you is still, as it is in eternity—
whether you are man or woman, rich or poor, dependent or independent,
fortunate or unfortunate; whether, in majesty, you bore the glory of the
crown, or, in lowly obscurity, bore only the toil and heat of the day;'
whether your name shall be remembered as long as the world stands, and
thus will have been remembered as long as it stood; or, without a name, you
ran nameless in the innumerable mass; whether the splendor with which
you were surrounded exceeded all human description, or whether you were
subjected to the harshest and most ignominious human judgment: eternity
asks of you, and of all these millions upon millions, only one thing, whether
you have lived in despair or not, whether you were in despair in such a way
that you did not know you were in despair, or in such a way that you bore
this sickness concealed in your innermost recess as your gnawing secret,
beneath your heart like the fruit of a sinful love, or in such a manner that
you raged in despair, a terror to others. And if this is so, if you have lived in

despair, whatever you #[144]# perhaps won or lost, then for you all is lost,
eternity does not acknowledge you, it never knew you'' —or even more
frightfully, it knows you as you are known," it binds you fast to your self in
despair!

THE FORMS OF THIS SICKNESS
(DESPAIR)

The forms of despair can be determined by reflecting upon the elements of

which the self, as a synthesis, consists. The self is composed of infinitude
and finitude. But this synthesis is a relation, and a relation which, even
though derived, relates to itself, which is freedom. The self is freedom. But
freedom is the dialectical element in the categories of possibility and
necessity.

Principally, however, despair must be viewed under the aspect of
consciousness. Whether despair is conscious or not constitutes the
qualitative distinction between one form of despair and another. To be sure,
regarded in terms of its concept, all despair is conscious, but it does not
follow from this that the person in whom it is present, the person who, from
a conceptual point of view, may be said to be in despair, is himself
conscious of this. Thus consciousness is what is decisive. In general,
consciousness—i.e., self-consciousness—is the decisive factor in relation to
the self. The more consciousness, the more self; the more consciousness,
the more will; the more will, the more self. A person who possesses no will
whatsoever is no self, but the more will he has, the more self-consciousness
he has as well.

a) DESPAIR CONSIDERED UNDER THE QUALIFICATION
FINITUDE/INFINITUDE

The self is the conscious synthesis of infinitude and finitude that relates to
itself, whose task is to become itself, which can only be done in the relation
to God. But to become oneself is to become concrete. But to become
concrete is neither to become finite nor to become infinite, for that which is
to become concrete is indeed a synthesis. The development must therefore
consist in coming infinitely away from oneself in the infinitization of the
self and in coming infinitely back to oneself in the finitization. If, on the
other hand, the self does not become itself, then it is in despair, whether it
knows this or not. Yet at every moment it exists, the self is in the process of
becoming, for the self xata Svvapiv' has not actually come into existence,
it is only that which shall come into existence. Insofar, then, as the self does
not become itself, it is not its self; but not to be one’s self is precisely
despair.

a) Infinitude’s despair is to lack finitude

That this is so is owing to the dialectical fact that the self is a synthesis, and
thus each component is always its opposite. No form of despair can be
defined straightforwardly (i.e., undialectically), but only by reflecting upon
its opposite. The condition of the despairing person while he is in despair
can be described straightforwardly, as is indeed done by the poet, who has
him speak. But the despair can be defined only by its opposite, and if the
words he speaks are to have poetic value, the play of colors in his
expressions must contain a reflection of the dialectical opposite.! Thus,
every human life which presumably has become, or which simply wants to

be, infinite—indeed, every instant in which a human life has become, or
simply wants to be, infinite—is despair. For the self is the synthesis in
which the finite is the limiting element and the infinite is the expansive
element. Infinity’s despair is therefore the fantastic, the boundless; for,
indeed, the self is only healthy and free of despair when, precisely by
having despaired, it is grounded transparently in God.

The fantastic is of course most closely related to the imagination, but
the imagination, in turn, is related to feeling, knowledge, and will, so that a
person #[147]# can have imaginary feeling, knowledge, and will.
Altogether, the imagination is the medium of infinitization; the imagination
is not a faculty like the other faculties—if it is to be spoken of in that
manner, it is the faculty instar omnium.' In the final analysis, whatever
feeling, knowledge, and will a person has depends upon what imagination
he has, that is, upon how this person reflects himself, that is, upon
imagination. Imagination is reflection that infinitizes, which is why the
elder Fichte quite rightly assumed that even in relation to knowledge,
imagination is the source of the categories.* The self is reflection, and the
imagination is reflection, is the reproduction of the self, which is the
possibility of the self. The imagination is the possibility of all reflection,
and the intensity of this medium is the possibility of the intensity of the self.

On the whole, the fantastic is what leads a person out into the infinite in
such a way that it only leads him away from himself and thereby prevents
him from coming back to himself.

When the feelings become fantastic in this manner, the self merely
becomes increasingly volatilized and finally becomes an abstract sensitivity
which, in inhuman fashion, does not belong to any human being, but which
inhumanly participates feelingly, so to speak, in the fate of one or another
abstraction, e.g., humanity in abstracto.! Just as a person with rheumatism
is not the master of his physical sensations (which are subject to the wind
and the weather), so that he involuntarily notices changes in the atmosphere
and so forth, so, too, with the person whose feelings have become fantastic:
in a way, he has been infinitized, but not in such a way that he more and
more becomes himself, for he more and more loses himself.

It is the same with knowledge when it becomes fantastic. The law
regarding the development of the self with respect to knowledge—insofar

as it is to be true that the self becomes itself—is that the increasing degree
of knowledge corresponds to the degree of self-knowledge, so that the more
the self knows, the more it knows itself. If this does not happen, then, the
more knowledge increases, the more it becomes a sort of inhuman
knowledge in the production of which the person’s self is squandered, more
or less as human beings were squandered in the building of the pyramids or
as human beings are squandered in that Russian horn music by being used
for one note, neither more nor less.°

When the will becomes fantastic, then, in similar fashion, the self
becomes more and more volatilized. Then the will no longer continues to
become concrete to the same degree that it is abstract, #[148]# so that the
more it is infinitized in purpose and determination, the more entirely
present and simultaneous it becomes with itself in that small portion of the
task that can be accomplished right now, so that in being infinitized, it in
the strictest sense comes back to itself, so that in being farthest away from
itself (when it is most infinitized in purpose and decision) it is at that same
instant nearest itself in accomplishing that infinitely small portion of the
work which can be accomplished even today, even at this hour, even at this
instant.

And then, when feeling or knowledge or will have become fantastic—
then, in the end, the entire self can become so, whether it thus assumes a
more active form in which a person plunges into the fantastic, or a more
passive form in which he is carried away: but in either case, he bears
responsibility. The self, then, leads a fantastic existence in abstract
infinitization or in abstract isolation, continually lacking its self, from
which it moves only farther and farther away. Thus, for example, in the
religious realm: the relation to God is infinitizing, but this infinitizing can
SO CalTy a person away in fantasy that it merely becomes a sort of
intoxication. It can seem to a person as though it were impossible to endure
existing before God because he cannot indeed come back to himself,
become himself. To characterize him with the help of a few lines, a
religious fantast of this sort would say: “That a sparrow can live*!i—that is
something I can grasp, it does not know that it exists before God—but to
know that one exists before God, and then not, at that very instant, go mad
or become nothing!”

But even though a person has become fantastic in this way and is thus in
despair—even though that usually becomes obvious—he can nonetheless
very well go on living, he can be, as it seems, a person engaged in temporal
affairs, he can get married, beget children, be honored and respected—and
perhaps people fail to notice that, in the deeper sense, he is lacking a self. In
the world, no great fuss is made over this sort of thing, for in the world, a
self is the thing that is least asked about and, most of all, is the thing that is
dangerous to show that you have. In the world, the greatest danger, that of
losing one’s self,! can occur so quietly that it is as if it were nothing at all.
No other loss can take place so quietly; every other loss—an arm, a leg, five
rixdollars,* a spouse, etc.—is noticed, however.

B) Finitude’s despair is to lack infinitude

That this is the case, as was shown under qa, is inherent in the dialectical fact
that the self is a synthesis, and thus either element is its opposite.

To lack infinitude is despairing limitedness, narrow-mindedness. But
this, of course, refers solely to ethical narrow-mindedness and limitedness.
In the world people really speak only of intellectual or aesthetic limitedness
or of the matters of indifference that are always most spoken of in the
world, for worldliness consists precisely in attributing infinite value to
matters of indifference. The worldly view of things always fastens onto the
difference between one person and another and of course has no
understanding of the one thing needful! (for to possess that is spirituality)
and thus no understanding of the limitedness and narrow-mindedness of
having lost oneself, not by having become volatilized in the infinite, but by
having become completely finitized, by having become a number, one more
person, one more repetition of the perpetual Einerlei," instead of being a
self.

Despairing narrow-mindedness consists in lacking primitivity or in
having deprived oneself of one’s primitivity, in having, spiritually
understood, emasculated oneself. Every human being is in fact primally
established as a self, destined to become oneself, and of course every self is,
as such, angular, but this means merely that one is to be polished, not that
one is to be ground down, not that one is entirely to give up being oneself

out of fear of people, or even that one should not, out of fear of people, dare
to be oneself in one’s essential accidentality (which is precisely what must
not be ground down), in which one is in fact oneself for oneself. But while
the one sort of despair goes astray in the infinite and loses itself, another
sort of despair permits itself, as it were, to be fooled out of its self by “the
others.” By looking at the mass of people with which one is surrounded, by
busying oneself with all sorts of worldly matters, by learning how things go
in the world, such a person then forgets himself, forgets what his name
(divinely understood) is, does not dare believe in himself, finds it too risky
to be himself and much easier and safer to be like the others, to become an
imitation, to become a number, a part of the crowd.

Now, the world takes just about no notice whatsoever of this sort of
despair. Precisely by losing himself in this way, someone of this sort has #
[150]# perfected himself so that he can properly become a part of the hustle
and bustle of daily life, indeed, can be a success in the world. Here there are
no delays, no difficulties concerning his self and its infinitization; he is as
worn down and polished as a pebble on the beach, accepted as readily as a
coin in circulation. So far is he from being regarded by anyone as being in
despair that he is precisely what a person ought to be. Generally speaking,
the world has of course no understanding of what is truly frightful.
Naturally, the despair that not only does not inconvenience a person’s life in
any way, but that in fact makes life easy and pleasant for a person, is in no
way regarded as despair. That this is the world’s way of viewing the matter
can be seen, for example, from virtually all proverbs, which are merely
counsels of prudence. Thus people say that a person regrets ten times for
having spoken for every one time that he regrets having remained silent—
and why? Because having spoken is an external fact that can involve a
person in unpleasantness, as it is something actual. But, to have remained
silent! And yet, that is the most dangerous thing of all. For by remaining
silent, a person is left entirely to himself; nothing actual comes to help him
by chastising him, by bringing upon him the consequences of his having
spoken. No, in that respect, remaining silent is something easy to do. But
for that reason, the person who knows what is frightful fears most of all
every error, every sin, that takes an inward course and leaves no outward
trace. In the eyes of the world, it is dangerous to dare in this way—and
why? Because one might lose. But not to venture—that is prudent. And yet,

by not venturing, one can so frightfully and easily lose something that
would be difficult to lose no matter how much one would have lost by
venturing, and in any case one could never have lost it in that way—as
easily, as entirely, as if it were nothing at all: oneself. For if I have ventured
wrongly, well then, life helps me with chastisement. But if I have not
ventured at all, who will help me then? And, if in addition, by not having
ventured at all in the highest sense (and to venture in the highest sense is
precisely to become aware of oneself), I cravenly win every earthly
advantage—and lose myself!

And this is precisely how it is with finitude’s despair. Because a person
despairs in this way, he can go on living very well (and actually, indeed,
even better) in the temporal world, be to all appearances a human being, be
praised by others, honored and respected, occupied with all the pursuits of
the temporal world. Indeed, precisely what people call worldliness consists
of nothing but of such people who, one can thus say, have made a pact with
the world.° They employ their talents, accumulate money, carry out worldly
enterprises, #[151]# calculate shrewdly, and so on and so forth, perhaps go
down in history—but they are not themselves; spiritually understood, they
possess no self, no self for the sake of which they could venture everything,
no self before God—however selfish they are in other respects.

b) DESPAIR CONSIDERED UNDER THE QUALIFICATION
POSSIBILITY/NECESSITY

Possibility and necessity are equally essential for becoming (and the self
must indeed become itself freely). Just as infinitude and finitude (aneipov—
mepac)"' belong to the self, so also do possibility and necessity. A self that
has no possibility is in despair, as is a self that has no necessity.

a) Possibility’s despair is to lack necessity

That this is the case, as has been shown, is inherent in the dialectic.®

As finitude is the limiting factor in relation to infinitude, so, in relation
to possibility, is necessity the constraint. Inasmuch as the self, as a synthesis
of finitude and infinitude, has been established and exists ata Svvatty,!

then, in order to become itself, it reflects itself in the medium of the
imagination, whereby infinite possibility reveals itself. %ata Suvapty, the
self is just as much possible as it is necessary, for it is of course itself, but it
must become itself. Insofar as it is itself, it is the necessary, and insofar as it
must become itself, it is a possibility.

Now, if possibility outruns necessity, then the self runs away from itself
in possibility so that it has no necessity to which it must return: this, then, is
possibility’s despair. This self becomes an abstract possibility which leaps
about in possibility until it is exhausted but does not move from the place
where it is, nor does it come to any place, for the necessary is precisely the
place; to become oneself is of course precisely a movement in place. To
become is a movement from a place, but to become oneself is a movement
in place.

To the self, it seems that possibility is greater and greater; more and
more becomes possible for it, because nothing becomes actual. Finally, it is
as if everything were possible, but this is precisely when the abyss has
swallowed up the self. Every little possibility, #[152]# after all, would take
some time in order to become actuality. But finally, the time that should be
used for actuality gets shorter and shorter; everything becomes more and
more momentary. Possibility becomes more and more intensive, but in the
sense of possibility, not in the sense of actuality, for in the sense of
actuality, what is intensive is that at least some of what is possible becomes
actual. At the moment that something appears to be possible, a new
possibility appears: finally, these phantasmagorias follow upon one another
so rapidly that it seems as if everything were possible, and that is precisely
the final moment when the individual himself has wholly become a mirage.

What the self now lacks is of course actuality; that is in fact what we
generally say when we say that a person has become unreal. But upon
closer inspection what he really lacks is necessity. What the philosophers
explain—that necessity is the unity of possibility and actuality’—is not in
fact true: no, actuality is the unity of possibility and necessity. Nor, when a
self gets lost in possibility in this way, is it merely a lack of strength, at any
rate not in the way that people generally understand it. What is lacking is
really the strength to obey, to submit to what is necessary in one’s self, to
what might be called one’s limit. Therefore, neither is the misfortune that a

self of this sort did not amount to something in the world—no, the
misfortune is that he did not pay attention to himself, did not pay attention
to the fact that the self he is is something quite definite and is thus the
necessary. Instead, he lost himself when this self reflected itself fantastically
in possibility. Even to be able to see oneself in the mirror, it is necessary to
know oneself, for if one does not know oneself, one does not see oneself,
but only a human being. But the mirror of possibility is no ordinary mirror;
it must be used with extreme caution, for in the highest sense, this mirror is
untruthful. That a self looks a certain way in the possibility of itself is only
a half-truth, for in the possibility of itself, the self is still far from being—or
is only halfway—itself. What matters, then, is how this self’s necessity
defines it more specifically. With possibility, the situation is similar to when
one invites a child to one or another amusement: the child is immediately
willing, but then it depends upon whether the parents will give their
permission—and as with the parents, so it is with necessity. #[153]#

Yet everything is possible in possibility. Thus, one can get lost in
possibility in every possible way, though essentially in two ways. The one
form is in wishing, longing; the other is in melancholia-fantasy (hope/fear
or anxiety). Fairy and folk tales often tell of a knight who suddenly catches
sight of a rare bird which he continues to pursue, for at first it looks as if he
is quite close to it, but then it flies off again, until night falls and he is
separated from his companions, lost in the empty landscape in which he
now finds himself. This is how it is with wish’s possibility. Instead of taking
possibility back into necessity, he runs after the possibility—and finally he
is unable to find his way back to himself. — With melancholia, the opposite
takes place in similar fashion: in his amorous melancholia, the individual
pursues an anxiety-laden possibility which, in the end, leads him away from
himself, so that he perishes in the anxiety or perishes in what he anxiously
feared perishing in.

B) Necessity’s despair is to lack possibility

If one were to compare running wild in possibility with a child’s efforts at
pronouncing vowels, then lacking possibility is like being dumb. Necessity,
as it were, is solely composed of consonants, but in order to pronounce
them, possibility is required. If the latter is lacking, if a human life is

brought to the point that it lacks possibility, it is in despair, and is in despair
at every moment it lacks possibility.

Now, generally speaking, people think that there is a certain age that is
especially full of hope, or they say that at a certain point in time, at a
particular moment in his life, a person is, or was, so full of hope and
possibility. All this, however, is merely human talk that does not get to the
truth; all this hope and all this despair is not yet either true hope or true
despair.

What is decisive is this: that for God everything is possible.' This is
eternally true, and thus is true at every instant. People indeed say something
like this all the time, and in everyday speech people put it this way, but the
decisive point comes only when the person is brought to his uttermost
extremity, when, humanly speaking, there is no possibility. Then the
question is whether he will believe that for God everything is possible—that
is, whether he will have faith. But this is the entire formula for losing one’s
understanding; to #[154]# have faith is precisely to lose one’s
understanding in order to gain God. Suppose the following. Imagine a
person who—with a shudder summoned up by the whole of his terrified
imagination—has envisioned one or another terrible thing that is absolutely
unendurable. Then it happens to him, precisely this terrible thing happens to
him. In human terms, his downfall is the most certain thing of all—and the
despair in his soul battles despairingly to be permitted to despair, to gain, if
you will, the peace in which to despair, the consent of his entire personality
to despair and to be in despair; thus he would curse nothing and no one
more than the person who attempted, and the attempt itself, to prevent him
from despairing, as is excellently and incomparably expressed by the poet
of poets:

Verwtinscht sei Vetter, der mich abgelenkt Von dem bequemen Wege
zur Verzweiflung (Richard II, act 2, scene 3)!!

Thus, in human terms, salvation is the most impossible thing of all, but for
God everything is possible! This is the battle of faith, which battles—
madly, if you will—for possibility. For possibility is the only salvation.
When someone faints, people shout: Water! Eau de cologne! Hoffmann’s
drops!® But when someone is about to despair, the cry is: Get possibility,

get possibility, possibility is the only salvation! A possibility: then the
person in despair breathes again, he revives; for in the absence of
possibility, it is as though a person cannot breathe. Sometimes, the
inventiveness of the human imagination suffices to procure possibility, but
in the final analysis—that is, when it is a matter of having faith—the only
thing that helps is this: that for God everything is possible.

Thus the struggle goes. Whether the person who struggles is to go down
in defeat depends simply and solely upon whether he obtains possibility,
i.e., whether he will have faith. And yet, he understands that, in human
terms, his downfall is the most certain thing of all. This is the dialectical
element in having faith. Ordinarily, a person knows nothing other than that
he hopes that this or that thing, most likely, etc., will not happen to him.
Then, if it happens to him, it is his downfall. The foolhardy person rushes
into a danger that entails one or another possibility, and if it then happens to
him, he despairs and meets his downfall. In human terms, the person of
faith sees and understands his downfall (in what has befallen him or in what
he has ventured), but he has faith. Therefore he does not succumb. He
leaves the means of his deliverance entirely to God, but he has faith that for
God everything is possible. It is impossible to have faith in his downfall. To
understand that, from a human point of view, it is his downfall and
nonetheless to have faith in possibility, is to have faith. Then #[155]# God
indeed helps him, perhaps through permitting him to escape the terror,
perhaps through the terror itself, that here, unexpectedly, miraculously,
divinely—help appears. Miraculously—for it is, after all, a curious sort of
narrow-mindedness to hold that receiving miraculous help is something that
can only have happened to a person eighteen hundred years ago. Whether a
person has been helped miraculously depends, essentially, upon the degree
of passion with which his understanding has understood that the help was
impossible, and, next, upon how honest he then is toward the power that
nevertheless helped him. But ordinarily people do neither the one thing nor
the other: they shriek that help is impossible without even having strained
their understanding to find help, and afterward they ungratefully lie.

The person of faith possesses the eternally sure antidote to despair:
possibility, because for God everything is possible at every instant. This is
the health of faith that resolves contradictions. The contradiction here is that
in human terms downfall is certain and that there is possibility nonetheless.

Generally speaking, health consists in the ability to resolve contradictions.
Thus, in the bodily or physical sense, a draft is a contradiction, for a draft
consists disparately or undialectically of cold and warmth, but a healthy
body resolves this contradiction and does not notice the draft. So, too, with
faith.

To lack possibility means either that everything has become necessary
for a person or that everything has become trivial.

The determinist, the fatalist, is in despair, and, as a person in despair, he
has lost his self, because for him everything has become necessity. His
situation is like that of the king who starved to death because all food
turned into gold.’ Personhood is a synthesis of possibility and necessity. Its
continued existence is thus like breathing (respiration): there is a breathing
in and a breathing out. The determinist’s self cannot breathe, for it is
impossible solely and only to breathe necessity, which simply suffocates a
person’s self. — The fatalist is in despair; he has lost God and thus his self,
for the person who does not have a God does not have a self, either. But the
fatalist does not have a God, or what is the same thing, his God is necessity.
Inasmuch as everything is possible for God, then God is that everything is
possible. Therefore, the fatalist’s worship of God is at most an interjection,
and in essence it is muteness, mute subjection—he cannot pray. To pray is
also to breathe, and possibility is for the self what oxygen is for breathing.
But possibility or necessity alone cannot suffice for the breathing of prayer
any more than a person can breathe solely oxygen #[156]# or nitrogen.!° In
order to pray there must be a God, a self—and possibility, or a self and
possibility in the eminent sense, for God is that everything is possible, or,
that everything is possible is God. And only the person whose being has
been so shaken that he became spirit by understanding that everything is
possible—only that person has engaged with God. The fact that God’s will
is the possible makes it possible for me to pray—were it merely what is
necessary, a human being would be essentially the same as an unspeaking
animal.

With the bourgeois philistine mentality, with triviality, which also
essentially lacks possibility, things are to some extent different. Bourgeois
philistinism is spiritlessness; determinism and fatalism are spiritual despair,
but spiritlessness is also despair. Bourgeois philistinism lacks every

characteristic of spirit and is taken up into the realm of probability, within
which the possible has its little bit of room. It thus lacks the possibility of
becoming aware of God. Devoid of imagination, as the bourgeois philistine
always is, he lives in a sort of trivial compendium of experiences about how
things turn out, what things are possible, what usually happens—regardless
of whether the bourgeois philistine is a bartender or a cabinet minister. In
this way, the bourgeois philistine has lost himself and God. For in order to
become aware of one’s self and of God, imagination must propel a person
to something higher than the murky atmosphere of probability, it must wrest
him free of that, and—in making possible what extends beyond the
qvantum satis! of all experience—it must teach him to hope and to fear, or
to fear and to hope. But the bourgeois philistine has no imagination, does
not want to have it, abominates it. Thus, here there is no help to be had.
And if existence occasionally helps by means of terrors that go beyond the
trivial parrot-wisdom of experience, bourgeois philistinism despairs—that
is, it becomes evident it was despair; it lacks the possibility of faith, of
being able, through God, to save a self from certain downfall.

Fatalism and determinism, after all, have sufficient imagination to
despair of possibility; they have possibility enough to discover the
impossibility; bourgeois philistinism reassures itself with trivialities,
equally in despair whether things go well or badly. Fatalism and
determinism lack the possibility needed to relax and mitigate—to temper—
necessity, that is, possibility as mitigation; bourgeois philistinism lacks
possibility as an awakening from spiritlessness. For bourgeois philistinism
believes it has #[157]# possibility at its disposal, believes that it has fooled
that enormous elasticity into the trap or madhouse of probability, believes
that it holds it captive. It leads possibility about, captive in the cage of
probability, exhibits it, imagining itself to be the master, not noticing that
precisely by doing so it has imprisoned itself as the slave of spiritlessness
and is the most wretched of all. For the person who has become lost in
possibility soars with the daring of despair; the person for whom everything
became necessary overreaches himself in life, crushed in despair; but
bourgeois philistinism, devoid of spirit, triumphs.

B

Despair considered under the qualification:
consciousness

DESPAIR CONTINUALLY INCREASES IN its intensity as the degree of
consciousness increases or in proportion to its increase: the more
consciousness, the more intense the despair. This is seen everywhere, and
most clearly in the maximum and minimum of despair. The devil’s despair
is the most intense despair, for the devil is pure spirit and in this respect is
absolute consciousness and transparency. In the devil there is no obscurity
that could serve as a mitigating excuse; his despair is therefore the most
absolute defiance. This is despair at its maximum. At its minimum, despair
is a state which (indeed, in human fashion, one could almost be tempted to
say this), in a sort of innocence, does not even know that it is despair. Thus,
despair is least when unconsciousness is at its maximum; it is indeed almost
as though there were a dialectical question as to whether one can justify
calling such a state despair.

a) THE DESPAIR THAT IS IGNORANT OF BEING DESPAIR, OR THE
DESPAIRING IGNORANCE OF HAVING A SELF AND AN ETERNAL
SELF

That this state nonetheless is despair and is rightly designated as such is an
expression of what, in the good sense, is called the obstinacy of truth.
Veritas est index sui et falsi.' But this obstinacy of truth is not truly
respected, just as it is far from being the case that people generally #[158]#
regard the relationship to truth, of relating oneself to the truth, as the highest
good, and are very far from the Socratic principle of regarding being in
error as the greatest misfortune; most often, the sensuous element in them
greatly outweighs their intellectuality. When a person is supposedly happy
in this fashion, imagining himself to be happy, although regarded in the
light of truth, is in fact unhappy, then most often it is far from being the

case that he wishes to be rescued from this error. On the contrary, he
becomes embittered, he regards the person who does this as his worst
enemy; he regards it as an assault, almost a murder, as when people say “it
murders his happiness.” And what is the reason for this? It is because he is
completely dominated by the sensuous and the sensuous-psychic; it is
because he lives in the categories of the sensuous, the pleasant and the
unpleasant, turning his back on spirit, truth, and the like; it is because he is
too sensate to have the courage to dare be, and endure being, spirit.
However vain and conceited people may be, most often they nonetheless
have a very feeble conception of themselves, that is, they have no
conception of being spirit, the absolute that a human being can be; but they
are vain and conceited—comparing themselves to one another. Imagine a
house consisting of a cellar, a ground floor, and a second floor, !? occupied
or arranged in such a manner that there was, or was intended to be, a
difference in social class between the occupants of each floor—and if we
were to compare being a human being with a house of this sort, then with
most people, the sad and ludicrous result is that they prefer to live in the
cellar of their own house. Every human being is a psychic-physical
synthesis destined to be spirit: this is the building. But he prefers to live in
the cellar, that is, in the categories of the sensuous. And not only does he
prefer to live in the cellar, he loves it so much that he becomes infuriated if
someone wants to suggest that he move into the belle-étage, which is vacant
and available to him—for, after all, the house he lives in is his own.

No, being in error is what people, quite un-Socratically, fear least. One
can see amazing examples that illustrate this on a grand scale. A thinker
erects an enormous building, a system, a system that encompasses the
whole of existence and the history of the world, etc.—and if one observes
his personal life, one discovers, #[159]# to one’s astonishment, the frightful
and ludicrous fact that he himself does not personally occupy this enormous
palace with its lofty ceilings, but lives in an outbuilding beside it, or a
doghouse, or at most in the porter’s lodge. If one were to take the liberty of
pointing out this contradiction by saying a single word about it, he would be
offended. For he has no fear of being in error, as long as he is able to
complete the System!*—assisted by being in error.

Thus, the fact that the person who is in despair does not himself know
that his condition is despair makes no difference in the matter: he is in
despair all the same. If despair consists of going astray, then the fact that a
person is ignorant of the fact that he is in despair merely means that he is
also under a delusion. The relation of ignorance to despair is similar to the
relation of ignorance to anxiety (see The Concept of Anxiety by Vigilius
Haufniensis!*); the anxiety of spiritlessness is recognizable precisely by
the spiritless sense of security. But nonetheless, the anxiety is present
underneath—so, too, is despair also present underneath, and when the
bewitchment of the senses comes to an end, when existence begins to
waver, then it immediately becomes apparent that despair, too, was lying
undermeath.

Compared with the person who is conscious of being in despair, the
despairing person who is ignorant that he is in despair is merely one
negativity further away from truth and salvation. Despair itself is a
negativity, ignorance of it is a new negativity. But in order to reach the
truth, one must go through every negativity, for here, what the old folk tale
says about breaking a certain enchantment is true: the entire piece must be
played backward or the enchantment will not be broken.!° But it is only in
one sense—the purely dialectical sense—that the person who is ignorant of
his despair is further from truth and salvation than the person who knows of
his despair and yet nonetheless remains in despair, for in another sense, the
ethical-dialectical sense, the person who is conscious of being in despair
and remains in despair is further from salvation, because his despair is more
intense. But so far is ignorance from dispelling the despair or transforming
despair into non-despair that on the contrary, it can be the most dangerous
form of despair. In ignorance, the person in despair is—albeit unto his own
corruption—in a way protected against becoming aware: that is, he is quite
securely in the grasp of despair.

A person is furthest from being conscious of himself as spirit when he is
ignorant of being in despair. But precisely this—not being conscious of
oneself as spirit #[160]#—is despair, it is spiritlessness, whether it be a state
of utter extinction, a merely vegetative life, or an energized life of which
the secret is nonetheless despair. In the latter case, the condition of the
person in despair is like that of a person who suffers from consumption: he

feels at his best, regards himself as most healthy, perhaps is regarded by
others as the picture of health—precisely when the illness is at its most
dangerous point.

This form of despair (ignorance of it) is most common in the world;
indeed, what people call the world, or more precisely, what Christianity
calls the world—paganism and the natural human being in Christendom,
paganism as it was historically and the paganism within Christendom—is
precisely this type of despair: it is despair but does not know it. It is true
that paganism and the natural human being make a distinction between
being in despair and not being in despair, as if there were only some
specific individuals who were in despair. But this distinction is just as
deceptive as that which paganism and the natural human being make
between love and self-love, as if all this love were not essentially self-love.
But it is impossible for paganism and the natural human being to come any
further than this deceptive distinction, because despair’s specific
characteristic is precisely this: to be ignorant of the fact that it is despair.

Now, from this it can easily be seen that the aesthetic concept of
spiritlessness does not in any way provide a criterion for judging what is
and is not despair, which, incidentally, is quite in order, for if, indeed, spirit
cannot truly be defined aesthetically, how then should aesthetics be able to
answer a question that simply does not exist for it! It would of course also
be enormously foolish to deny that both pagan peoples en masse and
individual pagans have accomplished amazing feats that have inspired and
will continue to inspire poets, foolish to deny that the pagan world boasts
examples of things which, from the point of view of aesthetics, cannot be
sufficiently admired. It would also be foolish to deny that in paganism lives
have been led, and can be led, by the natural human being, lives that are
rich in the greatest aesthetic enjoyment and that in most tasteful fashion
employ every possible advantage and indeed allow art and science to help
enhance, beautify, and refine the enjoyment. No, the aesthetic definition of
spiritlessness does not provide #[161]# the criterion for what is and is not
despair—the definition that must be employed is the ethical-religious: spirit
or, negatively, lack of spirit, spiritlessness. Every human existence that is
not conscious of itself as spirit or personally conscious of itself, before God,
as spirit; every human existence that thus is not grounded transparently in
God in this way, but that reposes obscurely within, and is subsumed into,

some abstract universal (the state, the nation, and the like); or, in obscurity
concerning itself, regards its abilities merely as active capacities without
becoming in any deeper sense conscious of itself with respect to why’ it has
them; that regards itself, if it is to have any inner understanding of itself, as
an inexplicable something: every such existence, whatever else it
accomplishes—the most astounding things; whatever else it explains, if it
were the whole of existence; however intensely it enjoys life aesthetically:
every such existence is nonetheless despair. This was what the ancient
Church Fathers meant when they said that the virtues of the pagans were
glittering vices:!° they meant that the pagan’s innermost self was despair,
that the pagan was not conscious of himself before God as spirit. This is
also why (I cite this as an example, though it also has a deeper connection
to this entire investigation) the pagans had such an irresponsible view of
suicide—indeed, they praised it!’—even though for the spirit it is the most
decisive of sins to escape from existence like this: rebellion against God.
The pagan lacked the spirit’s definition of a self; therefore he judged
Suicide! as he did, and this was done by the very same pagan who passed
such strict moral judgment against theft, fornication, and the like. He lacked
the point of view for suicide, he lacked the God-relationship and the self;
considered in purely pagan terms, suicide is a matter of indifference, it is
something anyone can do if he pleases, because it is no one else’s business.
From a pagan standpoint, if there is to be an admonition against suicide, it
must take the roundabout way of pointing out that a person would be
defaulting on his obligations to other people. The point about suicide, that it
is really a crime against God, entirely escapes the pagan. Thus one cannot
say that suicide was despair, which would be a thoughtless hysteron-
proteron;! one may say that the fact that the pagan judged suicide as he did
was despair.

Nevertheless, there is and remains a difference, and it is a qualitative
difference, between paganism in the stricter sense and paganism within
Christendom, the difference that has been pointed out by Vigilius
Haufniensis in relation to anxiety:'® #[162]# that paganism indeed lacks
spirit, but nonetheless is oriented in the direction of spirit, whereas
paganism within Christendom lacks spirit in the direction away from spirit,

or by having fallen away from it, and is therefore spiritlessness in the
strictest sense.

b) THE DESPAIR THAT IS CONSCIOUS OF BEING DESPAIR, THAT
THUS IS CONSCIOUS OF HAVING A SELF IN WHICH THERE IS AT
ANY RATE SOMETHING ETERNAL, AND THEN EITHER IN
DESPAIR WILLS NOT TO BE ITSELF OR IN DESPAIR WILLS TO BE
ITSELF

Here, of course, a distinction must be made as to whether the person who is
conscious of his despair has the true conception of what despair is. Thus,
according to the conception of despair he has, a person can certainly be
correct in saying that he is in despair; he can also be correct concerning the
fact that he is in despair, yet this does not mean that he possesses the true
conception of despair—it could be possible that someone, having
considered that person’s life in light of this conception, must say:
“Fundamentally, you are far more despairing than you know, the despair is
rooted far more deeply than that.” Thus, with the pagan (to recall what has
been said): When he regarded himself, in comparison to other pagans, as
being in despair, he was certainly right about being in despair, but he was
wrong in thinking that the others were not—that is, he did not have the true
conception of despair.

Thus, on the one hand, the true conception of despair is required for
there to be conscious despair. On the other hand, what is required is clarity
regarding oneself—that is, insofar as clarity and despair can be thought at
the same time. The extent to which complete clarity regarding oneself
concerning the fact that one is in despair can be combined with being in
despair—that is, whether this clarity of knowledge and self-knowledge
must not simply wrest a person out of despair, make him so fearful for
himself that he ceases to be in despair—we will not decide here; we will not
even make an attempt to do so, because later we will find a place to
investigate this entire matter. But here, without pursuing the thought to that
dialectical extreme, we will merely point out that just as there can be very
different degrees of consciousness concerning what despair is, so also is this
the case with respect to degrees of consciousness conceming one’s own

condition, that is, the fact that it is one of despair. #[163]# Actual life is too
multifarious merely to set forth such abstract oppositions as those between a
despair that is utterly ignorant of being despair and a despair that is
completely conscious of being so. Most often, the situation of a person in
despair is likely one of semi-obscurity (though, here again, with many
nuances) concerning his own condition. To a certain degree, he himself
does indeed know that he is in despair; he senses it within himself as a
person senses within himself that he is living with a sickness in his body,
but he does not truly want to admit what sickness it is. At one moment, it
almost becomes clear to him that he is in despair, but then at the next
moment it seems to him, after all, as though his indisposition has some
other cause, as though it lay in something external, in something outside of
him, and that if this were changed, he would not be in despair. Or perhaps,
by means of diversions and other methods—for example, work and
busyness as means of diversion—he seeks to preserve an obscurity
concerning his situation, though once again in such a way that it does not
become quite clear to him that he is doing what he does in order to bring
about this obscurity. Or perhaps he is even aware that he is working like this
in order to submerge his soul in obscurity, that he is doing it with a certain
perspicacity and shrewd calculation, with psychological insight; but in the
deeper sense he is not clearly aware of what he is doing, of how
despairingly he is behaving, etc. For in fact there is, in all obscurity and
ignorance, a dialectical interplay of knowledge and will, and one can err in
understanding a person by accentuating only knowledge or by accentuating
only the will.

But as has been noted earlier, the degree of consciousness intensifies
despair. The truer a person’s conception of despair—if he nonetheless
remains in it, and the more clearly conscious he is of being in despair, if he
nonetheless remains in despair—the more intense is the despair. If a person
is conscious of the fact that committing suicide is despair and, having this
true conception of what despair is, commits suicide, his despair is more
intense than that of the person who commits suicide without having the true
conception that suicide is despair; but conversely, the latter’s untrue
conception of suicide is less intense despair. On the other hand, the greater
the clarity of consciousness of himself (self-consciousness) in a person who
commits suicide, the more intense is his #[164]# despair compared with that

of a person whose soul, by comparison, is in a state of confusion and
darkness.

In what follows, I will go through the two forms of conscious despair in
a way that also points out an increase in the consciousness of what despair
is and in the consciousness that one’s condition is despair, or (what amounts
to the same thing and is of decisive importance) an increase in
consciousness of the self. But the opposite of being in despair is having
faith, and thus precisely what was set forth earlier as the formula describing
a state in which there is no despair whatsoever is also the formula for
having faith: in relating to itself, and in willing to be itself, the self is
grounded transparently in the power that established it. (See A, A.'’)

a) In despair not to will to be oneself, weakness’s despair

Calling this form of despair that of weakness already implies a reflection on
the other form (8"), in despair to will to be oneself. These oppositions are
thus only relative. No despair is entirely devoid of defiance; defiance is also
implicit in the expression itself: “not to will to be.” On the other hand, even
despair’s most intense defiance is after all never without some weakness.
The difference is thus only relative. The one form, so to speak, is feminine
despair, the other is masculine despair.*

#[165]#

1) Despair over the earthly or over something earthly

This is pure immediacy or immediacy containing in itself a quantitative
reflection. — Here there is no infinite consciousness of the self, of what
despair is, or that the condition is one of despair; despair is merely
something passive, a surrender to the pressure of external circumstances—it
does not in any way emanate from within as an action. It is only through an
—if you will—innocent misuse of language, a playing with words (as when
a child plays soldier), that words such as “the self” and “despair” appear in
the language of immediacy.

The immediate person (insofar as immediacy can exist entirely without
reflection) is qualified solely psychically; his self, and he himself, is a
Something within the bounds of the temporal and the worldly, #[166]# in

immediate continuity with the other (to etepov™ ), and has only an illusory
appearance of having something eternal within itself. In this way, the self
coheres immediately with the other—wishing, desiring, enjoying, etc., yet
passively; even in desiring, this self is in the dative case, like the child’s
“me.”!9 Its dialectic is the pleasant and the unpleasant; its concepts: good
fortune, misfortune, fate.

Now something happens, something befalls (falls upon) this immediate
self, something that causes it to despair; it cannot happen any other way, for
the self has no reflection within itself. That which brings him to despair
must come from without, and the despair is a suffering, something solely
passive. That in which the immediate person has his life or, insofar as he
has a little bit of reflection within himself, the portion of it to which he is
especially attached, is taken from him “by a stroke of fate.” In short, he
becomes, as he says, #[167]# unhappy—that is, the immediacy within him
has received such a blow that it cannot reproduce itself: he despairs. Or
(and this is something that is not often seen in actuality, but dialectically is
quite in order) this, the despair of immediacy, makes its entry owing to what
the immediate person calls an altogether too great a stroke of good fortune;
immediacy, as such, is indeed something terribly frail, and every quid
nimis" that requires reflection of it brings it to despair.

So he despairs—that is, with an odd backwardness and totally mystified
with respect to himself, he calls it despair. But to despair is to lose the
eternal—and of this loss he of course does not speak, he does not dream of
it. In itself, to lose the earthly is not to despair, and yet this is what he talks
about, and this is what he calls despairing. What he says is true in a certain
sense, but it is not true in the way he understands it—he is turned around,
and what he says must be understood backward: he stands and points at
what is not despair, explaining that he is in despair, and in the meantime, it
is quite true that despair is going on behind him and he does not realize it. It
is as if someone stood with his back to the city hall and courthouse,”
pointed straight ahead, and said: “There is the city hall and courthouse.”
That person is correct, it is right there—if he turns around. He is not in
despair, it is not true—and yet he is right in saying it. But he says he is in
despair, he regards himself as dead, as a shadow of himself. Yet he is not
dead—there is still, if you will, life in the person. If suddenly everything—

everything external—were changed and his wish were fulfilled, then there
would once again be life in him, then immediacy would arise once again,
and he would begin life anew. But this is the only way that immediacy
knows how to fight, the only thing it knows: to despair and to swoon—and
yet it knows least of all what despair is. It despairs and swoons and then it
lies completely still as though it were dead, like the trick known as “playing
dead”; immediacy is like certain lower orders of animals that have no other
weapons or means of defense than that of lying completely still and
pretending to be dead.

Meanwhile, time passes. If external help arrives, life also returns to the
despairing person; he begins where he left off: a self he was not and a self
he did not become, but now he goes on living, #[168]# solely in the
categories of immediacy. In actual life, if no external help arrives, most
often something else happens. Then life returns to the person after all, but
he says that “he will never be himself again.” Now he acquires a bit of
common sense about life, he learns to mimic other people, the way they get
on with life—and now, that is how he lives as well. In Christendom he is
also a Christian, goes to church every Sunday, listens to and understands the
priest—indeed, they understand one another; he dies; for ten rixdollars, the
priest ushers him into eternity*!—but a self he was not, a self he did not
become.

This form of despair is: in despair not to will to be oneself; or, still
lower: in despair not to will to be a self; or, lowest of all: in despair to will
to be someone other than oneself, to wish for a new self. Immediacy really
has no self, it does not know itself, thus neither can it recognize itself and
therefore usually ends up in fantasy. When immediacy despairs, it does not
even have enough self to wish or to dream that it had in fact become what it
did not become. The immediate person helps himself in another way: he
wishes to be someone else. One can easily ascertain that this is the case by
observing immediate people: in the moment of despair, they wish for
nothing so much as that they had become, or that they could become,
someone else. In any case, one can never keep from smiling at a despairing
person such as this, who, humanly speaking, despite being in despair, is so
very innocent. Frequently, a despairing person such as this is infinitely
comical. Imagine a self (and, next to God, there is nothing so eternal as a

self), and then imagine that a self comes up with the notion concerning
whether it might not be possible to become someone other—than itself.
And yet, a despairing person such as this—whose sole wish is for this, the
maddest of all mad transformations—he loves, he loves the delusion that
the change could take place as easily as putting on a different outfit. For the
immediate person does not know himself, he knows himself only, quite
literally, from his outfit, he knows what it is to have a self (and here, again,
is something infinitely comical) from external appearances. It is not easy to
find an error more ludicrous than this, for a self is indeed infinitely different
from external appearances. Then, when all external appearances have
changed for the immediate person, and he has despaired—then he goes a
step further, he thinks as follows, and it becomes his wish: “What if I
became someone else, got myself a new self?” Yes, what if he #[169]#
became someone else—I wonder whether he would recognize himself.
They tell the story of a peasant who had come to the capital bare-legged and
made enough money to buy himself a pair of shoes and stockings and still
had enough left over to get drunk—they say that when, in his drunken state,
he went to find his way home, he lay down in the middle of the road and
fell asleep. Then a wagon came along, and the driver shouted to him, telling
him to move or he would drive over his legs. The drunken peasant woke up,
looked at his legs, and when he did not recognize them because of the shoes
and stockings, he said: “Just go ahead, they’re not my legs.” That is how it
is with the immediate person when he despairs: it is impossible to present
him truthfully without some comedy—if I may say so, it takes some artistry
merely to speak in the jargon about a self and about despair.

When immediacy is assumed to have some reflection within itself, the
despair is somewhat modified; there is more consciousness of the self, and
thus in turn, of what despair is and of the fact that a person’s condition is
one of despair. It makes some sense for such a person to speak of being in
despair, but the despair is essentially that of weakness, a suffering; its form
is: in despair not to will to be oneself.

The progress, in comparison with pure immediacy, is immediately
visible here in the fact that the despair does not always come about as a
consequence of a blow, of something happening, but can be brought about
by the very reflection within oneself, so that the despair, if it is present, is
not merely a passive suffering and a submission to external circumstances,

but is to some degree self-activity, action. Here there is indeed a certain
degree of reflection, that is, a certain degree of consideration of oneself; this
degree of reflection upon oneself marks the beginning of the act of
separation through which the self becomes aware of itself as essentially
different from the surrounding world and external circumstances and from
their influence upon the self. But this is only to a certain degree. When,
with a certain degree of reflection within itself, the self wills to proceed in
taking charge of the self, it perhaps stumbles against one or another
difficulty in the composition of the self, in the self’s necessity. For just as
no human body is perfect, neither is any self. Whatever this difficulty may
be, he recoils from it. Or something befalls him that breaks more
profoundly with the immediacy within him than he has done by means of
his reflection. Or his #[170]# imagination discovers a possibility which, if it
made its appearance, would thereby become the rupture with immediacy.

So he despairs. In contrast to the despair of self-assertion, his despair is
that of weakness, a suffering of the self. But, assisted by the relative
reflection he has within himself, he makes—and in this, once again,
different from the purely immediate person—an attempt to protect his self.
He understands that this letting go of the self is indeed an exchange in
which some things are gained and others are lost; the battle does not put
him in an apoplectic state as it does with the immediate person; helped by
reflection, he understands that there is much he can lose without losing the
self. He makes concessions—he is capable of doing so—and why? Because
he has to some extent separated his self from external circumstances,
because he has an obscure notion that there might even be something
eternal in the self. But in doing this he is fighting in vain; the difficulty he
has run up against requires a break with the whole of immediacy, and he has
neither the self-reflection nor the ethical reflection required for that. He has
no consciousness of a self that is won through infinite abstraction from
everything external—this self, unlike the clothed self of immediacy, this
naked, abstract self, which is the first form of the infinite self and is what
propels forward the entire process whereby a self infinitely takes possession
of its actual self, with its difficulties and its advantages.

So he despairs, and his despair is: not to will be himself. On the other
hand, what definitely does not occur to him is the ridiculous notion of
wanting to be someone else; he maintains the relation to his self: to that

extent, reflection has bound him to the self. His relation to the self, then, is
similar to how the situation can be with a man in relation to the place where
he lives (what is comical is that the relation of the self to itself is certainly
not in any way nearly as detached as a man’s relation to the place where he
lives), which becomes repulsive to him because of the presence of smoke or
for whatever reason; then he leaves it, but he does not move away, he does
not sign a new lease, he still regards the old place as his dwelling; he
assumes the problem will pass. This is also how it is with the person in
despair. As long as this difficulty persists, he does not dare (as the
expression so pointedly puts it) come to himself, does not want to be
himself. But surely this will pass, perhaps things will change, the dark
possibility will surely be forgotten. As long as this persists, he therefore, so
to speak, pays a visit to himself only every so often to see whether the
change has occurred, and as #[171]# soon as it occurs, he moves home
again, “is himself once again,” as he says, though this means only that he
begins where he left off—thus, he was a self to a certain degree, and he
became nothing more than that.

But if no change takes place, he helps himself in another way. He turns
entirely away from the inward direction, the path along which he should
have advanced in order truly to become a self. In the deeper sense, the
entire question of the self turns into a sort of false door in the background of
his soul, behind which there is nothing. He appropriates what he in his
language calls his self, that is, whatever abilities, talents, etc., may have
been granted him; all this he appropriates, though in the outward direction,
toward, as it is called, life, the actual, the active life; he associates very
cautiously with the little bit of reflection he has within himself—he fears
that it might emerge again, that thing in the background. So he gradually
succeeds in forgetting it; as the years pass, he finds it almost ludicrous,
especially when he is in the good company of other capable and active men
who bring with them sense and competence for real life. Charming! At this
point, as they write in novels, he has already been happily married for a
number of years, is an active and enterprising man, father, and citizen,
perhaps even a great man; at home in his house, the servants call him
“Himself”; in the city, he is among the distinguished citizens; his conduct
shows that he is respected as a person or with the respect for someone who
is, to judge from all appearances, a person. In Christendom he is a Christian

(in entirely the same sense as he would be a pagan in the pagan world and
in Holland a Dutchman), one of the cultured Christians. He has often
concerned himself with the question of immortality, and he has more than
once asked the priest whether there is such an immortality, whether one
would actually recognize oneself again—something in which he must of
course take particular interest, for he hasn’t any self.

It is impossible to depict a despair of this sort truthfully without a
certain addition of satire. What is comical is that he wants to speak of
having been in despair; what is frightful is that after having, as he would
have it, overcome despair, his condition is precisely one of despair. What is
infinitely comical is this: that the basis of this life-wisdom that is praised so
highly in the world, that the basis of all this devil’s abundance of good
counsel and words of wisdom and waiting-and-seeing and putting-up-with-
one’s-fate and forgetting-about-it—that, ideally understood, the basis of all
this is to be found in total ignorance #[172]# of where the danger really lies,
of what the danger really is. But this ethical stupidity is, in turn, what is
frightful.

Despair over the earthly or over something earthly is the most common
form of despair, and especially under the second form: immediacy with a
quantitative reflection. The more thoroughly reflected despair is, the more
rarely it is seen or is found in the world. But this proves that most people
have not indeed gone particularly deep in despairing, though by no means
does it prove that they are not in despair. There are very few people who
even approximate living in the category of spirit; indeed, there are not even
very many who make an attempt at this life, and of those who do, most soon
fall by the wayside. They have not learned to fear, not learned to “have to,”
regardless, infinitely regardless, of whatever else happens. Therefore they
cannot endure what seems even to themselves to be a contradiction, which
in the reflection of the surrounding world appears to be so much, much
more glaring: that to the world, being concerned for one’s soul and wanting
to be spirit looks like a waste of time, indeed, like an indefensible waste of
time that ought, if possible, to be punishable by law, and in any case should
be punished with disdain and mockery as a species of treason against
humanity, as a defiant madness that insanely fills up time with nothing.
Then there comes a moment in their lives—alas, this is their best time—
when they begin, after all, to direct their attention inward. Then they come

to what is more or less the first difficulty. There they turn aside—it seems to
them as though the road led to a bleak desert—und rings umher liegt schone
griine Weide.' Then they go off and soon forget these, their best days,
forgetting them, alas, as if they were something childish. They are also
Christians—reassured by the priests regarding the matter of their salvation.
As has been noted, this is the most widespread form of despair; it is so
widespread that one could explain, on that basis alone, the view, widely
held in everyday discourse, that despair is supposedly a property of youth,
present only in early years, but not found in the adult man who has reached
the age of discretion and maturity. This is a desperate misapprehension, or
more correctly, a desperate mistake, which overlooks—yes, and what is
even worse, it overlooks the fact that what it overlooks is almost the best
thing that can be said about a person, because what most often happens is
something much worse—it overlooks the fact that most people, throughout
their entire lives, fundamentally never come any further than #[173]# being
what they were in their childhood and youth: immediacy with the addition
of a dash of reflection. No, despair is truly not something that appears only
among the young, something one simply outgrows—“as one outgrows an
illusion,” which of course is not something people do, even if they are
foolish enough to think so. On the contrary, one quite often encounters men,
women, and elderly people who have childish illusions as much as those of
any youth. But people overlook the fact that illusion has essentially two
forms: that of hope, and that of recollection. The youth has the illusion of
hope. The older person has that of recollection, but precisely because he is
in an illusion, he also has the entirely one-sided notion that the only illusion
is that of hope. And it is clear that the older person is not plagued by the
illusion of hope, but on the contrary, he is plagued, among other things, by
the queer illusion of looking down upon the illusions of youth from a
supposedly higher standpoint that is without illusions. The youth is in an
illusion: he hopes for the extraordinary, both from life and from himself. On
the other hand, with the elderly person one often finds illusions in the way
he remembers his youth. In her memories of herself as a young girl, of how
happy she was at the time, how beautiful, etc., an older woman, who now
has supposedly given up all illusions, is found to entertain fantastic illusions
as much as any young girl does. This fuimus,' which is so often heard from

older people, is fully as great an illusion as the youth’s illusion concerning
the future: both of them lie or poetize.

But far more desperate is the erroneous view that despair belongs only
to youth. Altogether, the belief that things go quite easily in connection with
faith and wisdom, that they actually show up more or less as a matter of
course as one grows older—like teeth, a beard, and so forth—is enormously
foolish and is a misunderstanding of what spirit is, as well as a failure to
appreciate the fact that a human being is spirit and not merely an animal
creature. No, whatever a human being comes to as a matter of course, and
whatever comes as a matter of course, faith and wisdom definitely do not.
But the fact is this: as the years pass, a human being, spiritually understood,
does not come to anything as a matter of course—that category is precisely
the spirit’s most diametrical opposite; rather, as the years pass, it is very
easy to leave something behind. And as the years pass, a person may
perhaps leave behind the little bit of passion, feeling, and imagination, the
fragment of inwardness, he possessed and enter, as a matter of course (for
such things do come as a matter of course) into the trivial definition of the
sensible life. This . . . improved situation, which indeed has come about as #
[174]# the years passed, he now, in his despair, regards as something good;
he easily assures himself (and in a certain satirical sense, nothing is more
certain) that now it could never occur to him to despair—no, he has
protected himself, he is in despair, spiritlessly' in despair. Why, I wonder,
did Socrates love youth, if not because he knew humanity!

And if a person does not sink into the most trivial sort of despair as the
years pass, it does not by any means follow from this that despair is the sole
province of youth. If a person actually develops over the years, if he
matures in his essential consciousness regarding the self, then he may
perhaps despair in a higher form. And as the years pass, if he does not
develop essentially and does not simply sink into triviality—that is, if he
remains more or less as he was as a young man, a youth, in spite of being a
man, a father, and a graybeard—then, while preserving some of the good
qualities of youth, he will of course also be vulnerable to despairing, as a
youth does, over the earthly or over something earthly.

There can indeed be a difference between the despair of an older person
such as this and that of a youth, but only in purely accidental respects, not

essentially. The youth despairs over what is to come, as a present in futuro"!
there is something in what is to come that he does not want to make his
own, he does not want to be himself with it. The elderly person despairs of
what is past as a present in praeterito!!' that refuses to recede further and
further into the past—for he is not, after all, so much in despair that he has
succeeded in forgetting it entirely. This past is perhaps even something that
repentance should really take hold of. But if repentance is to emerge, he
must first despair completely, despair utterly, so that the life of the spirit
might break through from the ground up. But in despair as he is, he does not
dare let it come to such a decision. So he remains standing there, time
passes—unless, in even greater despair and assisted by forgetfulness, he
succeeds in making things whole, so that instead of becoming a penitent, he
becomes the recipient of his own stolen goods.*? Essentially, however, the
despair of such a youth and of such an elderly person is the same; no
metamorphosis arrives in which consciousness of the eternal in the self
breaks through so that there might be a start to the battle that either
intensifies the despair into a still higher form or leads to faith.

But then, is there no essential difference between the two expressions
which up to now have been used identically: to despair over the earthly (the
category of totality), #[175]# and to despair over something earthly (the
particular)? Indeed there is. When, in imagination, the self despairs with
infinite passion over something earthly, the infinite passion makes this
particular, this something, into the earthly in toto;! this means that the
category of totality is inherent in and belongs to the despairing person. The
earthly and the temporal, as such, are precisely what fall out from one
another into something, into the particular. It is actually impossible to lose
or be deprived of everything earthly, for the concept of totality is a category
of thought. Thus it is the self that first magnifies the actual loss infinitely,
and then comes to despair over the earthly in toto. But as soon as this
distinction (between despairing over the earthly and despairing over
something earthly) is to be essentially set forth, there is also an essential
advance in consciousness of the self. This formula, to despair over the
earthly, is thus a dialectical initial expression of the next form of despair.

2) Despair of the eternal or over oneself

Despair over the earthly or over something earthly is indeed really also
despair of the eternal and over oneself, insofar as it is despair, for this is
indeed the formula for all despair.* But the despairing person, as he was
presented in the preceding pages, was not aware #[176]# of what was, so to
speak, taking place behind him; he thinks he is despairing over something
earthly and constantly talks about what it is he is despairing over. And yet
he is despairing of the eternal, for the fact that he attributes so much value
to the earthly—or to be more specific, the fact that he attributes so much
value to something earthly, or that he first makes something earthly into
everything earthly and then attributes so much value to the earthly—is of
course precisely to despair of the eternal.

Now this despair is a significant step forward. If the preceding despair
was in weakness, this form is despair over one’s weakness, while still
remaining within the category: weakness’s despair—as distinct from
(defiance). There is thus only a relative difference, which is that the
preceding form has weakness’s consciousness as its final consciousness,
whereas in this case consciousness does not stop at that point, but
intensifies itself into a new consciousness, consciousness of its weakness.
The despairing person himself understands that it is weakness to be so
affected by the earthly, that it is weakness to despair. But then, instead of
truly turning away from despair and toward faith, humbling himself before
God under his weakness, he immerses himself in despair and despairs over
his weakness. Thereby, his entire point of view is turned around: he now
becomes more acutely conscious of his despair, conscious that he despairs
of the eternal; he despairs over himself that he could be so weak as to
attribute such great significance to the earthly—which for him now
becomes the despairing expression of the fact that he has lost the eternal
and himself.

Here is the ascent. First of all, in the consciousness of the self, for it is
indeed impossible to despair of the eternal without having a conception of
the self, that there is something eternal in it or that it has had something
eternal in it. And if one is to despair over oneself, one must oneself also be
conscious of having a self; yet this is what he despairs over, not over the
earthly or something earthly, but over himself. Moreover, here there is
greater consciousness of what despair is, for despair consists precisely in

having lost the eternal and oneself. There is, of course, also greater
consciousness that one’s condition is one of despair. Furthermore, here
despair is not only a passive suffering, but an action. For when the earthly is
taken away from the self and he despairs, it is as if the despair came from
without, even though it always comes from the self; but when the self
despairs over this, over its own despair, then this new despair comes from
the self, indirectly-directly from the self, as a counterpressure (reaction),
and, in this, it differs from defiance, which comes #[177]# directly from the
self. Finally, here there is in fact yet another step forward, albeit in another
sense—for precisely because this despair is more intensive, it is in a certain
sense closer to salvation. A despair such as this is difficult to forget—it is
too deep; but at every instant the despair is held open, there is also a
possibility of salvation.

But nonetheless, this despair is to be classified under the form: in
despair not to will to be oneself. As when a father disinherits his son, the
self does not want to acknowledge itself after having been so weak. In its
despair, it cannot forget this weakness; in a way, it hates itself; it will not
humble itself in faith under its weakness in order to in that way regain itself
—no, in despair, it, so to speak, does not want to hear anything about itself,
does not want to have anything to do with itself, so to speak. But neither
can there be any question of being helped through forgetting, nor, with the
assistance of forgetting, of slipping in under the category of spiritlessness
and thus become a man and a Christian like other men and Christians—no,
the self is too much self for that. As indeed it often went with the father
who disinherited his son, the outward fact was of but scant help to him—by
having done it, he did not get free of his son, at least not in his thoughts. As
often happens with the lover’s curse upon the hated one (that is, upon the
beloved): it does not help much, it almost imprisons one even more—that is
how things go with the despairing self regarding itself.

This despair is a qualitative level deeper than the one described
previously, and is a despair of the sort that is found less often in the world.
That false door spoken of earlier, behind which there was nothing, is here
an actual door—but, to be sure, a very carefully closed door—behind which
sits the self, as it were, looking after itself, busy with—or passing the time
with—not willing to be itself, and yet it is self enough to love itself. This is
what is called self-enclosure. And from here on, we will be dealing with

self-enclosure, which is diametrically opposed to immediacy, and—in a
number of areas, including the realm of thought—has enormous contempt
for it.

But then, does not such a self exist in actuality—has he fled beyond the
actual, into the desert, the monastery, the madhouse? Is he not an actual
human being, dressed like other people or, like others, clad in the customary
outer garments? Oh, of course, why not? But he shares this self with no one,
not a soul. He feels no need to do so, or he has learned to repress such a
need—just listen when he himself speaks of it: “Indeed, #[178]# only
absolutely immediate people—who from the point of view of spirit are
situated at about the same point as the child in the earliest stage of early
childhood, when with utterly lovable unconstraint, it lets everything come
out—it is only absolutely immediate people who are utterly incapable of
holding back. This is the sort of immediacy which with great pretensions
often calls itself “Truth, being true, a true person, and entirely as one is’>—
which indeed is just as true as it is untrue that an older person gives way the
instant he feels a bodily urge. Every self that is even the least bit reflective
has, after all, at least some notion of restraining the self.” And our
despairing person is thus sufficiently self-enclosed to be able to keep
everyone whom it does not concern—that is, everyone—away from this
matter of the self, while outwardly he is entirely “a real person.” He is an
academically educated man, a husband, a father, even an unusually capable
public official, a respectable father, pleasant to be with, very sweet to his
wife, carefulness itself with his children. And a Christian? —well, yes, he is
that, too, in a way—however, he prefers to avoid talking about it, even if he
is happy to see, with a certain wistful joy, that his wife occupies herself
edifyingly with godly things. He goes to church very rarely because it
seems to him that most of the priests don’t really know what they are
talking about. He makes an exception with one particular priest who, he
grants, does know what he is talking about, but there is another reason he
does not want to listen to him: he fears that it might lead him too far. On the
other hand, he not infrequently feels a need for solitude—for him it is a
vital necessity, at some times like breathing, at others like sleep. The fact
that he, more than most people, has this vital necessity is thus another sign
that he possesses a more profound nature. Generally speaking, the need for
solitude indicates that there is at any rate some spirit in a person and is the

measure for whatever there is of spirit. “The utterly frivolous non-persons
and fellowmen” feel so little need for solitude that, like lovebirds, they die
immediately if they must be alone for an instant; as the little child is lulled
to sleep, these people need the tranquilizing lull of sociability in order to be
able to eat, drink, sleep, pray, fall in love, etc. But in antiquity as well as in
the Middle Ages, people were aware of this need for solitude and had
respect for what it signifies. In the perpetual sociability** of our times,
people shrink back from #[179]# solitude to such an extent that people
(what a splendid epigram!) have no idea of what to use it for other than as a
punishment for criminals.7° It is true, in fact, that in our times it is indeed a
crime to possess spirit, so it is of course fitting that such people, the lovers
of solitude, should be classed with criminals.

The self-enclosed despairing person thus lives on, horis successivis,!
in hours which, even if they are not lived for eternity, nonetheless have
something to do with the eternal, concerned with his self’s relation to itself.
But he does not really get any further. Then, when this has been done, when
the need for solitude has been satisfied, then, as it were, he goes out—even
when he goes in or occupies himself with his wife and his children. Aside
from his natural good nature and his sense of obligation, what makes him so
gentle as a husband, so solicitous as a father, is the admission he has made
to himself, deepest within himself, concerning his weakness.

Were it possible for someone to become his confidant in his self-
enclosure, and were someone then to say to him, “Of course, it is pride, you
really are proud of yourself,” he certainly would scarcely confess that to
anyone else. When he was alone with himself, he would probably confess
that there was something to it, but the vehemence with which his self had
understood his weakness would soon cause him to imagine once again that
it was impossible that it could be pride, for of course he was despairing over
his weakness—as if it were not pride that placed such enormous emphasis
on the weakness, as if it were not because he wanted to be proud of himself
for being unable to endure this consciousness of weakness. — If one were
to say to him: “This is a curious complication, a curious sort of knot, for the
entire misfortune really lies in the way in which thought contorts itself;
otherwise it is in fact even normal—it is precisely the path you must take,
you must go to the self through the despair of the self. Your concern with

weakness is quite proper, but that is not what you are to despair over; the
self must be broken in order to become itself, just stop despairing over it”—
if someone were to speak to him in this manner, in a dispassionate moment
he would understand it, but soon the passion would again see things
erroneously, and then he would again take the wrong turn, into despair.

As has been stated, despair of this sort is rather rare in the world. Then,
if he does not remain standing at this spot, merely marking time—and if, on
the other hand, no upheaval takes place within the despairing person, so that
he gets on the right path toward faith—then #[180]# a despair of this sort
will either intensify itself into a higher form of despair and continue to be
self-enclosure, or it will break through to the outside and destroy the
outward cloak in which a despairing person of this sort has been living, as
in an incognito. In the latter case, a despairing person of this sort will then
hurl himself into life, perhaps into the diversion of great ventures; he will
become a restless spirit whose existence leaves much in its wake, a restless
spirit who wants to forget—and when the clamor within becomes too great,
more powerful means must be employed, albeit of a different sort than
those Richard III uses in order not to hear his mother’s curses.7° Or he will
seek forgetfulness in sensuality, perhaps in debauchery; in despair, he wants
to return to immediacy, though always with the consciousness of the self he
does not want to be. In the former case, when the despair is intensified, it
becomes defiance, and now it becomes apparent how much untruth there
was in that matter of weakness; it becomes apparent how dialectically
correct it is that the first expression for defiance is precisely despair over his
weakness.

But in conclusion, let us once again take a brief look at the self-enclosed
person, who is marking time in his self-enclosure. If this self-enclosure is
maintained absolutely, omnibus numeris absoluta,! then suicide will be
the danger closest to him. Most people are such that they naturally have no
idea of what such a self-enclosed person is capable of bearing; if they found
out, they would be astonished. But, again, for the absolutely self-enclosed
person the greatest danger is suicide. On the other hand, if he speaks with
someone, if he opens himself to one single person, then in all likelihood his
state of tension will be sufficiently reduced or toned down, so that suicide
will not result from the self-enclosure. A self-enclosure such as this, in

which one confidant is included, is an entire level less severe than absolute
self-enclosure. He will thus probably avoid suicide. Though it can happen
that he despairs precisely over the fact that he has opened himself to
another, so that it seems to him that it would have been far preferable if,
instead of having had a confidant, he had held out in silence. There are
examples of this, where a self-enclosed person has been brought to despair
precisely by having had a confidant. Then suicide can be the consequence
after all. In a poetic treatment, the turning point of the plot (assuming
poetice' that the main character is a king or an emperor) could also be
fashioned so that he has the confidant put to death. One could imagine a
demonic tyrant of this sort who felt the need to speak with #[181]# someone
about his torment and who then consumed a great number of people, one
after the other, for it was certain death to be his confidant: as soon as the
tyrant had told him what he had to tell, he was put to death. — It would be a
task for a poet to present a demonic person’s agonizing self-contradiction—
to be unable to do without a confidant and unable to have a confidant—and
resolve it in this manner.

B) The despair, in despair to will to be oneself, defiance

As it was shown?’ that one could call a feminine despair, so could one call
this despair masculine. Thus, in comparison to the foregoing section, this is
despair viewed under the category of spirit. But viewed in this way,
masculinity essentially belongs under the category of spirit, while
femininity is a lower synthesis.

The despair described under « 2" was over one’s weakness: the
despairing person does not will to be himself. But if he goes a single
dialectical step further, the person who despairs in this manner then comes
to consciousness about why he does not will to be himself—then things are
reversed,® then defiance is there, for then it is precisely because he wills in
despair to be himself.

First comes despair over the earthly or over something earthly; next
comes despair of the eternal, over oneself. Then comes the defiance that is
really despair with the help of the eternal, the despairing misuse of the
eternal that is in the self in order in despair to will to be oneself. But
precisely because it is despair through the help of the eternal, it is ina

certain sense very close to the truth; and precisely because the truth is very
close at hand, it is infinitely far away. The despair that is the passageway to
faith is also through the aid of the eternal; through the aid of the eternal, the
self has the courage to lose itself in order to gain itself; here, on the other
hand, it does not want to begin by losing itself, but wills to be itself.

In this form of despair there is now an increase in consciousness of the
self, that is, a greater consciousness of what despair is and of the fact that
one’s condition is one of despair; here the despair is conscious of itself as
an act: it does not come from without as a passive suffering under the
pressure of external circumstances, it comes directly from the self. And
thus, compared to despair over one’s weakness, defiance is indeed a new -
qualification. #[182]#

To be able in despair to will to be oneself, there must be consciousness
of an infinite self. This infinite self, however, is really only the most
abstract form, the most abstract possibility of the self. And it is this self that
he despairingly wills to be, wresting the self loose from every relation to the
power that established it, or wresting it loose from the notion that such a
power exists. With the help of this infinite form, the self wills in despair to
rule over itself or to create itself, making his self into the self he wills to be,
to determine what he will and will not have in his concrete self. His
concrete self, or his concretion, of course has necessity and limitations, is
this quite definite self, with these abilities, aptitudes, etc., in this set of
concrete relations, etc. But with the help of the infinite form, the negative
self, he wants first to take upon himself the task of reshaping this whole in
order then to obtain from it a self such as he wants, produced with the
assistance of the infinite form of the negative self—and in this way he wills
to be himself. This means that he wants to begin a bit earlier than other
people, not at and with the beginning, but “in the beginning”;' he does not
want to put on his own self, does not want to see his task in the self that has
been given to him: he himself wants to construct his self, assisted by being
the infinite form.

If one wanted to have a common name for this despair, one could call it
Stoicism, though not as if one were thinking only of that sect. And in order
to illuminate this type of despair more fully, one does best to differentiate
between an active self and a passive self, and to show how the self relates to

itself when it acts, and how the self, when it is acted upon, relates to itself in
being acted upon, and that the formula always remains: in despair to will to
be oneself.

If the despairing self is an active self, it really relates to itself only in
experimenting fashion, no matter what it undertakes, however great,
however amazing, however doggedly. It acknowledges no power higher
than itself; therefore, in the final analysis, it lacks seriousness and can
conjure up an appearance of seriousness only when it itself devotes its
greatest attention to its experiments. Now, this is a sham seriousness: as
with Prometheus’s theft of fire from the gods, this is stealing from God the
thought (which is seriousness) that God takes notice of a person, instead of
which the despairing self is satisfied with taking notice of itself; this is
supposed to endow his undertakings with infinite interest and significance,
while in fact it is precisely what makes them into experiments. For even if
this self #[183]# does not go so far in despair that it becomes an
experimentally constructed god, no derived self can, by taking notice of
itself, in fact endow itself with more than it itself is: it still remains itself
from beginning to end—in reduplicating itself, it becomes neither more nor
less than the self. Insofar as the self, in its desperate effort at willing to be
itself, moves in precisely the opposite direction, it really becomes no self. In
the entire dialectic within which it acts there is nothing firm; at no moment
does what the self is stand firm—that is, eternally firm. The negative form
of the self exercises a power that is as much loosening as it is binding;! at
any moment it can quite arbitrarily begin all over again, and however long a
thought is pursued, the entire affair is within a hypothesis. So far is the self
from succeeding in becoming more and more itself, that it merely becomes
more and more apparent that it is a hypothetical self. The self is its own
master—absolutely its own master, as is said—and precisely this is the
despair, but is also what it regards as its desire, its delight. Nonetheless,
upon closer inspection one easily ascertains that this absolute ruler is a king
without a country, he actually rules over nothing; his situation, his kingdom,
is subject to the dialectic that rebellion is always legitimate. In the final
analysis, it depends arbitrarily upon the self itself.

Thus, the despairing self continually builds nothing but castles in the air
and is continually merely fencing in the air." All these experimenting

virtues make a splendid show; for a moment, they enchant like oriental
poetry; such self-control, such imperturbability, such ataraxia, etc., almost
border on fable. Yes, they truly do, and the basis of it all is in fact nothing.
In its despair, the self wants to enjoy the entire satisfaction of making itself
into itself, of developing itself, of being itself—it wants to be honored for
this poetic, masterly design, for how it has understood itself. And yet, in the
final analysis, what it understands itself to be is an enigma; at the very
instant at which it seems to be closest to having completed the building, it
can arbitrarily dissolve it all into nothing.

If the despairing self is acted upon, the despair is nonetheless: in despair
to will to be oneself. Perhaps, when it is tentatively orienting itself in its
concrete self, an experimenting self such as this, which in despair wills to
be itself, runs into one or another difficulty, something that the #[184]#
Christian would call a cross, a fundamental impairment, whatever it might
be. The negative self, the infinite form of the self, would perhaps simply
disregard this utterly, pretend that it did not exist—at any rate, have nothing
to do with it. But this does not succeed; its virtuosity in experimentation
does not reach that far; not even its virtuosity in abstraction reaches that far:
in Promethean fashion,~” the infinite, negative self feels itself to be nailed
fast to this servitude. This, then, is a self that is acted upon. How, in this
case, does this despair—in despair to will to be oneself—manifest itself?

Now, the preceding passage presented the form of despair that despairs
over the earthly or over something earthly, understood such that this
fundamentally is—and reveals itself to be—despair of the eternal, i.e., to be
unwilling to let oneself be consoled and be healed by the eternal, to value
the earthly so highly that the eternal cannot be of any consolation. But it is
also a form of despair to be unwilling to hope in the possibility that an
earthly suffering, a temporal cross, can be taken away. Now, this is
something this despairing person, who in despair wills to be himself, is
unwilling to do. If he has convinced himself that this thorn in the flesh!
(whether it is actually present or his passion makes it seem to him that it is)
gnaws so deeply that he is unable to abstract from it,* then he wills, as it
were, to make it a part of himself for eternity. So he is offended by it, or
rather, he lets it serve as the occasion to be offended at the whole of
existence: then he wills to be himself in spite of it, not be himself in spite of

it #[185]# without it (that, of course, would be to abstract from it, and that
he cannot do—or it would be movement in the direction of resignation): no,
in spite of, or in defiance of, all of existence, he wills to be himself along
with it, he wills to take it along with himself, almost defying his agony. For
to hope for the possibility of help, especially by virtue of the absurd, that
for God everything is possible:" no, that he will not do. And seek help from
someone else? No, he would not do that for all the world—rather than seek
help, he would prefer to be himself with all the torments of hell (if that was
how it had to be).

And truly, what they say—“that it is obvious that a person who suffers
would willingly accept help if only someone could help him”—is far from
being true, even if the alternative examples are not always as despairing as
in this case. This is the how things stand: A person who suffers generally
has one or more ways in which he could wish to be helped. If he can be
helped in that fashion, yes, he will gladly accept help. But when, in the
deepest sense, one is to be helped in earnest, especially by someone higher,
or by that which is highest of all—this, the humiliation of having to accept
help unconditionally in every way, of having to become as if nothing in the
hand of “the helper”! for whom all things are possible, or merely of having
to give way to another person, of having to give up being oneself as long as
one is seeking help: ah, there is certainly much suffering, even protracted
and agonizing suffering, from which the self indeed does not shrink as it
does from this, and which, if it is permitted to continue being itself, the self
therefore fundamentally prefers.

But the more consciousness there is in a such a suffering person who in
despair wills to be himself, the more the despair also intensifies itself and
becomes demonic. Its origin is usually as follows: a self who in despair
wills to be itself is in agony stemming from one or another pain that cannot
in fact be taken away or be separated from his concrete self. It is precisely
at this agony that he then directs the whole of his passion, which finally
becomes a demonic rage. Now, even if God in heaven and all the angels
offered to help him out of it—no, he would not have it, now it is too late. At
one time, he would gladly have given everything to be free of this agony.
He had been compelled to wait. Now it is after the fact: now, now he would
rather rage against everything—he would rather be that one person in the

whole world who was unjustly treated by life, for whom it is of specific
importance to make sure that he has his agony ready at hand, that no one
takes it from him—for in that case he would of course be unable to prove to
others and to convince himself that he is right. This, finally, lodges itself so
firmly in his head #[186]# that he fears eternity for a quite peculiar reason,
namely, that it would deprive him of his (demonically understood) infinite
advantage over other people: his (demonically understood) justification for
being the person he is. — Himself is what he wills to be; he began with the
infinite abstraction of the self, and now he has finally become so concrete
that it would be impossible to become eternal in that sense, and yet he wills
in despair to be himself. Ah, demonic madness: he rages most at the thought
that eternity could think of depriving him of his misery.

Despair of this sort is rarely seen in the world; such figures really
appear only in the poets, that is, the real poets, who always endow their
creations with “demonic” ideality (this term understood in the purely Greek
sense). Nonetheless, such despair also occurs in real life. What, then, is the
externality that corresponds to it? Well, there is nothing that “corresponds,”
since an externality that corresponds to self-enclosure is a self-
contradiction, for if it corresponds, it of course discloses. But here
externality is a matter of complete indifference, here, where the self-
enclosure—or what one could call an inwardness with a lock that has been
jammed—is so clearly the feature that must command our attention. The
lowest forms of despair, in which there was not really any inwardness—and
concerning which, in any case, there is nothing to be said—the lowest
forms of despair might be presented by describing or by saying something
about the external characteristics of such a despairing person. But the more
spiritual the despair, the more, in its self-enclosure, inwardness becomes a
world unto itself, the more inconsequential are the external things under
which the despair conceals itself. But precisely the more spiritual despair
becomes, the more it itself is careful to employ demonic shrewdness in
keeping the despair confined in self-enclosure, and therefore the more intent
it is on making external things a matter of indifference, making them as
insignificant and indifferent as possible. As in fairy tales, when the troll
disappears through a crevice that no one can see, so, too, with despair: the
more spiritual it is, the more important it is for it to live in something
external behind which it ordinarily would never occur to anyone to search

for it. This concealment is indeed something spiritual and is among the
protective measures taken to ensure having, as it were, behind actuality, an
enclosure, a world exclusively”? for oneself, #[187]# a world in which the
despairing self is restlessly and tantalizing engaged in willing to be itself.

We began (a 1') with the lowest form of despair: in despair not to will to
be oneself. Demonic despair is the most intensified form of the despair,
which in despair wills to be itself. This despair does not even will to be
itself in stoical infatuation with itself and self-deification; it does not will to
be itself in a manner that is admittedly mendacious, but that nonetheless is
in a certain sense in accordance with its perfection; no, it wills to be itself in
hatred of existence, to be itself in accordance with its misery; it does not
even will to be itself in defiance, or defiantly, but to be itself in spite; it does
not even will in defiance to wrest itself free of the power that established it
— it spitefully wills to force itself upon it, to thrust itself upon it spite, wills
to cling to it out of malice—and it is clear that a malicious objection must
of course also take every precaution in sticking to that to which it is
objecting. In rebelling against the whole of existence, it believes that it has
found proof against it, against its goodness. The despairing person believes
that he himself is this proof, and that is what he wills to be, that is why he
wills to be himself, himself in his agony, in order, with this agony, to protest
against the whole of existence. As the weak, despairing person will not hear
anything of the consolation eternity has for him, neither will a despairing
person of the above sort hear anything about it, but for a different reason:
this very consolation would indeed be his downfall—as an objection against
the whole of existence. To describe it figuratively, it is as if an author had
made a slip of the pen and that this error became conscious of itself as such
—perhaps, in fact, it had not really been a slip of the pen, but in a far higher
sense an essential component of the entire presentation—and now it is as if
this slip of the pen wanted to rebel against the author, preventing him, out
of hatred, from making the correction, saying to him, in maniacal defiance:
“No! I will not be erased, I will stand as a witness against you, a witness to
the fact that you are a mediocre author.”

DESPAIR IS SIN

i is: before God, or with the conception of God, in despair not to will to

be oneself, or in despair to will to be oneself. Sin is thus intensified
weakness or intensified defiance: sin is the intensification of despair. The
point of emphasis is: before God, or that the conception of God is included;
that which makes sin, dialectically, ethically, religiously, into what jurists
call “aggravated” despair is the conception of God.

Even though the present part, and least of all section A, is not the place
for psychological sketches (nor is there room for such), mention may
nonetheless be made here of the most dialectical boundary area between
despair and sin—of what could be called a poet-existence tending toward
the religious, an existence that has something in common with the despair
of resignation, except that the conception of God is present in it. As can be
seen from the conjunction and position of the categories, a poet-existence of
this sort will be a poet-existence of the most eminent sort. From a Christian
point of view, every poet-existence (all aesthetics notwithstanding) is sin,
the sin of poetizing instead of being, of relating oneself to the good and the
true by way of imagination instead of being it—that is, existentially striving
to be it. The poet-existence with which we here concern ourselves differs
from despair in that it includes the conception of God, or that it is before
God; but it is enormously dialectical, as if there were an impenetrable
dialectical tangle concerning the degree to which it is obscurely conscious
of being sin. A poet of this sort can have a very deep religious craving, and
the conception of God is incorporated in his despair. He loves God above

everything, God who is his only consolation in his secret agony, and #
[192]# yet he loves the agony—he will not let go of it. He wants very much
to be himself before God, but not with respect to the fixed point concerning
which the self suffers—there, in despair, he does not will to be himself; he
hopes that eternity will remove it, and here in the temporal realm, no matter
how much he suffers under it, he cannot resolve to take it upon himself and
humble himself under it in faith. And yet he continues to relate himself to
God, and this is his sole salvation; to have to do without God would be the
greatest of terrors for him—‘“it would be something to despair over”; and
yet he actually permits himself (though perhaps unconsciously) to alter God
poetically into something a little other than what God is, a bit more of a
fond father who is altogether too indulgent in granting the child’s—only
wish. As a person unhappy in love became a poet blissfully praising the
happiness of love, he became the poet of religiousness. He became unhappy
in religiousness; he understands obscurely that it is required of him that he
let go of this agony—that is, that he humble himself under it in faith and
take it upon himself as a part of the self—for he wants to distance it from
himself, and precisely in doing so, he clings to it, even though he indeed
believes (something that, like every word from a person in despair, is
correct—in reverse—and thus must be understood backward) that this must
mean that he is to separate himself from it, to escape from it, as much as is
possible for a person. But to take it upon himself in faith, this is something
he cannot do, that is, this is something that, in the final analysis, he does not
want to do—or, this is where his self ends in obscurity. But as with the
aforementioned poet’s description of love, so does this poet’s description of
the religious possess an enchantment, a lyrical flight such as is possessed by
no married man and no reverend. Nor is what he says untrue, by any means:
what he presents is simply his happier, his better “I.” In relation to the
religious he is an unhappy lover; that is, he is not a believer in strict sense;
he possesses only the first part of faith: despair—and in it a fervent longing
for the religious. His conflict is really this: Has he been called? Does the
thorn in the flesh mean that he is to be used for the extraordinary? Before
God, is it entirely in order that he has become this extraordinary person? Or
is the thorn in the flesh! something under which he is to humble himself in
order to attain what is universally human? — But enough of this, I can say

with the emphasis of truth: To whom am I speaking? Such psychological
investigations to the nth degree—who bothers about them? People are
better able to understand the Niimberg pictures! painted by the priest; #
[193]# they bear a deceptive resemblance to anyone and everyone, to
people as they are in general and, spiritually understood, to nothing.

Chapter One

THE GRADATIONS IN THE
CONSCIOUSNESS OF THE SELF

(THE QUALIFICATION: BEFORE GOD)

Te preceding part continuously pointed out increasing levels in the

consciousness of the self. First came unconsciousness of having an eternal
self (C. B. a.'), then knowledge of having a self in which there is at any rate
something eternal (C. B. b.'), and then, gradations were in turn pointed out
under this rubric (a. 1-2. B.'"). This entire consideration must now be
turned dialectically in a new direction. This is the situation: the gradations
within the consciousness of the self with which we have concerned
ourselves thus far lie within the category of the human self, or the self
whose standard of measure! is the human being. But this self takes on a
new quality and qualification by being a self directly before God. This self
is no longer the merely human self but is what—in the hope of not being
misunderstood—lI would call the theological self, the self directly before

God. And what infinite reality the self acquires by being conscious of itself
as existing directly before God, by becoming a human self whose standard
of measure is God! A cowhand who (if this were possible) is a self directly
before cattle is a very low self; similarly, a ruler who is a self directly before
slaves is really no self—for in both cases the criterion is lacking. When he
becomes a man, the child (who previously had only his parents as a
criterion) becomes a self by acquiring the state as his criterion; but what an
infinite emphasis falls upon the self by acquiring God as a criterion! The
criterion for the self is always that directly before which it is a self, but of
course this in turn is the definition of what a “criterion” is. Just as one can
add like things only to like, so is every thing qualitatively that by which it is
measured; and that which, qualitatively, is its criterion, is, ethically, its
goal;* and the criterion and the goal are, qualitatively, what a thing is—
though this does not apply to the way things are in the world of freedom,
where, if a person has failed to be qualitatively that which is his goal and
his criterion, he must have brought this disqualification upon himself, so #
[194]# that with respect to passing judgment, the goal and the criterion
remain the same, making it clear what he is not: namely, that which is his
goal and criterion.

It was a very correct notion to which, in earlier periods, dogmatics
frequently resorted (while more recent dogmatics have objected to
dogmatics because dogmatics did not understand it or have a sense for it)—
it was a very correct notion, even if it was occasionally applied improperly:
that what made sin into something frightful was the fact that it is before
God.? It was on this basis that they then proved that punishment in hell was
eternal. Later, people became clever and said: “Sin is sin; sin is not greater
because it is against God or before God.” How odd! even jurists speak of
aggravated crimes, even jurists make a distinction concerning whether, for
example, a crime is committed against a government official or against a
private citizen; they make a distinction between the punishment for murder
of one’s father and for ordinary murder.

No, the older dogmatics were right in saying that sin was infinitely
intensified by the fact that it was against God. What was mistaken was that
people regarded God as something external and that they appeared to
assume that only some sins were against God. But God is not something

external in the sense that a police officer is. What must be noted is that the
self has a conception of God and that it nonetheless does not will as he wills
and thus is disobedient. Nor is it the case that it is only now and then that
sin is before God,* for every sin is before God, or, more correctly, that
which really makes human guilt into sin is that the guilty person has the
consciousness of existing before God.

Despair is intensified in proportion to consciousness of the self; but the
self is intensified in proportion to the criterion for the self and is intensified
infinitely when God is the criterion. The greater the conception of God, the
more self; the more self, the greater the conception of God. Only when a
self, as this specific individual self, is itself conscious of existing before
God, only then is it the infinite self; and this self then sins before God.
Therefore, despite everything that can be said about it, the selfishness of
paganism is not nearly as aggravated as that of Christendom, insofar as
selfishness can in fact be found there, for the pagan did not have his self
directly before God. The pagan and the natural human being have the
merely human self as their criterion. Thus, from a higher point of view, one
can certainly be correct in regarding the pagan world as lying in sin, but
paganism’s sin was really despairing #[195]# ignorance about God, about
existing before God; it is: “to be without God in the world.”! From another
perspective, it is therefore true that in the strictest sense, the pagan did not
sin because he did not sin before God, and all sin is before God. Moreover,
in a sense it is also quite certain that, many a time, a pagan has been helped
to slip through the world irreproachably simply because he was rescued by
his frivolous Pelagian notion;° but then his sin was something else: that
frivolous Pelagian notion. On the other hand, viewed from another angle, it
is also quite certain that in many cases, precisely by having had a strict
Christian upbringing, a person has in a certain sense been plunged into sin
because the entire Christian view was too serious for him, especially at an
early age; but then, in another sense, this was itself something of a help to
him, this more profound notion of what sin is.

Sin is: before God in despair not to will to be oneself, or before God in
despair to will to be oneself. But is this definition—even if in other respects
it is perhaps conceded that it has its merits (the most important among them
being that it is the only one supported by Scripture, for Scripture always

defines sin as disobedience)—is it not too spiritual? To this, the reply must
be, first and foremost: A definition of sin can never be too spiritual (unless
it becomes so spiritual that it abolishes sin), for sin is precisely a
qualification of spirit. And next: Why is it supposedly too spiritual?
Because it does not speak of murder, theft, fornication, and the like?! But
doesn’t it, in fact, speak of them? Are they not also forms of willful
opposition to God, disobedience that defies his commandments? But on the
other hand when, in speaking of sin, one speaks only of sins such as these,
it is so easily forgotten that things of this sort, which are matters of “to a
certain degree,” can be in order from the human point of view, and yet the
whole of life be sin, sin of the well-known sort: the glittering vices,° the
willful opposition which either spiritlessly or defiantly continues to be—or
wills to be—in ignorance with respect to the infinitely greater sense in
which a human self is obligated to be obedient to God with respect to its
most hidden wishes and thoughts, with respect to a readiness to grasp and a
willingness to follow even the least indication from God as to what his will
is for this self. The sins of the flesh are the willfulness of the lower self, but
how often is it not the case that a devil is driven out with the devil’s help,'
and the end is worse than the beginning." For indeed, this is precisely how
things go in the world: first, a person sins out of frailty and weakness, and #
[196]# then—yes, then he perhaps learns to take refuge in God and is
helped to the faith that saves from all sin—but that is not what we are
talking about here: then he despairs over his weakness and either becomes a
Pharisee, who in despair pushes things to a sort of legal righteousness, or in
despair casts himself once again into sin.

Therefore, the definition indeed covers every conceivable and every
actual form of sin; though of course it properly emphasizes the decisive
point, that sin is despair (for sin is not the unruliness of flesh and blood,’
but is the spirit’s consent to that unruliness) and is: before God. As a
definition it is algebraic; in this brief treatise it would be out of place—and
furthermore, it would be an effort that would necessarily fail—if I were to
begin to describe specific, individual sins. Here the most important thing is
simply that the definition be like a net that covers all forms. And this it
does, which can indeed be seen when one tests it by setting forth its
opposite, the definition of faith, by which I steer my course throughout this

work as from a fixed navigation point. Faith is: that the self, in being itself
and in willing to be itself, is grounded transparently in God.

But indeed, this fact has often been overlooked: that the opposite of sin
is by no means virtue. That is to some extent a pagan view, which is
satisfied with a merely human criterion that is in fact ignorant of what sin
is, that all sin is before God. No, the opposite of sin is faith, as is therefore
stated in Romans 14:23: “Whatsoever is not of faith is sin.” And this is one
of the definitions that is decisive for the whole of Christianity: that the
opposite of sin is not virtue, but faith.

THE OPPOSITION: SIN—FAITH IS the Christian one which refashions all ethical
concepts in a Christian manner, providing them with an additional
extension of their range. This opposition rests upon the decisive Christian
stipulation “before God,” a determinant which in turn includes the decisive
Christian criterion: the absurd, the paradox, the possibility of offense. And
it is of utmost importance that this be pointed out #[197]# in every
definition of Christianity, because offense is Christianity’s weapon against
all speculation.® Where, then, is the possibility of offense in this? It is that a
human being should have this reality: the reality of existing, as an
individual human being, directly before God, and therefore, in turn, what
follows from this: that a human being’s sin should be of concern to God.
This matter of the individual human being—before God—is something that
speculation will never get into its head; it merely universalizes individual
human beings imaginatively into the race. This was also precisely why a
faithless Christianity invented the notion that sin is sin, and that it makes no
difference whether or not it is before God. In other words, they wanted to
get rid of the qualification “before God,” and to this end they came up with
a higher wisdom which, curiously enough, was neither more nor less than
what higher wisdom indeed is most often: the paganism of antiquity.

Now, it is often said that people are offended by Christianity because it
is so gloomy and dark, offended by it because it is so strict, etc.; what
would be most correct, however, would be to explain, for once, that the real
reasons a person is offended by Christianity are that it is too lofty, that its
goal is not a human being’s goal, that it wants to make the human being into
something so extraordinary that he cannot get it into his head. A quite
simple psychological exposition of what offense is will explain this and will
in addition show how infinitely foolishly people have behaved in defending
Christianity by having removed the offense; how people have, stupidly or
impudently, ignored Christ’s own instruction which frequently, and with
great concern, warns against the offense'—that is, he himself points out that
the possibility of offense is there and must be there, for if it did not have to
be there, if it were not an eternal, essential component of Christianity, then
it is of course human nonsense for Christ to be concerned and to warn
against it instead of removing it.

If I were to imagine a poor day laborer and the mightiest emperor who
ever lived, and that this mightiest of emperors suddenly had the idea of
sending for the day laborer, who indeed had never dreamt, “and neither had
it entered into his heart”! that the emperor knew he existed, and who would
indeed count himself indescribably fortunate merely to be permitted one
day to see the emperor, in which case it would be something he would relate
to his children and grandchildren as the most important event in his life—if
the emperor were to send for him and inform him that he wanted to have
him #[198]# as his son-in-law—what then? Then, in human fashion, the day
laborer would become somewhat—or very much—awkward, embarrassed,
and self-conscious; in human fashion, it would (and this is what is human
about it) seem to him something extremely odd, mad, something he would
least of all dare tell anyone else, for in his heart of hearts, he himself was
already close to settling upon an explanation that might soon be eagerly
taken up by his next-door neighbors and the people across the way: that the
emperor wanted to make a fool of him, so that the day laborer would
become an object of ridicule for the entire town—he would be depicted in
the newspaper, the tale of his engagement to the emperor’s daughter would
be sold by the market women who hawk popular verses. But this matter of
his becoming the emperor’s son-in-law: would that it might become public

knowledge as soon as possible, so that the day laborer could ascertain
through his senses whether the emperor is serious about it or merely wants
to get the better of the poor fellow, make him unhappy for the rest of his
life, and help him end up in the madhouse, for here there is a quid nimis!
that can so infinitely easily turn into its opposite. A little indication of favor,
that is something the day laborer could get into his head; it would be
understood in the market town,” by the esteemed, cultivated public, by all
the market women who hawk popular verses, in short, by the five times one
hundred thousand people who lived in that market town which, true enough,
with respect to the size of its population, was indeed even a very large city
—though, on the other hand, with respect to having an understanding of and
a sense for what is extraordinary, was a very small market town—but this,
this matter of becoming a son-in-law, yes, this was much too much. And
now, suppose that the matter under consideration was not an external
reality, but an internal one, so that facticity could not help the day laborer
gain certainty, but faith itself was the only facticity, and thus everything was
left to faith, left to whether he had sufficient humble courage to dare believe
it (for brazen courage does not help one have faith): How many day
laborers would there be, indeed, who had this courage? But the one who
lacked this courage, he would be offended; to him, the extraordinary would
sound almost like a mockery of him. Then, perhaps he would confess,
honestly and straightforwardly: “This sort of thing is too lofty for me. I
cannot get it into my head. To tell the truth, it seems like foolishness to
me.”

And now Christianity! Christianity teaches that this individual human
being, and thus every individual human being, whether he happens to be a
man, woman, serving maid, cabinet minister, merchant, barber, university
student, etc., this individual human being exists before God—this individual
human being, #[199]# who would perhaps be proud of having once in his
life spoken with the king, this human being who does not in the least
imagine that he is on confidential terms with this person or that person, this
human being exists before God, can speak with God at any moment he
wishes, certain of being heard by him—in short, this human being is offered
the opportunity to live on the most intimate terms with God! Moreover, for
this person—indeed, for this person’s sake—God comes to the world, lets

himself be born, suffer, and die; and this suffering God practically begs and
pleads that this person at least accept the help that is offered him! Truly, if
there is anything over which to lose one’s reason, it is indeed surely this!
Every person who does not have the humble courage to dare to believe this
—he is offended. But why is he offended? Because it is too lofty for him,
because he cannot get it into his head, because, when faced with it, he
cannot summon up his frank and cheerful manner, and therefore he must get
rid of it, have it annihilated, made into madness and nonsense, for it seems
as if it will suffocate him.

For what is offense? Offense is unhappy admiration. It is thus related to
envy, but it is an envy that turns against oneself—in an even stricter sense,
it is worst against oneself. The selfishly narrow heart of the natural human
being cannot permit itself to accept the extraordinary that God has intended
for him; so he is offended.

Now, the degree of offense depends on the passion of a person’s
admiration. More prosaic people, who are lacking in imagination and
passion, and who therefore are not really fitted for admiring—they, too, are
certainly offended, but they limit themselves to saying: “I cannot really get
that sort of thing into my head, I will let it be.” These are the skeptics. But
the more passion and imagination a person has—the closer he is in a certain
sense (namely, in possibility) to being able to have faith, n.b. by humbling
himself under the extraordinary in adoration—the more passionate the
offense, which, finally, cannot be satisfied with anything short of getting
this exterminated, annihilated, trodden into the dust.

If you want to gain an understanding of offense, then study human envy,
a course of study I offer as an outside field for my examinations, and I
daresay that I have studied it thoroughly. Envy is concealed admiration. An
admirer who feels that he cannot become happy in devoting himself to the
object of his admiration chooses to become envious of what he admires.
Then he speaks a different language; in this language he now says that what
he really admires is nothing, something stupid and insipid and odd and
overwrought. #[200]# Admiration is happy self-surrender, envy is unhappy
self-assertion.

Thus, too, with offense, for what is “admiration/envy” in the relation
between one human being and another is “adoration/offense” in the relation

between God and a human being. The summa summarum' of all human
wisdom is this “golden”!°—or perhaps more correctly, this gold-plated—ne
quid nimis:" too little or too much ruins everything. This is received and
passed on as wisdom from one person to another, it is honored with
admiration, its exchange rate never fluctuates, its value is guaranteed by the
entire human race. Then if, once in a while, there is a genius who goes a bit
over the line: he is declared mad—by those who are shrewd and clever. But
Christianity takes an enormous giant step beyond this ne quid nimis, into
the absurd; that is the beginning point for Christianity—and offense.

It can now be seen how extraordinarily (so that at least something that is
extraordinary might remain) stupid it is to defend Christianity, what little
knowledge of human beings it reveals, how doing so—even if
unconsciously—colludes with offense by making Christianity into a sort of
miserable thing which am Ende" needs to be rescued with a defense. It is
therefore quite certain and true that the person who first came up with the
idea of defending Christianity in Christendom is de facto a Judas no. 2; he,
too, betrays with a kiss, except that his treason is that of stupidity. To
defend something is always to disparage it. Suppose someone had a
warehouse full of gold, and suppose he was willing to give away every
ducat of it to the poor—but suppose, too, that he was foolish enough to
launch this charitable undertaking of his with a defense in which he set
forth three reasons justifying his action: it would not take much for people
almost to begin to have doubts about whether what he was doing was an act
of charity. But now take Christianity! Indeed, the person who defends it has
never believed in it. If he believes, then the enthusiasm of faith is—not a
defense, no, it is attack and victory; a believer is a victor.

This is how it is with Christianity and offense. Its possibility, then, is
truly present in the Christian definition of sin. It is this: before God. A
pagan, the natural human being, is quite willing to concede that sin exists,
but this “before God,” which, after all, is what really makes sin into sin—
this is too much for him. To him, it is (even if in a manner other than that
shown here) making much too much of what it #[201]# is to be a human
being; if it were a bit less, he could go along with it—“but too much is too
much.”

Chapter Two

THE SOCRATIC DEFINITION OF SIN

Sin is ignorance. This, as is well-known, is the Socratic definition! which

like everything Socratic is always an authority worthy of attention.
However, in connection with this Socratic point, as with so many of
Socrates’ other views, people have learned to feel a need to go further.”
How many countless numbers of people have not felt an urge to go further
than this Socratic ignorance*>—presumably because they felt that it was
impossible for them to remain standing at that point; for how many are
there, really, in any generation who were able to endure expressing
ignorance existentially about everything, even for just one month?

Therefore I certainly will not dismiss the Socratic definition on the
grounds that one cannot remain standing at that point; rather, precisely
because the Socratic definition is so genuinely Greek, with the Christian
position in mente,! I will make use of the Socratic definition in order to
place Christianity in sharp focus, so that here, as always, every other
definition that is not strictly Christian in the strictest sense—that is, every
halfway definition—will exhibit its emptiness.

Now, what is suspect about the Socratic definition is that it allows a
more detailed understanding of this ignorance, of its origin, etc., to remain
unclear. This means that even if sin is ignorance (or what Christianity might

prefer to call stupidity), which in one sense simply cannot be denied: Is this
an original ignorance—that is, is this condition that of a person who has not
known and who has not been able to know anything about the truth—or is it
a produced, a later ignorance? If it is the latter, then sin must of course be
rooted in something other than ignorance, it must be rooted in the act by
which a person has obscured his knowledge. But even if this is assumed, the
obstinate and tenacious irregularity recurs, for the question remains as to
whether, at the moment he began to obscure his knowledge, the person was
clearly #[202]# conscious of doing so. If he was not clearly conscious of it,
then his knowledge had of course already been obscured before he began
doing it, and the question merely arises once again. On the other hand, if it
is assumed that, at the point when he began to obscure his knowledge, he
was Clearly aware of what he was doing, then sin (even if it is ignorance,
insofar as that is the result) is of course rooted not in knowledge, but in the
will, and the question that necessarily arises concerns the relation of
knowledge and will to one another. With all such considerations (and here
one could continue questioning for many days) the Socratic definition does
not really concern itself. Socrates was certainly an ethicist (something with
which antiquity of course unconditionally credits him: the inventor of
ethics),* the first one, as he is and remains the first of his kind, but he
begins with ignorance. Intellectually, he tends toward ignorance, toward
knowing nothing. Ethically, what he understands by ignorance is something
completely different, and thus he begins with that. But on the other hand,
Socrates, quite naturally, is not an essentially religious ethicist, even less is
he what a Christian ethicist would be: a dogmatist. Therefore, he never
really enters at all into the investigation with which Christianity begins,
does not enter into the prius! in which sin presupposes itself and which
Christianity explains with the dogma of inherited sin,” though in the present
investigation we come only to the boundary of that dogma.

Socrates therefore does not really come to the category of sin, which is
certainly problematic in connection with a definition of sin. How can this
be? If, in fact, sin is ignorance, then of course sin does not really exist, for
of course sin is precisely consciousness. If sin is ignorance of what is right,
so that one therefore does what is wrong, then sin does not exist. If this is
what sin is, then we assume what Socrates also assumed: that it cannot

happen that someone, knowing what is right, does what is wrong, or,
knowing that something is wrong, does what is wrong.° Thus, if the
Socratic definition is correct, then sin simply does not exist. But look, from
a Christian standpoint this is entirely in order, and in a deeper sense entirely
correct; in the interest of Christianity it is quod erat demonstrandum."
The concept by which Christianity most decisively and qualitatively
differentiates itself from paganism is, precisely: sin, the doctrine of sin, and
therefore Christianity supposes—again, entirely consistently—that neither
paganism nor the natural man knows what sin is; indeed, it assumes that
there must be a revelation from God in order to make manifest what sin is.
Indeed, it is not #[203]# the case, as superficial observation assumes, that
the doctrine of the atonement is what constitutes the qualitative difference
between paganism and Christianity. No, the beginning must be made far
deeper, with sin, with the doctrine of sin, as Christianity indeed does. What
a damaging objection against Christianity it would have to be, then, if
paganism had a definition of sin that Christianity was compelled to
acknowledge as correct.

What sort of a category is it, then, that Socrates lacks in defining sin? It
is the will, defiance. Greek intellectuality was too happy, too naive, too
aesthetic, too ironic, too witty—too sinful to get it into its head that a
person could knowingly refrain from doing the good, or knowingly, with
knowledge of the right thing to do, do wrong. The Greek mentality
proposes an intellectual categorical imperative.’

By no means ought the truth of this be overlooked, and it is surely
necessary to emphasize this in times such as these, which have run wild in
so terribly empty and self-important and fruitless knowledge, so it is
certainly the case that now—entirely as in Socrates’ time, only more so—it
is necessary that people be starved out a bit in Socratic fashion.® It is
enough to make one laugh and weep, all these assurances of having
understood and grasped what is highest, plus the virtuosity with which
many people know how to present it in abstracto,' in a certain sense
entirely correctly—it is enough to make one laugh and weep when one then
sees that all this knowledge and understanding exercises no power
whatsoever over peoples’ lives, that they do not in the least way express
what they have understood, but rather the opposite. At the sight of this

inconsistency, which is as pathetic as it is ludicrous, one involuntarily cries
out: “But how in all the world is it in fact possible that they could have
understood it? And is it indeed true that they have?” To this, the old ironist
and ethicist? replies: “O, dear friend, don’t ever believe it; they have not
understood it, for if they had truly understood it, their lives would also have
expressed it, they would have done what they had understood.”

To understand and to understand—are they two different things, then?
Exactly so; and the person who has understood this—though, to be sure, not
in the sense of the first sort of understanding—is eo ipso! initiated into all
the secrets of irony. It is really this contradiction with which irony is
occupied. To regard as comic the fact that a person is actually ignorant of
something is a very low form of the comic and is beneath irony’s dignity.
There is really nothing profoundly comic in the fact that there have lived
people who believed that the earth stood #[204]# still—when they didn’t
know any better. Presumably something similar will in turn happen to our
age in comparison with an age that possesses greater knowledge of the
physical universe. The contradiction is between two different eras; there is
no deeper point at which they coincide—a contradiction of this sort is not
essential and thus neither is it essentially comical. No, but when a person
stands there and says the right thing (and thus has understood it), and then,
when he is to act, does the wrong thing (and thus shows that he has not
understood it): yes, that is infinitely comical. It is infinitely comical that a
person who is moved to the point of tears, so that not only sweat, but tears,
too, are pouring from him: that he can sit and read or listen to an account of
self-denial, of the nobility of sacrificing one’s life for the truth—and then,
at the next instant, ein, zwei, drei, vupti,!! almost with tears still in his eyes,
he is fully engaged, in the sweat of his brow" and to the best of his abilities,
with helping untruth to triumph. It is infinitely comical that an orator,
whose voice and expression radiate truth, who is profoundly moved and
profoundly moving, who presents the truth in heartrending fashion, who
confronts every evil, the forces of hell, face to face, with a certainty in his
stance, a courageous gaze, and an admirable propriety in his step—it is
infinitely comical that, at almost that very instant, almost while still wearing
his Adrienne,!° he is capable, in cowardly and fearful fashion, of running
away from the least little difficulty. It is infinitely comical that someone can

understand the entire truth about how wretched and petty the world is, etc.
—that he can understand this and then cannot recognize what he has
understood; for at almost that very instant he himself goes off and
participates in the same pettiness and wretchedness, is honored by it and
accepts the honor: that is, acknowledges it. Ah, when one sees a person!
who gives assurances that he has understood completely how Christ went
about in the form of a lowly servant,’ poor, despised, mocked, as Scripture
says: spat upon!'—when I then see that same person so painstakingly seek
out the place where, in the worldly sense, it is pleasant to be,’ and ensconce
himself there in complete security, when I see him so anxiously—as if it
were a matter of life and death—avoiding every unfavorable gust of wind
from the right or left, so happy, so extremely happy, so madly happy—
indeed, to top it off, so madly happy that, profoundly moved, he even
thanks God for it, for being unreservedly honored and esteemed by
everyone, everyone: then I have often said to myself and by myself:
“Socrates, Socrates, Socrates, how could it be possible that this person has
understood what he says he has understood?” This is what I have said—
indeed, I have, furthermore, wished that #[205]# Socrates was right. For it
seems to me, after all, as if Christianity were too rigorous, nor can I make
turning such a man into a hypocrite comport with my own experience. No,
Socrates, you I can understand; you make him into a joker, a sort of merry
fellow; you make him a prey of ridicule; you have no objection to my
preparing him and serving him up comically—indeed, you applaud—
provided I do it well.

Socrates, Socrates, Socrates! Yes, one may surely say your name three
times; it wouldn’t be too much to say it ten times, if it would be of any help.
People think the world needs a republic,'* and people think it needs a new
social order and a new religion: but no one thinks that, after all, a Socrates
is surely what is needed by this world, confused as it is with knowledge.
But it goes without saying that if anyone thought of that—not to mention if
many thought of it—he would be less needed. What an error needs most is
always what it thinks of least—naturally, for otherwise it of course would
not be an error.

Therefore an ironical-ethical corrective such as this is something for
which there could be a great need in our age, is perhaps the only thing it

really needs—for it is obvious that this is what it least thinks about; instead
of going further than Socrates, it is very necessary that we simply return to
this Socratic saying: to understand and to understand are two different
things—not as a conclusion that ultimately helps people who are in the
most profound misery (for it simply abolishes the difference between to
understand and to understand), but as the ethical understanding of daily life.

The Socratic definition, then, helps itself in the following way. When
someone does not do what is right, then neither has he understood it; his
understanding is an illusion; the assurance he gives of having understood it
is a false indication of direction; his repeated assurances that he damn well
did understand it are a very, very long distance down the most circuitous
detour possible. But then the definition is indeed correct. If someone does
what is right, he certainly does not sin; and if he does not do what is right,
then neither has he understood it. Had he truly understood it, it would
quickly have moved him to do it, quickly have made him into an acoustic
figure!’ of what he understood: ergo sin is ignorance.

But where, then, is the defect lodged? It is lodged at a point of which
the Socratic position is itself aware and which it remedies, though only to a
certain degree: that it lacks a dialectical specification concerning the
transition from having understood something to doing it. In this transition is
the beginning of #[206]# Christianity. Then, in continuing along this path, it
is shown that sin lies in the will, arriving at the concept of defiance—and
then, in order to fasten the end of the thread properly, it adds the dogma of
inherited sin'*—for alas, the secret of the speculative way of
comprehending things consists precisely in sewing without making the end
fast and without knotting the thread, and therefore—amazingly—it can
continue sewing and sewing, i.e., pulling the thread through. Christianity,
on the other hand, makes the end fast with the assistance of the paradox.

In pure ideality, which does not concern itself with the actual individual
human being, transitions’? happen by necessity (in the System,!° of course,
everything takes place by necessity!”), or there is no difficulty whatsoever
connected with the transition from understanding to doing. This is the
Greek mentality (though not the Socratic, for Socrates is too much of an
ethicist for that). And the whole secret of modern philosophy’ is really the
same, for it is this cogito ergo sum,' to think is to be (the Christian

position, on the other hand, is this: “Let it be done for you according to your
faith,”!! or as you have faith, so you are; to have faith is to be). Thus we see
that modern philosophy is neither more nor less than paganism. And this is
not even the worst of it; kinship with Socrates is not the worst of things. But
what is utterly un-Socratic about modern philosophy is that it wants to
imagine, and wants us to imagine, that this is Christianity.

On the other hand, in the actual world, where it involves the individual
human being, there is this tiny little transition from having understood to
doing; it is not always cito citissime,'" it is not (if unable to speak
philosophically, I must speak German) geschwind wie der Wind.'” On the
contrary, here begins a very long-winded story.

There is no standing still in the life of the spirit (neither is there any
state of affairs'"—everything is actuality); if a person, at the very same
second that he knows the right thing, does not do it—well, then, first of all,
his knowledge comes off the boil. And next comes the question of what the
will thinks of this knowledge. The will is dialectical and, in turn, has
beneath itself the entire lower nature of a person. Now, if it does not like
this knowledge, it does not indeed follow that the will goes and does the
opposite of what was understood by this knowledge—such powerful
contrasts are probably rather infrequent. But then the will lets some time
pass; there is an interim called: “Well, we’ll wait until tomorrow and see.”
During all this, knowing becomes more and more obscured, and the lower
nature is #[207]# increasingly victorious; for the good, alas, must be done
immediately, immediately, as soon as it is known (and that is why in pure
ideality the transition from thinking to being goes so easily, because there
everything is immediate), but the strength of the lower nature consists in
dragging things out. Little by little, the will becomes less opposed to what is
happening—it almost looks the other way. And then, when knowing has
been sufficiently obscured—then knowing and the will are better able to
understand one another; finally, they agree completely, for then knowing
has gone over to the will’s side and acknowledges that what it wants is
entirely right. And perhaps this is how a great many people live: little by
little, they work at obscuring their ethical and ethical-religious knowledge,
which together would lead them toward decisions and consequences with
which their lower nature is not enamored; on the other hand, they expand

their aesthetic and metaphysical knowledge, which, from the ethical point
of view, is a diversion.

Still, with all this we have not yet come further than to the Socratic
position, for, Socrates would say, if this happens, it simply shows that a
person of this sort has not in fact understood what is right. This means that
the Greek mentality lacks the courage to say that a person knowingly does
what is wrong—that, knowing what is right, a person does what is wrong:
so it helps itself by saying, “If someone does what is wrong, it is because he
has not understood what is right.”

It is quite true that no human being can come any further than this: on
his own, and of himself, no human being can say what sin is, precisely
because he is in sin. All his talk about sin is fundamentally an extenuation
of sin, an excuse, a sinful toning-down. For this reason, Christianity indeed
begins in another way: a revelation from God is necessary in order to
enlighten a person as to what sin is: that sin does not in fact come from a
person’s not having understood what is right, but from his unwillingness to
understand it and his unwillingness to do what is right.

Even with respect to the distinction between being unable to understand
and being unwilling to understand, Socrates does not really provide any
enlightenment, whereas he is the grand master of all ironists when it comes
to making use of the distinction between understanding and
understanding.”° Socrates explains that when a person does not do what is
right, he has not understood it, either;! but Christianity goes a bit further
back and says that this is because he is unwilling to understand it, and this,
in turn, is because he is unwilling to do what is right. Next, it teaches that a
person does what is wrong (actual defiance) despite the fact that he
understands what is right, or that he refrains from doing what is right
despite the fact that he understands it;! #[208]# in short, the Christian
doctrine of sin is a direct insult to the human being—accusation upon
accusation, it is the charge that the Deity, as prosecutor, permits itself to
lodge against the human being.

But can any human being comprehend this Christianity? Absolutely not
—of course, this, too, is indeed Christianity, thus it is fraught with offense.
It must be believed. “Comprehending” is the extent of a human being’s
relation to human matters, but “believing” is the human being’s relation to

the Deity. How, then, does Christianity explain this incomprehensibility?
Entirely consistently, in a manner that is equally incomprehensible—by
enlisting the aid of revelation.

Thus, Christianity understands that sin lies in the will, not in knowing;
and this corruption of the will affects the individual’s consciousness. This is
entirely consistent, for otherwise the question of how sin began would have
to arise for each individual.

Here, once again, we have the mark of offense. The possibility of
offense is inherent in the fact that a revelation from God is required in order
to enlighten the human being about what sin is and how deeply it is
lodged.** The natural human being, the pagan, thinks as follows: “All right,
I admit that I haven’t understood everything in heaven and on earth. If a
revelation is needed, then let it enlighten us about heavenly things. But that
a revelation is needed in order to inform us about what sin is—that is the
most unreasonable of all things. I don’t pretend to be a perfect person, far
from it, but this is something I know, and I am willing to concede how far I
am from being perfect: so am I, then, not supposed to know what sin is?”
But Christianity replies, “No, what you know least of all is how far you are
from being perfect and what sin is.” — Look, in this sense, from a Christian
point of view, it is in fact true that sin is ignorance: it is ignorance of what
sin is.

The definition of sin given in the previous chapter therefore still needs
to be completed as follows: Sin is, after having been enlightened by a
revelation from God concerning what sin is, before God in despair not to
will to be oneself, or in despair to will to be oneself. #[209]#

Chapter Three

THAT SIN IS NOT A NEGATION, BUT
A POSITION!

That this is the case is something that has been steadfastly defended by

orthodox dogmatics and by orthodoxy in general, which have rejected as
pantheistic every definition of sin that made it into something merely
negative, into weakness, sensuality, finitude, ignorance, and the like.
Orthodoxy has very correctly recognized that this is where the battle is to be
joined, or, to recall our earlier remarks, that this is where the end of the
thread has to be made fast, this is where to take a stand. Orthodoxy has
correctly recognized that if sin is defined negatively, the whole of
Christianity wavers. Orthodoxy therefore insists that there must be a
revelation from God in order to teach the fallen human being what sin is, a
communication which, quite consistently, must be believed, for it is a
dogma. And it is self-evident that the paradox, the faith, the dogma—these
three resolutions—form an alliance and a harmony that is the most certain
support and bulwark against all pagan wisdom.

Thus with orthodoxy. Owing to a peculiar misunderstanding, a so-called
speculative dogmatics* has indeed involved itself with philosophy in
suspect fashion, thinking itself capable of comprehending that this

specification of sin is a position. But if that is true, then sin is a negation.
The secret of all comprehending is that comprehending is itself higher than
every position that it posits. The concept posits a position, but the fact that
it is comprehended means precisely that it is negated. Although speculative
dogmatics is itself to some extent aware of this, it knows of no other
remedy than (and this is indeed quite unbecoming for a philosophical
science) to put into the field a detachment of assurances at the point where
the movement is being made. They utter assurances with ever greater
solemnity, swearing oath upon oath and uttering imprecations to the effect
that sin is a position, that to say that sin is merely a negation is pantheism
and rationalism and God knows what else, but altogether something that
speculative dogmatics renounces and abominates—and then they proceed to
comprehend the fact that sin is a position. That is, that it is only a position
to a certain degree, no more than that a person can in fact comprehend it. #
[210]#

And this same duplicity on the part of speculation emerges at a different
point, though touching upon the same issue. The category of sin, or the
manner in which sin is defined, is of decisive importance for the category of
repentance. Seeing as this business of the negation of the negation is indeed
so speculative, then, inevitably, repentance must be the negation of the
negation’-—and thus sin of course becomes the negation. — Furthermore, it
surely would be desirable if, for once, a sober-minded thinker would make
clear the extent to which this purely logical matter—which calls to mind
both logic’s original relation to grammar (two negatives constitute an
affirmative) and mathematics—the extent to which this logical matter has
validity in the world of actuality, in the world of qualities; whether the
dialectic of qualities is not something altogether different; whether “the
transition”* does not play another role here. Sub specie eeterni, aeterno
modo,' etc., there are no distances between one thing and another;
therefore, everything is, and there is no transition whatsoever. In this
abstract medium, to posit is eo ipso the same as to annul. But to regard the
actual world in the same manner is indeed something close to madness. One
can also say, entirely in abstracto, that the Perfectum" follows the
Imperfectum." But if a man were to conclude from this that in the actual
world it followed of itself and followed immediately that a work he had not

completed (imperfectum) became complete, he would indeed be quite mad.
But this is also how it is with the so-called position of sin when the medium
in which it is posited? is pure thought: the medium is much too volatile for
the matter of its being a position to be taken seriously.

However, all this sort of thing is of no concern to me here. I simply
continue to stand firmly behind the Christian doctrine that sin is a position
—though not as something that can be comprehended, but as a paradox that
must be believed. In my view, this is the right way to approach this. If one
can simply show that all the attempts at comprehending it are self-
contradictory, the matter falls into its proper place, and it then becomes
clear that the question of whether or not one will believe must be left to
faith. — I can well comprehend (and this is by no means too divine to be
comprehended) that a person who absolutely must comprehend, and who
can approve only of what makes claims of comprehending, will find this
very paltry. But if the whole of Christianity depends upon this point—that it
shall be believed and not comprehended, that either it shall be believed or a
person shall be offended by it—is it then so meritorious to want to
comprehend? Is it meritorious, or is it not rather either shamelessness or
thoughtlessness to want to comprehend #[211]# that which does not want to
be comprehended? If a king gets the idea of wanting to be entirely
incognito, to be treated in every way as an ordinary man, then—if most
people think it is a greater distinction to pay royal homage to him—is that,
then, the right thing to do? Or is it not in fact an assertion of oneself and of
one’s ideas against the king’s will, of doing what one wants instead of
deferring? Or, if the king did not want to be treated like that, I wonder
whether it would please him if such a person became increasingly inventive
in paying him homage as a subject, that is, in demonstrating increasing
ingenuity in opposing the king’s will? — So let others admire and praise the
person who claims to be able to comprehend Christianity: I regard it as a
straightforward ethical task—which perhaps requires not a little self-denial
in such speculative times, when all “the others” are busy comprehending—
to confess, indeed, that one neither can nor is obliged to comprehend it.
This, however, is precisely what the times, what Christianity, needs: a little
Socratic ignorance® with respect to Christianity—but note well, a little
“Socratic” ignorance. Let us never forget (though how many are there,

indeed, who have ever really known it or thought it?)—let us never forget
that Socrates’ ignorance was a form of the fear and worship of God,’ that
his ignorance was a Greek version of the Jewish saying: “The fear of God is
the beginning of wisdom.”! Let us never forget that it was precisely out of
reverence for the Deity that he was ignorant, that to the extent a pagan was
capable of doing so, he stood guard as a judge, keeping watch on the
boundary between God—and human beings, keeping a close watch in order
that the deep gulf ' of qualitative difference remain fortified between them,
between God and human beings, so that God—and human beings might
not, as it were, philosophice, poetice," etc., merge into one. Look, this
was why Socrates was the one who knew nothing, this was why the Deity
acknowledged him as wisest.® — But Christianity teaches that everything
Christian exists only for faith; therefore it wants to be precisely a Socratic,
god-fearing ignorance that, by means of ignorance, protects faith against
speculation, keeping watch in order that the gulf of qualitative difference
between God—and human beings might remain fortified as it is in the
paradox and in faith, so that God and human beings do not, in a manner
even more frightful than ever happened in paganism, philosophice, poetice,
merge, as it were, into one—in the System.°

Here, the fact that sin is a position can be illuminated #[212]# only from
one side. In the preceding part, the account of despair showed a continuing
ascent. The expression of this ascent was both the intensification of the
consciousness of the self and the ascent from passive suffering to conscious
action. Both expressions in turn unite as an expression of the fact that
despair does not come from without, but from within. And to the same
degree it indeed also increasingly asserts itself positively, i.e., as a position.
But according to the definition of sin that has been set forth here, the self,
infinitely intensified by the conception of God, belongs to sin as, in turn,
does the greatest possible consciousness of sin as an act. — This is an
expression of the fact that sin is a position: the fact that it is before God is
precisely what is positive about it.

Moreover, in a completely different sense, the qualification of sin as a
position also has within itself the possibility of offense, the paradox. The
paradox indeed follows as a consequence of the doctrine of the atonement.
First, Christianity proceeds to establish sin so firmly as a position that the

human understanding can never comprehend it; and then, it is this same
Christian doctrine which, in turn, takes it upon itself to remove this position
in a manner that the human understanding can never comprehend it.
Speculation, which talks itself out of the paradoxes, trims things back a bit
at each of these two points, so things go a bit more easily: it makes sin out
to be not at all so positive—and despite this, it cannot really get it into its
head that the sin is to be entirely forgotten. But here once again,
Christianity, the original inventor of the paradoxes, is as paradoxical as
possible; it works counter to itself, as it were, when it establishes sin so
firmly as a position that it then seems completely impossible to get rid of it
again—and then it is precisely Christianity which, through the atonement,
wants to abolish sin so completely that it is as though it were drowned in
the sea.'

APPENDIX TO A
But then, does not sin in a certain sense become a great rarity? (the moral)

IN THE FIRST PART it was pointed out that the more intensive despair
becomes, the more rarely it occurs in the world. But here sin is of course
despair that has been qualitatively intensified once again, and mustn’t it
surely be #[213]# very rare? Strange difficulty! Christianity subjects
everything to sin;' we have striven to present the Christian view as
rigorously as possible—and now this curious result emerges, this curious
result that sin is indeed not found at all in paganism, but only in Judaism
and Christianity, and even there it is very rare.

But it is quite true that this is in fact how things are, though only in one
sense. To sin is: “after having been enlightened by a revelation from God
concerning what sin is, before God in despair not to will to be oneself, or in
despair to will to be oneself,” and it is indeed rarely the case that a person is
so developed, is so transparent to himself, that this can apply to him. But
what, then, follows from this? Indeed, this is certainly something worthy of
careful scrutiny, for here there is a peculiar dialectical turn. For of course it
does not follow, if a person is not in despair in the more intensive sense—
from this, it of course does not follow that he is not in despair. On the

contrary; it has been shown precisely that most people—by far, by far most
people—are in despair, but in despair to a lesser degree. Nor is it any way
meritorious to be in despair to a higher degree. Aesthetically, it is an
advantage, for aesthetics has an eye only for strength; but ethically, the
more intense form of despair is further from salvation than the lesser form.

And this is also how it is with sin. Most people’s lives, characterized
from a neutral-dialectical point of view, are so far from the good (faith) that
they are almost too spiritless to be called sin, indeed, almost too spiritless to
be called despair.

To be a sinner in the strictest sense is certainly very far from being
meritorious. But on the other hand, how in all the world can one find an
essential consciousness of sin (and note, this is something Christianity
indeed wants) in a life that is so sunken in triviality, in silly mimicking of
“the others,” that one almost cannot call it—that it is too spiritless to be
called—sin, and is worthy only, as Scripture says, “of being spit out.”!

But this is not the end of the matter, for then the dialectic of sin simply
takes hold in a different fashion. How, indeed, does it come about that a
person’s life becomes so spiritless that it is as if Christianity simply cannot
be brought to bear upon it, as when a jackscrew (and Christianity’s capacity
to lift upward is similar to that of a jackscrew) cannot be brought to bear #
[214]# because there is no solid ground, but only marsh and bog? Is it
something that happens to a person? No, it is the person’s own fault. No one
is born spiritless, and however many, in death, bring that with them as life’s
only outcome—it is not life’s fault.

But it must be said as unreservedly as possible that this so-called
Christendom—in which everyone by the millions is thus without further
ado Christian, so that there are just as many, exactly as many, Christians as
there are people—that this is not merely a poor edition of Christianity, full
of typographical errors that distort the meaning, and with thoughtless
omissions and additions, but that it is a misuse of it, that it has taken
Christianity in vain. In a little country, scarcely three poets are born in each
generation, but there are plenty of priests, many more than can find
positions. With poets, people speak of their having a call; in the view of
many people (and thus, of Christians), in order to become a priest it is
sufficient to have passed the examination. And yet a true priest is even more

rare than a true poet—and yet the word “call” was originally used in
connection with religious matters. But in Christendom people have still
preserved the idea that being a poet amounts to something, and that there is
something to the notion of its being a call. On the other hand, in the view of
many people (and thus, of Christians), being a priest is bereft of every
elevating notion, lacking the least bit of anything mysterious—in puris
naturalibus! a way of making a living. A “call” is an official appointment;
people speak of getting a call; but to have a call—yes, people speak of that
as well, of someone having a call in his gift.!°

Alas, and the fate of this word in Christendom is like an epigram on the
whole of Christianity. The problem is not that Christianity is not spoken of
(thus, neither is the problem that there are not enough priests), but that it is
spoken of in such a way that, in the final analysis, the mass of people don’t
attach any meaning to it (just as the mass of people think that being a priest
is absolutely no different from the utterly everyday pursuits of being a
merchant, an attorney, a bookbinder, a veterinarian, etc.), so that what is
loftiest and holiest makes no impression whatever, but is spoken and is
heard as something that nowadays, God knows why, has become customary
usage like so much else. What wonder, then, that—instead of finding their
own conduct indefensible—people find it necessary to defend Christianity.
— #[215]#

After all, a priest surely ought to be a believer. And a believer! A
believer, after all, is surely someone who is in love—indeed, the most in
love of all who are in love, after all, is only as an adolescent compared with
a believer. Now, think of a lover. Isn’t it true that day in and day out, as long
as the day lasts, and the night as well, he would be capable of speaking of
his beloved? But do you think it would occur to him, do you think it would
be possible for him, don’t you think that it would be an abomination to him
to advance three reasons in an attempt to prove that there was, after all,
something to this business of being in love—more or less as when the priest
proves, on the basis of three reasons, that it is beneficial to pray: then the
market price of prayer has sunk so low that three reasons are needed in
order to help it gain a bit of respect. Or as when the priest (and this is the
same sort of thing, merely more ludicrous) proves, on the basis of three
reasons, that prayer is a blessedness that surpasses all understanding.' Oh,

priceless anticlimax: that something that surpasses all understanding is
proven by three—reasons, which, if they are good for anything at all, surely
do not surpass all understanding, and, precisely to the contrary, must make
it clear to the understanding that this blessedness does not in any way
surpass all understanding, for “reasons” are of course situated well within
the range of the understanding. No, for that which surpasses all
understanding—and for the person who believes in it—three reasons mean
no more than three flasks or three stags!!! And now, further, do you think
that it would occur to a lover to plead in defense of his being in love, that is,
that he would concede that for him it was not something absolute,
categorically absolute, but that he thought of it in combination with the
objections that could be advanced against it, and that his defense emerged
from this; that is, do you think that he could or would concede that he was
not in love, that he would bear witness against himself to the effect that he
was not in love? And if someone were to suggest to the person in love that
he speak like this, don’t you think that he would regard him as mad? And if,
in addition to being in love, he was also a bit of an observer, don’t you think
that he would become suspicious that the person who suggested this to him
had never known what it is to be in love or that he wanted him to betray and
deny his love—by defending it? — Isn’t it, after all, obvious that it could
never occur to someone who is genuinely in love to want to prove it on the
basis of three reasons, or to defend it, for he is something that is more than
all reasons and more than every defense: he is in love. And the person who
does this—he #[216]# is not in love; he merely pretends that he is, and
unfortunately—or fortunately—he is so senseless that he merely bears
witness against himself, bears witness to the fact that he is not.

But this is precisely the way in which Christianity is spoken of—by
believing priests: either they “defend” Christianity, or they transpose it into
“reasons,” to the extent that they do not also dabble in “comprehending” it
speculatively. This is what is called preaching, and in Christendom it is
even regarded as something great that there is preaching of this sort and that
someone then listens to it. And it is precisely for this reason (this is the
proof of it) that Christendom is so far from being what it says it is, that the
lives of most people, understood in the Christian sense, are indeed too
spiritless even to be called sin in the strictly Christian sense.

THE CONTINUATION OF SIN

Eigen state of sin is a new Sin; or, as it might be more accurately

expressed and will be expressed in what follows, the state of being in sin is
the new sin, is the sin. This perhaps seems to the sinner to be an
exaggeration; at most, he acknowledges every actual new sin! as a new sin.
But eternity, which keeps his account, must enter the state of being in sin as
a new sin. It has only two categories, and “Whatsoever is not of faith is
sin.”! Every unrepented sin is a new sin, and every moment it is unrepented
it is anew sin. But how rare is the person who has continuity with respect to
his consciousness of himself! Most often, people are only conscious of
themselves momentarily, conscious in connection with major decisions, but
the everyday ones receive no attention; people are spirit of a sort once a
week for an hour’s time—clearly, this is indeed a rather bestial way of
being spirit. But eternity is the essential continuity, it demands this
continuity of a person, or that he be conscious of himself as spirit and have
faith. The sinner, on the other hand, is subjected to the power of sin in such
a way that he has no notion of its character as a totality, of the fact that he
has gone astray on the road to perdition.' He pays attention only to each
individual new sin with which, as it were, he increases his momentum on
the road to perdition, just as if he had not at the preceding moment been
proceeding down that road with all the momentum of the previous sins. So
natural has sin become to him, or has sin become his second nature, that he
finds the everyday routine quite in order, and he himself stops only for a
moment each time he gets, as it were, new impetus from a new sin. In his

perdition, he is #[218]# blind to the fact that, instead of having the essential
continuity of eternity by being before God in faith, his life has the
continuity of sin.

But, “the continuity of sin”: isn’t sin precisely non-continuity? Look,
here it is again, the argument that sin is merely a negation to which one can
never acquire legal title, just as one can never acquire legal title to stolen
goods—a negation, an impotent attempt to constitute itself, but which,
suffering all of the torments of impotence in its despairing defiance, is
nonetheless incapable of doing so. Yes, viewed speculatively, that is how it
is, but from a Christian point of view (this must be believed, for it is of
course a paradox that no human being can comprehend) sin is a position
which develops an increasingly positive? continuity out of itself.

And the law governing the growth of this continuity is also different
from what applies to the growth of a debt or to a negation. For a debt does
not grow because it has not been paid—it grows every time it is increased.
But sin grows at every instant one does not come out of it. So far is it from
being possible that the sinner is correct in regarding only each new sin as an
increase in sin, that, from a Christian point of view, the state of being in sin
is really greater sin, is the new sin. There is even a proverb that says it is
human to sin, but to remain in sin is devilish;* but from a Christian point of
view it is properly understood somewhat differently. Merely desultory
observation—which looks only at the new sin and springs over the
intervening space, the interval between the particular sins—is a manner of
observation as superficial as if one were to assume that a railroad train
moved only each time the locomotive puffed. No, that puffing, and the
forward motion that follows it, is not really what one should look at, but
rather at the steady momentum with which the locomotive moves and
which gives rise to that puffing. And that is how it is with sin. In the deepest
sense, the state of being in sin is the sin; the particular sins are not the
continuation of sin but are the expression of the continuation of sin; with
each particular new sin the momentum of sin merely becomes more
noticeable to the senses.

The state of being in sin is a worse sin than the particular sins; it is the
sin. And, thus understood, the state of being in sin is the continuation of sin,
is new sin. Generally speaking, people understand it differently, they think

that it is as if the one sin gives birth to new sin. But it has a much deeper
origin, namely that the state of being in sin is new #[219]# sin. There is
psychological mastery in the lines Shakespeare assigns to Macbeth (act 3,
scene 2): “Stindentsprofsne Werke erlangen nur durch Siinde Kraft und
Starke. ”! That is, sin has an inner consistency, and in this consistency of
evil within itself, it also has a certain power. But if one looks only at the
particular sins, one never comes to see things in this way.

Most people surely live with altogether too little awareness of
themselves to have a notion of what consistency is; that is, they do not exist
qua spirit. Their lives consist either of a certain childlike, lovable naiveté, or
of nonsense, or of some sort of taking action and some sort of something
happening, of this and that; now they do something good, and then in turn
do something wrong, and then they begin all over again; now they are in
despair for an afternoon, perhaps for three weeks, but then they are fine and
jolly fellows again, and then are again in despair for a day’s time. They play
along in life, so to speak, but they never experience focusing completely on
one thing, they never arrive at the notion of being infinitely consistent with
oneself. That is why they are concemed only with the particular, with
particular good deeds, particular sins.

Every existence that is within the category of spirit, even if it is only on
a person’s own responsibility and at his own risk, has an essential
consistency within itself and a consistency in something higher, at least in
an idea. But such a person is in turn infinitely fearful of any inconsistency
because he has an infinite notion of what the consequence could be, that he
could be torn out of the totality in which he has his life. The least
inconsistency is an enormous loss, for he of course loses consistency; at that
very moment, the magic could perhaps be undone; the mysterious power
that bound all the forces in harmony could be exhausted; the spring could
lose its tension; the whole of it could perhaps become chaos in which the
forces contend with one another, causing the self to suffer, but in which
there is no agreement, none at all, with oneself, no momentum, no impetus.
The enormous machinery which in its consistency had been so tractable in
its iron strength, so flexible in all its power, is in disarray, and the more
excellent, the more splendid the machinery, the worse is the confusion. —
The believer, who as such reposes in and has his life in the consistency of

the good, has an infinite fear of even the least sin, for he has infinitely much
to lose. Immediate people, childish or childlike people, have nothing total to
lose, they continually lose and win only in the particular instance or in
connection with the particular thing. #[220]#

But with respect to the consistency of sin, as with the person of faith, so
also with his counterpart, the demonic person. As the drunkard continually
maintains his intoxication, day after day, out of fear of the stagnation, the
dullness, that would set in and the possible consequences if he were to
become entirely sober for one day, so, too, with the demonic person.
Indeed, as when a good person, if someone were to approach him, trying to
tempt him, exhibiting sin in one or another seductive form, would plead
with that person, “Do not tempt me”—so are there surely examples of
exactly the same thing with a demonic person. Confronted by someone who
is stronger in the good than he is, who wants to present him with the good
in all its blessed loftiness, the demonic person can plead, he can plead with
tears in his eyes, that the other not speak to him, that he not—as he puts it—
weaken him. Precisely because the demonic person is consistent in himself
and is in the consistency of evil—for precisely this reason he also has a
totality to lose. One single instant outside his consistency, one single dietary
indiscretion, a single glance to the side, seeing and understanding the whole
or even a part of it in a different manner for one instant—and he will
perhaps never be himself again, he says. This means that he has abandoned
the good in despair, it cannot indeed help him anyway, but it could certainly
disturb him, make it impossible for him ever again to attain the full
momentum of consistency, weaken him. Only in the continuation of sin is
he himself, only in that does he live, does he have the impression of
himself. But what does this mean? It means that the state of being in sin is
that which—deep down, where he has sunk—holds him together,
strengthening him, in ungodly fashion, with its consistency; it is not the
particular new sin which (indeed, how frightfully mad!) helps him—rather,
the particular new sin is merely the expression for the state of being in sin,
which properly is sin.

Thus, by “the continuation of sin,” with which we will now concern
ourselves, is meant not so much the particular new sins as the state of being
in sin, which in turn becomes sin’s intensification within itself, a remaining
in the state of sin with a consciousness of being in that state; thus here, as

everywhere, the law of motion for this intensification is that it is inward, in
more and more intensive consciousness.

The sin of despairing over one’s sin

SIN IS DESPAIR; THE intensification is the new sin of despairing over one’s
sin. It is also easily seen that this is indeed a matter of intensification—it is
not a new sin, as when someone who once stole one hundred rixdollars
steals one thousand rixdollars the next time. No, here there is no talk of
particular sins: the state of being in sin is the sin, and this is intensified in a
new consciousness.

To despair over one’s sin is the expression of the fact that the sin has
become or wants to be consistent with itself. It does not want to have
anything to do with the good, does not want to be so weak as to listen to
other talk once in a while. No, it wants to listen only to itself, wants to have
to do only with itself, shut itself up within itself; indeed, it wants to enclose
itself within yet another enclosure and, in despair over sin, secure itself
against every assault or pursuit by the good. It is itself conscious of having
destroyed the bridge behind it and in that way having made itself
inaccessible to the good, just as the good is inaccessible to it—thus, even if
in a weak moment it might itself will the good, that would nonetheless be
impossible. Sin is itself separation from the good, but despair over sin is a
second separation. This of course compels the sin to summon up its most
extreme demonic powers, endowing it with the ungodly toughness or
obduracy that compels it consistently to regard everything that goes by the
name of repentance, and everything that goes by the name of grace, not
only as empty and meaningless, but as its enemy, as that from which, above
all, one must protect oneself, exactly as the good protects itself from
temptation. Understood thus, the line by Mephistopheles (in Faust)*
correctly states the fact that nothing is more miserable than a devil who is in

despair, for by “despair” it is meant that a person is so weak as to be willing
to listen to something about repentance and grace. In describing the
intensification of the relation between sin and despair over sin, one could
say that the former is the break with the good, and the latter is the break
with repentance.

Despair over sin is an attempt to survive by sinking even deeper; as
when a person who ascends in a balloon rises higher by casting off weights,
the person in despair sinks by casting off, with ever-increasing
determination, everything that is good (for the weight of the good is what
lifts a person up). He is sinking: he himself believes that he is actually
ascending—he does, after all, become #[222]# lighter. Sin itself is the
struggle of despair, but then, when the powers have been exhausted, there
must be a new intensification, a new demonic self-enclosure in oneself: this
is despair over sin. This is an advance, an increase in the demonic—of
course, a deeper immersion in sin. It is an attempt to impart firmness and
importance to sin as a power, inasmuch as it is now eternally decided that a
person will hear nothing about repentance and nothing of grace.
Nonetheless, despair over sin is in fact conscious precisely of its own
emptiness, of the fact that it has nothing to live on, not even its notion of its
own self. Macbeth’s lines (act 2, scene 2) are psychologically masterly:
“von jetzt [after he has murdered the king—and now is despairing over his
sin] giebt es nichts Ernstes mehr im Leben; Alles ist Tand, gestorben Ruhm
und Gnade.” ' What is masterly is the double stroke in the final words
(Ruhm und Gnade “*Y ). Through sin, that is, through despair over sin, he
has lost every relation to grace—and, in addition, to himself. His selfish self
culminates in ambition. Now he has indeed become king, and yet when he
despairs over his sin and of the reality of repentance, of grace, he has also
lost himself—he cannot keep on, not even for himself, and he is equally far
from being able to enjoy himself in his ambition as he is from being able to
take hold of grace.

In life (that is, to the extent that despair over sin occurs in life—but in any
case, something occurs that people refer to as such), this despair over sin is

most often misunderstood, presumably because in the world people usually
encounter only frivolity, thoughtlessness, and sheer twaddle, and therefore
really become quite solemn and respectfully remove their hats at every
expression of anything deeper. Either out of a muddled unclarity concerning
itself and its significance, or with a dash of hypocrisy, or with assistance
from the cunning and sophistry that accompany all despair, despair over sin
is not disinclined to take on an appearance of being something good. Thus it
is supposed to be an expression of someone with a deeper nature, who is
therefore so profoundly affected by his sin. I will give an example: If there
were someone who had abandoned himself to one or another sin, but then
had for a long time #[223]# successfully resisted the temptation—if he has
a relapse and succumbs to the temptation once again, the dejection that
ensues is by no means always sorrow over sin. It can be something very
different; for that matter, it could also be indignation directed at
Governance, as if it were Governance that had let him succumb to the
temptation, as if it ought not to have been so severe with him, inasmuch as
he had in fact successfully resisted the temptation for such a long time. But
in any case, it is absolutely womanish simply to accept this sorrow at face
value, to fail to pay even the least attention to the duplicity that is present in
all passionateness, which is in turn something ominous that can result in the
passionate person subsequently realizing—sometimes almost to the point of
madness—that he has said the opposite of what he intended to say. Such a
person gives assurances—perhaps expressing himself more and more
emphatically—of how he is tortured and tormented by this relapse, how it
brings him to despair: “I will never forgive myself for it,” he says. And all
this is supposed to be the expression of how much good dwells within him,
of the depth of his character. This is deliberately misleading. In presenting
this, I have taken pains to let the catchphrase “I will never forgive myself
for it” slip in, an expression that is usually heard in just such circumstances.
And on the basis of precisely this expression one can immediately orient
oneself dialectically. He will never forgive himself for it—but now, if God
were to forgive him for it, he could at least have the decency to forgive
himself. No, his despair over sin—and most of all, of course, the more his
despair rages in the expression of its passion, whereby he informs against
himself (something that scarcely occurs to him) by saying that he “will
never forgive himself” for having been capable of sinning like that (for this

way of speaking is just about the opposite of the contrite brokenheartedness
that prays God for forgiveness)—his despair over sin is very far from being
in the category of the good, it is a more intensive category of sin, the
intensity of which is a further deepening in sin. The fact is that during the
time that he was successful in resisting temptation, he became better in his
own eyes than he actually was, he became proud of himself. It is in the
interest of that pride that the past now be altogether left behind. But with
the relapse, the past suddenly becomes entirely present once again. His
pride cannot bear this reminder, and thus this profound distress, etc. But the
direction of this distress is clearly away from God, a concealed self-love
and pride, instead of beginning humbly by humbly thanking God for having
indeed #[224]# helped him resist the temptation for such a long time, by
confessing to God and himself that this was already much more than he had
deserved, and then humbling himself under the recollection of how he has
been.
