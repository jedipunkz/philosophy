Introduction to: The Firmest Bond ‘Al-'Urwah al-Wuthqa’

A Reader's Guide to This Historic Text

The work you hold in your hands is not merely a translation of a
nineteenth-century newspaper. It is a living document, a cry of
awakening, and a political manifesto that speaks as urgently to our
6

time as it did to the generation of 1884. Al-'Urwah al-Wuthqa — The
Firmest Bond — was a weekly Arabic journal published in Paris by
two of the most influential Islamic thinkers of the modern era: Jamal
al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad 'Abduh. Though it lasted only eight
months and produced just eighteen issues, its impact was seismic.
Banned in Egypt and India, its readers fined for possessing it, the
journal nevertheless circulated in secret, shaping the consciousness of
a generation and laying the intellectual foundations for anti-colonial
movements that would follow.

But why should a reader in the twenty-first century — whether
Muslim or non-Muslim, Eastern or Western — care about a short-lived
newspaper from 1884? The answer lies in the profound resonance of
its central themes: the struggle against foreign domination, the
necessity of internal reform, the power of religious and cultural
solidarity, and the conviction that no empire, however mighty, can
permanently subjugate a people united by faith and purpose. These are
not historical curiosities; they are the very issues that define our
contemporary world, from the Middle East to Asia, from the rise of
new imperialisms to the resurgence of religious and nationalist
movements.

The World of 1884: An Empire in Decline, A World in
Transformation

To understand Al-'Urwah al-Wuthqa, we must first understand the
world that produced it. The late nineteenth century was an age of high
imperialism. European powers — Britain, France, Russia, and others
— had carved up much of Asia, Africa, and the Pacific into colonies
and spheres of influence. The Ottoman Empire, once the terror of
Europe, was now the "sick man of Europe," its territories shrinking, its
sovereignty routinely violated by Western governments. The Islamic
world, which had once stretched from Spain to India, was fragmenting
under the pressure of European expansion.

For Muslims, this was a time of profound crisis. The question that
haunted thinkers and leaders was simple but agonising: Why have we
fallen so low, and how can we rise again? Some answered by calling
for blind imitation of the West — adopting its institutions, its laws, its
very worldview. Others retreated into a defensive traditionalism,
insisting that the only path to salvation was a return to the past,
unchanged and unthinking. A third path emerged, more challenging
and more creative: reform that was rooted in Islamic principles but
open to the best of what the West had to offer. This was the path of
al-Afghani and 'Abduh.

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani and Muhammad ‘Abduh

Jamal al-Din al-Afghani (1838-1897) was one of the most enigmatic
and influential figures of his age. Born either in Afghanistan or Iran
(the debate continues), he was a restless wanderer, a "falcon" as he

called himself, who journeyed from Kabul to Istanbul, from Cairo to

Paris, from London to Tehran. He was a philosopher, a politician, and
a tireless opponent of British imperialism. His core message was
simple: Muslims must unite. The divisions between Sunni and Shia,

Arab and Turk, Persian and Indian, were fatal distractions. The real

enemy was European colonialism, which exploited these divisions to

conquer and divide. Al-Afghani was less interested in theological

minutiae than in political action. He believed that Islam, properly

understood, was a religion of power, not of passivity. It demanded
courage, initiative, and a willingness to sacrifice for the community.

It is also worth noting that al-Afghani was involved with Freemasonry
during his time in Egypt and elsewhere. In the context of the
nineteenth-century Middle East, this did not necessarily reflect a
Westernizing or secularizing orientation. On the contrary, Masonic
lodges in Egypt and the Ottoman Empire often served as spaces for
political organizing, networking among reformers, and discussing
nationalist and anti-colonial strategies in a period when open political
activity was severely restricted. Al-Afghani used these networks
pragmatically, as tools to build alliances and disseminate his ideas,
rather than as expressions of any particular ideological commitment to

Masonic principles. This practical, instrumental use of available
platforms exemplifies his broader approach to political action.

Muhammad 'Abduh (1849-1905) was the more systematic thinker of
the two. Born in the Nile Delta, he studied at Al-Azhar, the
thousand-year-old centre of Islamic learning. He was initially drawn to
Sufism, but his encounter with al-Afghani in Cairo transformed him.
He came to see that the stagnation of the Islamic world was not due to
Islam itself, but to the way Islam had been interpreted and practiced.
Muslims had become trapped in taqlid — blind imitation of past
scholars — and had lost the spirit of 1jtihad — independent reasoning.
'Abduh argued that Islam was inherently rational, that it called for the
pursuit of knowledge and science, and that it was fully compatible
with modernity. What Muslims needed was not to abandon their faith,
but to return to its original sources — the Qur'an and the authentic
Sunnah — and to reinterpret them for a new age. This was the birth of
what would later be called Islamic modernism.

The partnership between al-Afghani and 'Abduh was fruitful precisely
because of their complementary strengths: the one a fiery agitator, the
other a thoughtful scholar; the one a political strategist, the other a
religious reformer. Together, they formed a formidable force.

Why Was The Firmest Bond ‘AI-'Urwah al-Wuthqa’
Written?

The immediate trigger for the journal was the British occupation of
Egypt in 1882. Egypt was nominally part of the Ottoman Empire, but
it had become deeply indebted to European creditors. The 'Urabi
revolt — a nationalist movement led by Colonel Ahmed 'Urabi —
sought to break foreign control. Britain, fearing for the Suez Canal and
its route to India, intervened militarily, bombarded Alexandria, and
crushed the revolt. Egypt was now effectively a British colony, though
the fiction of Ottoman sovereignty was maintained.

For al-Afghani and 'Abduh, this was a catastrophe. Egypt was not just
any country; it was the intellectual and cultural heart of the Arab
world, the home of Al-Azhar, and the gateway to the two holy cities of
Mecca and Medina. Its fall to Britain was a profound blow to the
entire Islamic world. From their exile in Paris, the two men decided to
launch a journal that would galvanise Muslims everywhere. Al-'Urwah
al-Wuthqa — the title taken from the Qur'anic phrase meaning "the
firmest bond" — was to be a platform for Pan-Islamic resistance.

The journal's message was threefold:
1. Colonialism is not invincible. Britain might appear all-powerful, but

its strength was an illusion. It was overstretched, dependent on local

1]

collaborators, and internally divided. The vast Muslim population of
India alone could rise up if they were united.

2. Muslims must reform themselves. The real problem was not just the
external enemy but the internal decay: authoritarian rulers, corrupt
scholars, passive populations. Muslims had forgotten the principles

that had made them great: justice, consultation, knowledge, and
courage.

3. The bond of religion is stronger than any other. The West tried to
divide Muslims along national or ethnic lines, but the true community
of believers transcended these boundaries. The Caliphate, though
weakened, remained a symbol and a potential rallying point.

The journal was distributed free, smuggled into Egypt and India
despite the ban. Its language was powerful, its arguments sharp. It was
not a dry academic journal but a weapon of political warfare.

Relevance and Its Limitations

Reading Al-'Urwah al-Wuthga today requires both understanding and
caution. The text is a product of its time, and some of its assumptions
may strike the contemporary reader as dated or problematic. The
authors, for all their brilliance, were men of the nineteenth century.
They shared some of the prejudices of their era, including a tendency
to essentialise both "East" and "West." They wrote in a highly
rhetorical style that can feel overblown to modern ears. Their vision of
Pan-Islamic unity, while inspiring, was never fully realised and faced
immense practical obstacles.

Nevertheless, the core arguments of this text remain powerfully
relevant.

First, the critique of imperialism is as sharp as any written in the
twentieth or twenty-first century. Al-Afghani and 'Abduh understood
that colonial power was not just military but also economic, cultural,

and psychological. They saw how the colonisers used debt, treaties,

14

and local collaborators to control their subjects. They exposed the
hypocrisy of claims that colonialism brought "civilisation." This
analysis resonates with anyone familiar with the history of the Global
South.

Second, the call for internal reform remains urgent. The authors
insisted that Muslims could not blame foreigners for their decline
alone; they had to look at themselves. They criticised authoritarian
rulers, corrupt scholars, and a culture of passivity and fatalism. This is
a challenge that remains relevant in many parts of the Muslim world
today.

Third, the emphasis on solidarity has contemporary resonance. The
authors argued that divisions of sect and nation were fatal weaknesses.
This is a reminder to all communities, not just Muslims, of the
importance of unity in the face of common threats.

Fourth, and perhaps most provocatively, the text offers a model of
engagement with the West that is neither wholesale rejection nor blind
imitation. The authors believed that Muslims could learn from
European science and technology without abandoning their identity.
This is a middle path that remains attractive to many who struggle
with the extremes of nativism and assimilation.

The Larger Struggle

The world of 1884 was one of empires and colonies. The world of
today is also defined by global struggles, but the terms have shifted.
While al-Afghani and 'Abduh focused on British and French
imperialism, the contemporary landscape is more complex. The Cold
War pitted capitalism against socialism, and while that conflict has
largely receded, its echoes remain. The rise of neoliberal globalisation,
the continuing dominance of Western financial institutions, and the
emergence of China as a superpower have created a multipolar world
of competing economic systems.

For the Islamic world, these developments have meant both new
challenges and new opportunities. The promise of socialism, with its
emphasis on anti-imperialism and social justice, once attracted many

Muslim intellectuals and movements. Socialist and nationalist regimes
in Egypt, Syria, Iraq, and elsewhere offered a vision of development
that seemed independent of Western control. Yet these regimes often

became authoritarian, corrupt, and ultimately failed to deliver. The
"red" alternative, whether Soviet or Maoist, proved to be a form of
imperialism in its own right.

Capitalism, meanwhile, has become the dominant global system. The
Gulf states have embraced a petro-capitalist model, while other
Muslim-majority countries have sought to integrate into the global

16

economy. The result has been uneven development: immense wealth
for some, poverty and inequality for others. The ideals of free markets
and individualism have clashed with traditional communal values. The
question of whether Islam can accommodate capitalism — and what
kind of capitalism — is fiercely contested.

Perhaps the most acute contemporary challenge is the Zionist project.
The authors of Al-'Urwah al-Wuthga could not have foreseen the
creation of a Jewish state in Palestine, but their arguments about the
dangers of foreign penetration and the importance of religious
solidarity have been taken up by many Muslim opponents of Zionism.
The Israeli-Palestinian conflict, with its dimensions of colonialism,
displacement, and religious contestation, has become a central symbol
of Muslim grievances against the West. The parallels with the British
occupation of Egypt are not lost on those who see the historical
continuity of struggle.

The "clash of civilisations" narrative, so popular in some circles, 1s
unhelpful. It obscures the real issues: land, resources, sovereignty, and
justice. It also ignores the fact that there are many Muslims,
Christians, and Jews who share a commitment to a more just world.
The authors of this text did not preach a clash of civilisations; they
preached resistance against oppression, regardless of the identity of
the oppressor.

17

a Living Document

Al-'Urwah al-Wuthga is not a sacred text. It is a product of human
minds responding to human crises. It contains wisdom, but it also
contains errors. It offers inspiration, but it also demands critical
engagement. We should not read it as a blueprint to be slavishly
followed, nor as a curiosity to be dismissed. We should read it as a
conversation between the past and the present.

Al-Afghani and 'Abduh believed that Islam could be a force for
revival and resistance. They rejected the idea that Muslims were
destined to be weak and dominated. They insisted that the world could
be changed through knowledge, courage, and unity. These are not just
Islamic values; they are universal human values.

The "firmest bond" they spoke of was the bond of faith. But perhaps

the bond that matters most today is the bond of shared humanity. In a

world torn by conflict, inequality, and environmental crisis, we need

new forms of solidarity. The past offers us models and warnings. The
future depends on our ability to learn from both.

As you read these pages, think of the Egyptian peasant who smuggled
a copy of this newspaper, risking prison and fine. Think of the Indian
scholar who read it in secret. Think of the Sudanese tribesman who

18

fought against British rule. Their struggle was not so different from
the struggles of countless people today. And the voice that called them
to action, that reminded them of their dignity and power, is the voice
you are about to hear.

May it speak to you as it spoke to them.

Note on the Translation

This translation is based on the complete collection of the eighteen
issues of Al-'Urwah al-Wuthqa as published in Paris between March
and October 1884. The text includes the original editorial articles, the
news sections, and various appendices. The translator has made every
effort to preserve the voice and style of the original, including its
rhetorical flourish, its political passion, and its dense, layered
arguments. Some passages are long and complex; this reflects the

20

literary conventions of the time and the urgency of the message. The
translator has chosen to remain faithful to the text rather than to
smooth it over for modern sensibilities.

A glossary of key terms is provided at the end of the book. It is
strongly recommended that readers unfamiliar with Islamic
terminology consult this glossary while reading the text.

Sayyid Jamal al-Din al-Afghani

The Enigma of Origins

Sayyid Jamal al-Din al-Afghani remains one of the most enigmatic
and influential figures in modern Islamic history. Born in 1838 (or
1839, according to some sources), his place of birth has been a subject
of enduring controversy that continues to divide scholars to this day.
The dispute centers on whether he was born in Asadabad,
Afghanistan, near Kabul, or in Asadabad, Iran, near Hamadan, with
compelling evidence supporting both claims.

The Afghan origin theory, which al-Afghani himself actively
promoted, traces his lineage to a prominent Sayyid family in the
Kunar district of Afghanistan, claiming descent from Sayyid 'Ali

al-Tirmidhi, the famous hadith scholar, and ultimately from Imam
Husayn ibn 'Ali, the grandson of the Prophet Muhammad. According
to this narrative, he was raised in a Sunni Hanafi household and
received his early education in Kabul.

33

However, modern scholarship, particularly the influential work of
Nikki R. Keddie, has challenged this account. After extensive archival
research in Istanbul, Paris, India, and Cairo, Keddie and other scholars

argue that al-Afghani was actually born in Asadabad, Iran, to a Shi'a
family. They contend that his claim to Afghan origin was a calculated
strategy to gain influence among the Sunni majority of the Islamic
world and to escape the surveillance of the Iranian Qajar government.
His rival, Sheikh Abu al-Huda, even derisively called him Muta'afghin
— "the one who claims to be Afghan".

Adding another layer to this mystery, the scholar Mustafa 'Abd
al-Raziq, who wrote the biographical introduction to this collection,
offered a third possibility: that al-Afghani's father was from
Mazandaran, Iran, and served as an officer in the Iranian army, sent to
Afghanistan on a mission where he settled and married, giving birth to
Jamal al-Din there. This Persian-origin theory, like the others, remains
impossible to verify definitively, as al-Afghant's early life before his
connection with Muhammad 'Abduh~ in 1870 is shrouded in obscurity
and confusion.

What is certain is that al-Afghani adopted several pseudonyms
throughout his life, including al-Kabuli ("the one from Kabul"),
Asadabadi, Sadat-e Kunar, and even al-Rumi ("the Anatolian"). This
deliberate obfuscation of his origins reflects his profound
understanding that in the Islamic world, one's perceived identity could

34

be as important as one's actual identity in the pursuit of influence and
reform.

Early Education and Intellectual Formation

Regardless of his true birthplace, al-Afghani received an exceptional
education that would shape his worldview and equip him for his life's
mission. His father took great care in his upbringing, providing him
with a comprehensive education that combined traditional Islamic
sciences with rational and philosophical disciplines. He studied Arabic
sciences, religious sciences, rational sciences, and mathematical arts,
along with medicine and anatomy.

According to some accounts, when he reached the age of eight, his
father enrolled him in formal education. He was then taken to Qazvin,
then to Tehran, and finally, while still a youth, to the Twelver Shi'a
shrine cities in Ottoman Iraq (Najaf and Karbala) for further religious
and philosophical study. It is thought that the followers of the Twelver
revivalist Shaykh Ahmad influenced him during this period.

Al-Afghani displayed remarkable intelligence and brilliance from an
early age, with a voracious appetite for books and learning. He
demonstrated a particular fascination with philosophy and rational

oe,

inquiry. George Koutzi (Kotschy) notes that he attracted attention
from his youth due to his brilliant intelligence, strong memory, and
inclination towards military science. He mastered all the classical
works in Persian and Arabic in his youth, and he never ignored any
modern book translated into any Eastern language. He also became
fluent in Turkish, Arabic, Pashto, Dari, and Persian.

His education was not confined to books and traditional scholarship.
At the age of seventeen or eighteen, in 1856-1857, al-Afghani
travelled to British India. He spent about a year there, during which he
studied modern sciences and European methods. This exposure to
Western knowledge and, crucially, to the reality of British colonialism
in India, left an indelible mark on his thinking and would later inform
his anti-colonial activism.

The Wanderings of a "Falcon"

Al-Afghani's life was characterized by constant movement and restless
exploration. He once famously described himself as a falcon, saying:
"T am like a falcon, a long-distance flyer, who sees the vast space of

the universe too narrow for his flight. How then do you want to fetter

my feet and imprison me in a small cage? I am like the royal falcon".

36

After completing his studies, al-Afghani travelled to British India and
remained there for about a year, during which he studied something of
European sciences and methods. He then turned towards the Hijaz to
perform the pilgrimage. He spent about a year travelling through the
Arab lands before reaching Mecca. He returned afterwards to
Afghanistan and entered the service of Amir Dost Muhammad Khan,
rising in his esteem and accompanying him on some of his campaigns.
After the Amir's death, al-Afghani joined Amir Muhammad A'zam
Khan, who was engaged in a struggle against his younger brother,
Amir Sher 'Ali, and became the leader of Muhammad A'zam Khan's
forces, displaying remarkable competence. The Amir, however,
became apprehensive that al-Afghani might aspire to the throne and
gradually ceased to heed his counsel. After their defeat, the two set out
together for India. India was then in the grip of disturbances, and the
British government, fearing that the rebels might establish contact
with al-Afghani, sent him back from where he had come.

Al-Afghani did not feel secure about remaining under the new ruler,
Amir Sher 'Ali, and perceived the uneasiness in the Amir's mind. He
therefore sought permission to leave for the pilgrimage and travelled
by way of India with his servant Abu Turab. When he reached the
Indian frontier, the authorities received him with courtesy and respect,
but did not permit him to remain for long or to meet people except
under the watch of their officials. After about a month, the Indian
authorities sent him from the coast of India to Suez on one of their
vessels at their own expense. He then came to Egypt and remained
there for forty days, during which he visited al-Azhar, mixed with
many Syrian students of learning, and delivered lectures to them at his
residence. He subsequently turned towards Istanbul, where his

Egypt: The Formative Years

Al-Afghani arrived in Egypt in 1871, during the reign of Khedive
Ismail. He stayed for forty days, during which he frequented the
Al-Azhar Mosque and mixed with Syrian students of knowledge,

delivering lectures in his residence. The efforts of Riyad Pasha»

persuaded him to stay, and the Egyptian government granted him an

In Egypt, al-Afghani's home became a meeting place for researchers,
scholars, and students, especially from Syria. He gave lessons in
literature, logic, theology, philosophy, Sufism, jurisprudence, and

astronomy to the brightest students, including a number of the finest

scholars of Al-Azhar. He refused all fees and salaries for his teaching;
his school was his own home. He never went to Al-Azhar as a teacher;
he would only visit it, most often on Fridays.

He encouraged his students to write and compose literary, social, and
political pieces. They became the vanguard of the literary renaissance
in Egypt and the founders of its edifice. Among his most prominent
students was Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh, who would later become the
Mufti of Egypt and his most devoted disciple.

Al-Afghani also joined the Freemasons and advanced in its ranks. He
established a national lodge where he gathered the elite of his students
and followers, training them in public speaking, inspiring them with
his principles, preparing them for action, and awakening their patriotic
sentiments.

He witnessed in Egypt the final days of Ismail's rule and the opening
years of Tawfiq's, when the country was passing through economic,
social, intellectual, and political crises. These crises were connected
with Ismail's extravagance, Tawfiq's weakness, the collision between
the old and the new, political intrigues, and the increasing intervention
of foreigners. Throughout this period al-Afghani remained an
exceptionally sensitive and perceptive observer, distinguished by
broad vision, a powerful memory, penetrating insight, extensive
learning, and tireless energy. His remarkable qualities, eloquence, and
imposing presence drew to him princes, men of high standing,
scholars, and writers, while his gatherings became places of learning,
wisdom, literature, and political discussion.

In 1870, he travelled to Istanbul, where he received a warm welcome

from Ottoman officials and intellectuals. He was appointed a member

of the Council of Knowledge (Majlis al-Ma'arif) under the patronage

of 'Ali Pasha, the Grand Vizier. However, his suggestion that the spirit

of human society is either Prophethood or Wisdom incurred the

displeasure of the Sheikh al-Islam of that time, Hasan Afandi Fahmi.
Al-Afghani insisted on contesting the Sheikh al-Islam, and his trial
lasted for some time, until the order was issued for him to leave the

Ottoman lands.

In 1879, Khedive Tawfiq issued an order expelling him from Egypt,
because his political activities had incensed the English Consul and his

philosophical teachings had provoked the conservative clerics of

The Paris Years and Al-‘Urwah al-Wuthqa«

After his expulsion from Egypt, al-Afghani travelled to London and
then to Paris, where he arrived in 1883. In Paris, he met the
philosopher Ernest Renan, who described him as an Afghan,
untouched by the influences of Islam, belonging to that strong race
settled in Upper Iran. Renan said: "He made an impression on me that
few have made. He had a powerful effect on me... His freedom of
thought and his upright character led me to believe that I was in the
presence of a Muslim of the kind I have studied only in the
ancients—and I would consider him the equal of Avicenna or
Avetroes".

In Paris, al-Afghani began publishing his political ideas, fighting
against the interference of some Western states in the affairs of Islamic
nations, especially India and Egypt.

The greatest manifestation of his political and literary activity in Paris
was the founding of Al-‘Urwah al-Wuthqa (The Firmest Bond)—a
weekly Arabic journal. He was its political director, and Sheikh

Al

Muhammad 'Abduh was its editor. The journal was essentially a
political undertaking with branches in India, Egypt, and other parts of
the Islamic East. Its purpose was to awaken the Islamic nations from
their weakness and urge them to rise up for their affairs, undermining
Britain's position in the Eastern countries.

Only eighteen issues were published over eight months, with the final
issue appearing in October 1884. The journal was thoroughly
suppressed in India and Egypt, to the extent that it had to be placed in
covers to reach its intended recipients. It was even announced in the
official Egyptian gazette that anyone found with Al-‘Urwah al-Wuthqa
would be fined between five and twenty-five Egyptian pounds.
Nevertheless, it had a profound effect on all subsequent movements
for national freedom and liberty in the East.

The Persian Interlude

In 1886, al-Afghani travelled to Iran at the invitation of Shah Nasir
al-Din. He gained a high position, and princes, jurists, and dignitaries
crowded around him. He managed to enrol many of them in the
Masonic order. However, the Shah became suspicious of this, and his
fear of the growing spiritual authority of Jamal al-Din over a people
who surrounded him with reverence and love overwhelmed him.
Al-Afghani noticed the Shah's displeasure and left Persia for Russia,
where he was received with honour.

42

He travelled to Paris to visit its Great Exhibition in 1889, where he
met the returning Shah. The Shah kept tempting him to return to
Persia until he accepted the invitation. The Iranian people quickly
rallied around him once more. However, the Shah feared the reform
movement that al-Afghani was inspiring and was incited against him.
Al-Afghani was eventually escorted beyond the borders by fifty armed
horsemen.

In 1891, the Persian government granted a monopoly on tobacco to an
English company. Al-Afghani wrote a letter to Mirza Hasan al-Shirazi,
the chief jurist, criticising this harmful act. Shirazi issued a fatwa
prohibiting every believer from smoking tobacco until the government
withdrew from its project, forcing the government to comply. The call
for freedom and constitutional reform grew stronger in Persia,
eventually leading to the assassination of Shah Nasir al-Din in 1896.
Al-Afghani was implicated in this assassination.

Final Years in Istanbul

In 1892, al-Afghani went to London again and stayed there for eight
months, devoting all his efforts to opposing the Shah. Sultan Abdul
Hamid II invited him to Istanbul. After initial hesitation, al-Afghani

43

accepted. The Sultan provided him with a beautiful house near the
Yildiz Palace and assigned him a monthly salary. He spent five years
of his life in Istanbul, "living amid the deceptive appearances of the
Sultan's favour, and countless intrigues hatched by the men of the
palace against him". He repeatedly begged to be allowed to travel, but
he was detained for the rest of his life in a gilded captivity.

Al-Afghani died on 9 March 1897 (or 7 March, according to some
sources), at the age of 58, following a prolonged, painful illness. Most
Iranians and others who have written biographies of al-Afghani insist
that his death was not natural, and that he was given a toxic substance
on his lip, causing a condition resembling cancer—a plot orchestrated

by Abu al-Huda. The official cause of death was cancer of the jaw.

The Sultan ordered him to be buried within two hours of his death. His
corpse was escorted by a large number of police, for fear of a sudden
uprising by his followers, who doubted the causes of his death. His
grave in Istanbul remained dilapidated and abandoned for many years.

Legacy and Impact

Al-Afghani is considered one of the founders of Islamic Modernism
and a pioneer of modern Muslim anti-colonialism. He advocated for

44

Pan-Islamic unity to counteract European influence, calling for a
religious and cultural revival and organizing a united response to
Western pressure. He sought to revive Islamic thought and liberate the
Muslim world from Western influence.

He was less interested in minor differences in Islamic jurisprudence
than he was in organizing a united response to Western pressure. He
actively worked to establish the foundations of mutual rapprochement
between Sunnis and Shi'as.

His career as a thinker and activist had a deep impact on the Islamic
world and continues to be a source of inspiration and controversy for
many today. His influence remains alive in Turkey, Egypt, and Iran,
and his project of Islamic Modernism that he developed in his lectures,
writings, and political activism continues to resonate.

His life was a turbulent journey through Afghanistan, India, Turkey,
Egypt, Persia, and Russia. He connected with the Afghan government
in his youth, participating in its internal wars, and with the awakening

movements in every Eastern land he visited. He travelled to many
European capitals, wrote in their newspapers, spoke in their
assemblies, and mixed with their political, scientific, and literary men.

He witnessed the intrigues of British colonialism in Afghanistan and

India, and the British pursued him in Egypt and elsewhere.

45

He never became attached to any country as a homeland, and the idea
of nationalism, in this sense, was not part of his social doctrine. For
this reason, he intensely hated the English and lived as a bitter enemy
to them. He saw progress in Europe and decline in the Eastern lands
he visited. He witnessed foreign influence and the evil of autocratic
rule. His thought was directed towards awakening those lands as a
whole and individually, holding in his heart a love for these Eastern

Islamic kingdoms, which he sought to unite.

He was a man who lived as a combatant, influential in the events of
the Islamic East over a period of twenty years, more than any other
man of his time. His voice was loud and clear, echoing in the ears of
everyone who lived in his time from his creed. Every region of the
Islamic world heard his call.

Sheikh Imam Muhammad 'Abduh

Origins and Early Life

46

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh was born in the year 1849 (or 1266 AH,
according to some sources) in the village of Mahallat Nasr, in the
Shubrakhit district of the Beheira province, in the Nile Delta of Egypt.
The exact date of his birth remains uncertain, though most scholars
agree on the year 1849. He came from a family of modest means but
respectable standing. His father, 'Abduh Khair al-Din, was a man of
notable physical strength and prowess, skilled in archery and
horsemanship, earning him respect and influence in his community
beyond what his material wealth would have afforded. His mother,
whose name is not recorded in most historical sources, belonged to the
famous Arab tribe of Awlad 'Ali from the Western Desert region of
Upper Egypt, a lineage that would later instill in her son a deep sense
of pride in his Arab identity and his connection to the desert traditions
of his ancestors.

The circumstances of his parents' marriage were unusual and spoke to
the turbulent times in which they lived. His father had been forced into
exile, fleeing from some rulers, and during this period of
displacement, he married into this prominent Arab tribe. This
backstory of exile and displacement would later shape Muhammad
'Abduh's own experiences with exile and his deep empathy for the
displaced and oppressed.

His father initially intended for him to follow a different path. The
young Muhammad 'Abduh was sent to the village school, where he
memorized the Qur'an, an achievement that was the foundation of all

47

Islamic education at the time. He then went to Tanta, to the Ahmadi
Mosque, one of the great centres of religious learning in Egypt, to
perfect his recitation and to begin his studies in the Islamic sciences.

However, his early educational journey was not straightforward. He
was initially deterred from seeking knowledge and returned to his
village to take up farming, marrying at a remarkably young
age—despite his youth. This period of withdrawal from formal
education reflects the economic realities of rural Egypt at the time,
where practical labour often took precedence over scholarly pursuits.

The Sufi Awakening

The turning point in Muhammad 'Abduh's life came through his
maternal uncle's household, which contained a Sufi man named
Sheikh Darwish Khidr. This encounter would prove transformative.
Sheikh Darwish Khidr recognized the young man's potential and
intervened in his life, restraining his youthful exuberance, making him
a Sufi, and sending him back to pursue knowledge in Tanta.

This Sufi awakening introduced Muhammad 'Abduh to the world of
spiritual discipline and inner purification. Sufism, or Islamic
mysticism, emphasizes the cultivation of the inner self, the struggle

48

against one's own ego, and the pursuit of a direct, personal connection
with the Divine. For a young man of rural Egypt, this was a profound
intellectual and spiritual opening.

The influence of this Sufi upbringing on his character cannot be
denied. The Sufi path directed his youthful passions towards sacred
pleasures—the pleasures of the gnostic (‘arif). As Ibn Sina expressed
in his Isharat (Remarks): "The gnostic storms in a burst, stirs in an
hour, calms in a longing, and subsides in a moment, as if he sees the
Truth within it." The gnostic is brave, for he is removed from the fear
of death; generous, for he is removed from the love of falsehood; and
pure, for his mind is occupied with the Truth.

These teachings, in their luminous interiority and sacred spirit,
disciplined and adorned Muhammad 'Abduh's soul, shaping his
character in ways that would later be evident in his work as a reformer.
He learned to combine spiritual depth with intellectual rigour, a
combination that became a hallmark of his approach to reform.

The Azhar Years

After returning to his studies in Tanta, Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh
travelled to Cairo to join Al-Azhar, the thousand-year-old centre of

49

Islamic learning that was, at that time, the intellectual heart of the
Islamic world. Al-Azhar was both a place of profound scholarship and
a bastion of conservatism, where the centuries-old traditions of Islamic

jurisprudence and theology were taught and preserved.

At Al-Azhar, he attended the lessons of its senior scholars in various
Azhari sciences—yjurisprudence, theology, hadith, tafsir, and Arabic
language and literature—all while continuing his engagement with
Sufism. He was an eager student, absorbing the vast knowledge of the
Islamic tradition while also beginning to question its rigid structures
and limitations.

The turning point in his intellectual development came with the arrival
of Sayyid Jamal al-Din al-Afghani in Egypt in 1871. Muhammad
'Abduh attended al-Afghani's lessons and became his constant
companion. This relationship would prove decisive for his intellectual
and personal development. In a short time, the benefits of his
association with the Sayyid and his knowledge became evident.

Al-Afghani introduced Muhammad 'Abduh to a broader intellectual
horizon that included Western philosophy, modern science, and a
critical approach to Islamic tradition. He encouraged Muhammad

'Abduh to think independently, to question received wisdom, and to

see religion as a force for social and political transformation rather
than mere personal piety.

50

Under al-Afghani's influence, Muhammad 'Abduh wrote a treatise on
Sufism entitled Risalat al-Waridat (The Treatise on Spiritual
Influences), commented on the Maqamat, and wrote commentaries on
the poems concerning the Muhammadan creed of monotheism. He

also began writing articles in newspapers that attracted considerable
attention, marking his entry into public intellectual life.

The First Crises

In 1876, Muhammad 'Abduh obtained the 'Alamiyya (worldly)
certificate, second degree, after an examination in which it became
clear that the senior scholars were critical of his independent,
intellectual approach, which aligned with modern methodologies. This
was a Significant achievement, but the examination itself revealed the
tensions that would mark his entire career. The conservative scholars
of Al-Azhar were suspicious of his intellectual independence and his

openness to modern ideas. This suspicion would never fully fade.

Consequently, he was appointed a teacher at the Dar al-'Ulum (House

of Sciences) school and at the Khedivial School of Languages, rather

than at Al-Azhar itself. This reflected the desire of the authorities to
keep him away from the heart of traditional Islamic learning.

51

When Sayyid Jamal al-Din was exiled from Egypt in 1879,
Muhammad 'Abduh was dismissed from his posts and banished to his
village, where he was denied the freedom to move. This was the first

of many periods of exile and persecution that he would endure.
However, he was eventually pardoned and made an editor for the
official newspaper Al-Waqa'l' al-Misriyya (The Egyptian Gazette), and
later appointed its editor-in-chief. This position gave him a platform to
reach a wider audience and to begin his work of reform in earnest.

The 'Urabi Revolution and Exile

The 'Urabi Revolution of 1881-1882 marked a turning point in modern
Egyptian history. Ahmed 'Urabi~, a colonel in the Egyptian army, led a
movement against the growing influence of Europeans in Egypt and
the autocratic rule of the Khedive. Muhammad 'Abduh, like many
Egyptian intellectuals of the time, initially supported the revolution,
seeing it as an opportunity for reform and national liberation.

When the revolution was crushed by British forces following their
bombardment of Alexandria in July 1882, Muhammad 'Abduh was
tried with the revolutionary leaders. He was sentenced to three years
and three months of exile. He spent a portion of this exile in Syria,

a2

where he continued his intellectual work and began to build a
reputation as a reformer beyond the borders of Egypt.

In Syria, he was able to continue his teaching and writing in relative
freedom. He composed Risalat al-Tawhid (The Treatise on the
Oneness of God), which would become one of his most influential
works, and began working on other projects that would later become
central to his reformist thought.

The Paris Years and Al-'Urwah al-Wuthga

His master, al-Afghani, called him to Europe, and together, in Paris,
they published the journal Al-'Urwah al-Wuthqa (The Firmest Bond),
which lasted only about eight months. This journal was a bold
experiment in Pan-Islamic journalism, published in Arabic and aimed
at awakening the Islamic world to the dangers of colonialism and the
need for reform and unity.

Only eighteen issues were published over eight months, with the final
issue appearing in October 1884. The journal was thoroughly
suppressed in India and Egypt, to the extent that it had to be placed in
covers to reach its intended recipients. It was even announced in the
official Egyptian gazette that anyone found with Al-'Urwah al-Wuthgqa

53

would be fined between five and twenty-five Egyptian pounds.
Nevertheless, it had a profound effect on all subsequent movements
for national freedom and liberty in the East, serving as a model for
reformist and anti-colonial journalism.

During his time in Paris, Muhammad 'Abduh also met the philosopher
Ernest Renan and engaged in debates about the relationship between
Islam and science, demonstrating his ability to engage with Western

intellectuals on their own terms while defending the Islamic tradition.

Beirut: The Productive Years

After the cessation of the journal, Muhammad 'Abduh moved to
Beirut, where he devoted himself to teaching, authorship, and writing.
This was a period of intense intellectual productivity. He composed
Risalat al-Tawhid (The Treatise on the Oneness of God), his most
important theological work, which sought to present Islamic theology
in a rational and accessible form.

He also translated into Arabic Al-Radd ‘ala al-Dahriyyin (Refutation
of the Materialists), which had been written in Persian by Sayyid
Jamal al-Din al-Afghani. This translation was part of his broader effort

54

to combat materialism and atheism in the Islamic world, and to defend
religious faith on rational grounds.

He provided commentaries on Nahj al-Balagha (Peak of Eloquence),
the collection of the sermons and letters of Imam Ali ibn Abi Talib,
and the Maqamat of Badi' al-Zaman al-Hamadhani, the classical work
of Arabic prose. These commentaries demonstrated his deep
engagement with the Arabic literary tradition and his ability to
combine traditional scholarship with modern analytical methods.

In Beirut, he also married his second wife after the death of his first,
and began to establish a new life in exile.

The Return to Egypt and Rise to Prominence

Upon returning from his exile, Muhammad 'Abduh was appointed a
civil judge, then a counsellor in the Civil Court of Appeal. He was
then made the agent (deputy) for the Administration of Religious
Endowments (al-Awgqaf). These positions gave him practical
experience in the legal and administrative systems of Egypt and
allowed him to begin his work of reform from within the system.

>

Eventually, his renown grew to the point that he was appointed the
Mufti of the Egyptian territories, the highest religious office in Egypt,
remaining in this post until his death in 1905. As Mufti, he was the
chief interpreter of Islamic law, whose opinions (fatwas) were legally
binding on the Egyptian state.

During his tenure as Mufti, he wrote a significant report on the reform
of Sharia courts, arguing that they needed to be modernized to meet
the needs of a changing society. He issued important fatwas on a wide
range of issues, from family law to financial transactions, always
seeking to reconcile Islamic principles with the realities of modern
life.

He produced a commentary on Juz’ 'Amma (the 30th part of the
Qur'an), as well as commentaries on several Surahs and some
problematic verses. These commentaries sought to make the Qur'an
more accessible to ordinary Muslims and to present its teachings in a
rational and modern light.

He authored the book Al-Islam wa al-Nasraniyya ma'a al-'IIm wa
al-Madaniyya (Islam and Christianity in Relation to Science and
Civilisation), which engaged with the claims of modern science and
Western civilization, arguing that Islam was not opposed to reason or
progress, but could be fully compatible with them.

56

He wrote valuable articles for magazines and newspapers on religious
and secular subjects, using journalism as a medium for reform and
enlightenment. His articles reached a broad audience and helped to

shape public opinion on a wide range of issues.

The Reformer's Vision: Three Principles

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh's call for religious reform revolves around
three fundamental principles that would shape his work and his
legacy:

1. Liberating thought from the shackles of imitation (taqlid=), so that
the mind is not subjugated to any authority other than that of proof,
and is not dominated by the leaders of this world or the leaders of
religion. This principle was the foundation of his entire project. He
argued that blind imitation of past scholars had stifled intellectual
creativity and prevented Muslims from adapting to the changing
circumstances of modern life. Muslims needed to think for
themselves, to question received opinions, and to use their reason to
understand their religion.
2. Preserving religion as a beacon for science, not a rival to it. Each
has its own function; both are human needs, and neither can replace
the other. He rejected the claim that religion and science were

>]

necessarily in conflict. He argued that Islam, properly understood, was
a religion of reason and that the Qur'an encouraged the pursuit of
knowledge. He saw no contradiction between faith and reason, and
believed that the revival of Islamic civilization required a
reconciliation between religious faith and scientific inquiry.
3. Understanding religion in the manner of the early ancestors (salaf),
before the emergence of sectarian disputes, and returning to its
original sources to acquire its knowledge. The primary sources of
Islam, in its pure simplicity as brought by the Prophet himself, are the
Qur'an and a limited amount of the Sunnah (Prophetic tradition) in
practice. This principle, which later became central to the Salafiyya
movement, argued that the later developments of Islamic theology and
jurisprudence had obscured the original purity of the faith. Muslims
needed to return to the foundational texts and understand them
directly, without the accretions of later centuries.

The Man and His Character

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh was a man of remarkable presence and
charisma. He was of medium stature or slightly above, with a full,
robust physique, strong muscles, and agile, energetic movements. His
facial features were beautiful in their entirety and detail, enhanced by
a luminous white beard that surrounded a bright, radiant face with a
fair forehead from which the hair had receded, rising to reveal a space
that spoke of intellect, will, and intelligence. His eyes, of moderate

58

width, possessed a captivating brilliance that filled the beholder with
awe, admiration, and life.

His companion and biographer, Mustafa 'Abd al-Raziq, wrote: "I bear
witness that the beauty of Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh was among the
forces God had harnessed for his genius; and his sweet, compelling
voice was also among the forces of his genius."

The late Qasim Bey Amin, in describing the Master, wrote: "The
goodness of his soul reached a degree that was almost limitless.
Goodness attracted him as a magnet attracts iron—he would rush
towards it and strive for any benefit for others, general or particular.
He was a refuge for the poor, the orphans, the oppressed, the rejected,
and those afflicted by any calamity. And the people of Al-Azhar, who
are the most in need of help, for they are the backward ones, unable to
defend themselves in the arena of our modern life, in the midst of
present-day civilisation. He would exert his effort for the dearest
person to him, but he would also strive for the one who needed help
even if he knew that person had wronged him, slandered him, and
conspired with his enemies to spread slander and gossip, which never
ceased against him for a single day during his life. The Master
believed that evil is absolutely useless, and that tolerance and
forgiveness for everything and for every person are the best cure for
wrongdoing, and the most beneficial for reforming its perpetrator."

59

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh engaged with governmental positions,
political affairs, the scientific and literary movement, and acts of
charity. In all these fields, he had a fruitful activity, a reforming
opinion, and a determination that knew no retreat or fatigue. However,
the field in which he spent the best of his health, energy, intellect,
knowledge, and eloquence was the field of religious reform.

Influence and Legacy

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh had opponents who hated him, plotted
against him, and placed obstacles in the path of his reform; yet no one
could diminish his status or shake his dignity. On the contrary, all
sought to win his approval, saw proximity to him as a source of pride,
and considered being counted among his followers an honour. His
ability to maintain his dignity and influence despite the fierce
opposition he faced testifies to the strength of his character and the
power of his ideas.

He was beloved, venerated, and recognized for an unmatched rank of

Imamate among the elite classes of Egyptians and among the foreign

communities in Egypt. His fame spread throughout the Eastern lands,

and all eyes turned towards him. He was respected by both Muslims

and non-Muslims, by Egyptians and by foreigners, by traditionalists
and by modernists.

60

Had Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh wished, he could have been wealthy,
leaving his respected, ailing widow a fortune to ensure her comfort in
her old age after him, and to protect her from the humiliation of
poverty. But the Master Imam was too great of spirit and too deeply
contemptuous of this world to exert his effort in accumulating wealth.
Thus, he lived great and poor, and died poor and great.

His death on 11 July 1905 marked the end of an era, but his influence
continued to grow in the decades that followed. His students,
including his successor as Mufti, Sheikh Muhammad Bakhit, and the
founder of the Salafiyya movement, Rashid Rida, carried on his work
and spread his ideas throughout the Islamic world.

His approach to religious reform, his defence of reason and science,
and his call for Islamic unity and anti-colonial resistance, continue to
be debated and engaged with to this day. He remains one of the most
important and influential figures in modern Islamic thought, a bridge
between the traditional Islamic past and the modern world.

The Three Pillars of His Reform

61

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh's reformist vision can be understood
through three interconnected pillars that shaped his entire project:

The Intellectual Pillar: Liberation from Taqlid

At the heart of Muhammad 'Abduh's reform was the call to liberate the
mind from the shackles of blind imitation (taqlid). He argued that for
centuries, Muslims had been taught to follow the opinions of past
scholars without question, stifling intellectual creativity and
preventing the development of new ideas. This had led to the
stagnation of Islamic civilization and its inability to respond to the
challenges of the modern world.

He called for a return to independent reasoning (ijtihad), arguing that
Muslims had the right and the duty to think for themselves, to question
received opinions, and to interpret the sources of their religion in light
of their own circumstances and needs. This was not a rejection of
tradition, but a call to engage with it critically and creatively.

The Spiritual Pillar: Religion as a Beacon for Science

62

Muhammad 'Abduh rejected the claim that religion and science were
in conflict. He argued that Islam, properly understood, was a religion
of reason that encouraged the pursuit of knowledge and the
exploration of the natural world. He saw no contradiction between
faith and reason, and believed that the revival of Islamic civilization
required a reconciliation between religious faith and scientific inquiry.

He argued that Muslims had fallen behind the West not because Islam
was opposed to science, but because they had neglected the rational
and intellectual dimensions of their own tradition. By returning to the
original sources of their faith and engaging with them in a spirit of
inquiry, Muslims could reclaim their intellectual heritage and compete
with the West on its own terms.

The Historical Pillar: Returning to the Ancestors

Muhammad 'Abduh called for a return to the understanding of the
early ancestors (salaf), before the emergence of sectarian disputes and
the later developments of Islamic theology and jurisprudence. He
argued that the early Muslims had understood their religion in its pure
simplicity, and that the later additions and elaborations had obscured
this original purity.

63

By returning to the original sources of Islam—the Qur'an and the
Sunnah—and understanding them in the manner of the early ancestors,
Muslims could recapture the authentic spirit of their faith and use it to

guide their lives in the modern world. This approach would later
become the foundation of the Salafiyya movement, which continues to

be influential to this day.

A Final Reflection

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh was a man of profound contradictions—a
traditionalist who embraced modernity, a scholar who was also a
politician, a Sufi who was also a rationalist. He was a man of his time,
shaped by the challenges and opportunities of the late nineteenth
century, but his ideas continue to resonate in the twenty-first century.

He was a reformer who sought to revitalize Islam, to make it relevant
to the modern world, and to enable Muslims to compete with the West
on equal terms. He believed that Islam was not opposed to reason,
science, or progress, but that Muslims had neglected these dimensions
of their tradition. By returning to the original sources of their faith and
engaging with them in a spirit of inquiry, Muslims could reclaim their
intellectual heritage and build a better future.

64

His legacy is complex and contested. Some see him as a great
reformer who modernized Islam, others as a heretic who undermined
tradition. But whatever one's view, there can be no doubt that he was

one of the most important and influential figures in modern Islamic
thought, a bridge between the traditional Islamic past and the modern
world.

As Mustafa 'Abd al-Raziq wrote in his introduction to this collection:
"The legacy of a scholar is immortalised in the books in which he
deposits his genius; the artistic work of an artist is immortalised in his
art. But the reformer is he who prepares for his community a supreme
ideal that it had not known before him, and strives to turn hearts
towards that ideal—a striving that reveals the strength of his own soul
and his determination, and the abundance of genius and inspiration
bestowed upon him."

Sheikh Muhammad 'Abduh was such a reformer. He prepared for his
community a supreme ideal of a reformed, rational, and dynamic
Islam that could compete with the West and build a better future for
Muslims. He strove to turn hearts towards that ideal, and in doing so,
he revealed the strength of his own soul and his determination, and the
abundance of genius and inspiration bestowed upon him.

Chapter Three: Christianity and Islam, and Their

“Indeed, in that is a reminder for whoever has a heart or gives ear

God created man as a manufactured being, made easy for him the path
of work for himself, guided him to invention and innovation, and
decreed for him his sustenance from the work of his own hands. He
even made him the pillar of his own existence and the support of his
own life. All his states—hardship and ease, roughness and comfort,
Bedouinism and civilization—are the products of his own actions. His
provisions come from cultivating the earth or tending livestock; his
garments and what protects him from heat, cold, and injury are the
work of his own hands in weaving or stitching; his shelters and
dwellings are nothing but manifestations of his estimation and

secure the necessities of his livelihood and the needs of his life, so too

does he work to learn how to work, and to be able to continue

his vital affairs, he is a manufacturing artisan, as if he were separate

This is the human being in his eating, drinking, clothing, and dwelling.
Leave him in this state and look at him from another

among whom he grew up and was raised; the targets of his thoughts,

effect on psychological dispositions and spiritual qualities except in

terms of preparation and receptivity. And that effect 1s weak;

But have you considered, with this, that physical actions arise from
faculties and spiritual resolves, and that the spirit is the conquering
authority over the body? I think there is no room for doubt in this

human soul, at its origin, is like a polished tablet. The first thing
inscribed upon it is the sketch of religion, and then it proceeds to all

If we look at the two religions we are discussing, Christianity and

peaceableness and gentleness in everything. It came to abolish

and its vanities, and it preached the necessity of submission to every
authority ruling over its followers, leaving the wealth of rulers to
rulers, and keeping away from personal, racial, and even religious
conflicts. One of the Gospel’s commandments is: “Whoever strikes
you on your right cheek, turn to him the other also.” And its teachings
state that kings have authority only over bodies, which are perishable;
the true, lasting authority over souls belongs to God alone.

If this is the case, it is astonishing—indeed, it baffles the mind—to

are the very people who compete in boasting and vainglory for the

Chapter Four: The Decline of the Muslims, Their Inertia,
and the Cause Thereof

“Hold fast to God’s rope all together; do not split into factions.”
(Qur’an 3:103)

The Muslims possess a fervour in their religion, a strength in their
faith, and a firmness in their certainty, by which they boast over all
other creeds. In their belief lies the strongest of bonds for connecting
one to another. It is deeply ingrained in their souls that faith in God
and in what their Prophet brought 1s the complete guarantor of
happiness in both abodes, and that whoever is denied faith is denied
both happinesses. They are more distressed by one of them slipping
from his religion than they are distressed by death and annihilation.
This state—as it is among their scholars—is deeply rooted among
their common people. So much so that if any person, in any part of the
earth, learned or ignorant, who bears the mark of Islam, in any region
and of any race, were to apostatize from his religion, you would see
the one who receives this news burning with anguish and regret,
uttering invocations of distress (hawqalah and istirja') and counting
this calamity as one of the greatest disasters that could befall the one

afflicted, indeed, all who share his religion. If such an incident were
110

mentioned in history and a Muslim reader were to read it even two
hundred years later, he would not be able to control the agitation of his
heart and the boiling of his blood. Anger would drive him to fury and
compel him to relate what he saw, as if he were narrating something
strange or speaking of something wondrous.

Muslims, by virtue of their Sharia and its explicit rulings, are all
required to preserve what falls within their sovereignty of lands. Every
one of them is commanded to do so, with no distinction between the
near and the far, nor between those united in race and those differing
in it. It is an individual obligation (fard 'ayn) upon every one of them;
if a group fails to protect their territory, the sin falls upon all of them.
Among their duties, in the path of protection and preservation of
sovereignty, 1s the expenditure of wealth and lives, the undertaking of
every hardship, and the braving of every peril. They are not permitted
to make peace with those who overpower them in any situation until
they regain sovereignty, humbled before them alone. The Sharia even
went so far in demanding from them sovereignty over those who
oppose them to the extent that if a Muslim is unable to escape the
authority of another, emigration (hijra) from the land of war becomes
obligatory upon him. These are established principles in Islamic law,
known to the people of truth, and not altered by the interpretations of
the people of desires and whims in any age.

Every Muslim is urged by a caller within his own being, reminding
him of what the Sharia demands of him and what faith imposes upon

111

him—a caller of truth, which remains from the inspirations of his
religion.

Yet with all this, we see the followers of this religion in these days,
some of them heedless of what befalls others. They do not feel the
pain that others feel. The people of Baluchistan saw the movements of
the English in Afghanistan within sight, yet no zeal stirred in them,
nor did they have any anger for their brothers. The Afghans witnessed
the English intervention in Persia without being vexed or disquieted.
The English troops marched through the Egyptian lands, killing and
ravaging, yet no support was seen in the souls of their brothers, who
overlooked the course of their blood, whose eyes reddened at the
sights before them, under their feet, and around them.

The state of the Muslims—their deep faith and their strong sense of

the call of truth in their souls, combined with this condition they are

in—is a matter that compels wonder and invites bewilderment. The
explanation of its cause is as follows:

Intellectual ideas, religious doctrines, types of knowledge and
perceptions, and psychological feelings—though they are the motive
for actions, and by their decree actions are issued by the will of the
Almighty, the All-Knowing—yet actions nurture, strengthen, and
imprint these ideas upon the soul, until they become what we call

112

faculty and character. The effects that are appropriate to them then
follow.

Certainly, man is human only through his thought and his beliefs, but
what is reflected in the mirror of his mind from the visions of his sight
and the perceptions of his senses affects him profoundly. Every
observation generates a thought, every thought has an effect on an
impulse, and from every impulse an action arises. Then, from action, it
returns to thought, and the action and interaction between actions and

Chapter Eight: Virtues and Vices, and Their Effects

Virtues are dispositions of the soul whose inherent nature requires

cohesion in all their actions and purposes, or most of them. The unity

single person, moving with a single will and seeking in its movement
a single goal. The sum of all virtues is justice in all actions. When
justice encompasses a group of the human species, its individuals
cooperate and support one another. Each individual person has a
unique existence, and Divine care has instilled in him the powers by

existence, so that a share of its collective work returns to them. This is
as God has instilled in the organs of our bodies and our personal
