I
PANTHEISM AND NEO-HEGELIAN PHILOSOPHY

The term ‘pantheism’ belongs to the period of modern
philosophy and it is generally understood to imply religious oppro-
brium. Literally, it would seem to represent a form of theistic
thought. Actually, it has been regarded as a variant of atheism.
Few people have willingly had their philosophical views called pan-
theistic, and religious philosophers, in particular, have endeavored
to avoid the stigma of ‘pantheism’ with conspicuous alacrity. Yet
there have been some avowed and even enthusiastic claimants for
the title. |

The significance of this term, however, is by no means certain.
Meaning genetically ‘all is God’, it has been used to designate
not only every highly monistic system of thought which emphasizes
the conception of deity, but also views which are not so thoroughly
monistic and even systems which offer no explicit recognition of a
Supreme Being. Amid an extensive confusion of usage, two fairly
specific meanings are, nevertheless, pre-eminent. The first equates
the term with a philosophy of abstraction which identifies all real-
ity with deity and conceives God as a Being, Principle, or Substance
beyond every distinction of normal experience. The second desig-
nates as ‘pantheistic’ any philosophy which posits the reality of
concrete existence but presents existence as a completely determined
system within the divine nature. These are obvious types of panthe-
ism, and possibly the only types which can be clearly distinguished.
The semi-popular view that pantheism represents a complete im-
manence of the divine in a unified system of reality leaves out of
account that a relative transcendence of God might mean that God
is none the less integral to reality as a whole and that a view
involving some conception of transcendence may be a form of
pantheism.

The criteria of pantheism must, accordingly, be those implied
by the types distinguished above. The truly obvious tests of pan-
theistic thought are the questions as to (1) whether a philosoph-
ical system posits any genuine existence apart from God, and (2)
whether, if God is conceived as all-embracing, finite beings are re-
garded as having any essentially real freedom within the scheme
of actuality. At first thought, this second test might seem unneces-
sary. If God is all-inclusive, how can free beings exist within his

3

nature? Ingenious have been the attempts, however, to harmonize
the ideas of human freedom and the absoluteness of God. To do
justice to these efforts we must appeal to our second criterion. It
is the acid test of pantheistic thought, when reality is not conceived
as fundamentally abstract.

Among the streams of thought to be called ‘pantheistic’ are
the Hegelian and Neo-Hegelian philosophies. Their opponents
have frequently labelled these views ‘pantheism’, and their advo-
cates have anxiously endeavored to repudiate the title. It is the
purpose of this study to inquire into the justice with which the term
‘pantheism’ may be used to characterize the Neo-Hegelian view-
point. Two formulations of this philosophical perspective have
been selected as the special objects of inquiry. The first is that of
Edward Caird, one of the earliest and most orthodox Neo-
Hegelians of Great Britain. The second is A. S. Pringle-Pattison’s,
a recent British philosopher who has earnestly endeavored to avoid
the difficulties characteristic of the earlier forms of Neo-Hegelian-
ism. Caird’s philosophy will be our first concern, and we shall now
apply our criteria to his thought.

II
THE PHILOSOPHY OF EDWARD CAIRD

In the first place, we must note that Caird distinguished his
viewpoint from all the major forms of pantheism which he recog-
nized. The objective pantheism of India in which thought “rose
above all particular objects, even the highest, and confused them
together in the one Being’”’ is a type of philosophy to which he ex-
pressed emphatic opposition. Spinoza, seeking “the divine in an
absolute substance which, indifferently, was conceived as either
having an infinite number of attributes or none at all”, was equally
to be rejected because he confined reality to the blank oneness of
substance. he Spencerian infinite ‘‘which is beyond even the dif-
ference of subject and object’, which presents “‘the idea of God as
a universal principle against subjective and objective religion
alike’, and which allows us to think of a God ‘‘who is within as
He is without us” is ultimately to be conceived as unknowable.
‘The attempt to say anything definite of Him must involve the con-
tradiction of excluding Him from other things, or other things
from Him.’”?

pounded a view of God as a synthetic ‘principle of unity’ compre-
hending and conserving everything finite with all its especial dis-
tinctiveness. For Caird, God was the Absolute, the personal inte-
grated totality of all that is real, but Caird endeavored to think
of Him as concrete rather than abstract. The extent to which he
failed in doing this determines for us, from one standpoint, the de-
gree to which his view is liable to the charge of pantheism. The
extent to which he succeeded without at the same time providing
adequately for a relative finite independence indicates the degree
to which, from another viewpoint, he is liable to the same charge.
It is possible, in fact, that a supposedly concrete ‘principle of unity’
may be a substantially abstract conception or else so deterministic
in its implications that it provides for no reality but God. A con-
crete ‘principle of unity’, if such is an actual possibility, may be so
thoroughly systematized, so rigidly determined in its nature, and
its particulars so devoid of individual significance that it must be
thought to represent a pantheistic viewpoint. In any case, we may
well inquire whether Caird’s philosophy, from either perspective,
gives adequate place to finite individuals within the divine economy.
First of all, let us consider whether there is any marked tendency
toward abstraction in his thought.

Caird’s very language, to begin with, is decidedly abstract.
The phrase ‘principle of unity’, which is the key-phrase of his
system, is most noticeably so. A ‘principle’ in itself contains the
idea of a general law which has been abstracted from a series of
particular facts and which leaves largely out of account all their
differences. A ‘principle of unity’, in the second place, which Caird
intends to signify that which is both concrete and universal, gives
on the contrary, an additional abstractness to that implied by the
term ‘principle’ as such. It is undoubtedly true, as Hegelians and
Neo-Hegelians have always been ready to remind us, that there
can be no unity without differences through which the unity runs.
Nevertheless, a point of view may so emphasize the unity as to
minimize the differences, and this is the actual tendency of Caird’s
most focal phrase.

Scarcely secondary in his usage, moreover, is the term ‘self-
consciousness’. This, it is pointed out, denotes the coherent form
of all experience or, in Kantian phrase, the ‘transcendental unity of
apperception’. This usage, however, is arbitrary and unnatural.
The term really suggests the consciousness of self as distinct from

other objects, and thus indicates an abstraction from the total con-
sciousness of normal experience. If, nevertheless, we take the term
in the sense which Caird intends, it is none the less abstract. It
represents the hypostatizing of the form of experience as such and
its separation from its own continuously concrete content.

This tendency toward abstraction is confirmed by other usages.
The ideas of relation, system, and unity were Caird’s most charac-
teristic thoughts. These indeed imply the existence of actual ob-
jects, for there must be things to be related, systematized, and
unified. Yet they may be a sort of necessary evil or, at any rate,
quite incidental to the view. A few of Caird’s statements will show
that this tends to be the case in his philosophy. “All existence’, he
maintained, ‘‘is but the manifestation, and all knowledge but the
apprehension, of relations.’’* “Reality lies—not . . . in the mere
individual taken by itself, as common-sense supposes—nor, as SCci-
ence seems to teach, in the mere particular which is related to other
particulars; it lies in the relation, or the principle of relation, itself,
—in the universal which differentiates or particularises itself and
yet is one with itself in its particularity.’’® “We cannot say a single
rational word without expressing or implying a principle of unity
which manifests itself in and through the difference of self and the
world; and the utmost goal of all our knowledge, nay, we may say
of our whole rational life, is to discover what is contained in that
principle.”* The “ultimate unity which is presupposed in all differ-
ences is the unity of thought with itself, the unity of self-conscious-
ness, and . . . in this unity is contained the type of all science,
and the form of all existence; in other words, that I=I is the for-
mula of the universe.’ Such statements unquestionably empha-
size the primacy of relations and of unity, and such an emphasis
means a slurring over distinctions, a magnifying of unity at the
expense of the individual particulars which are organized into a
system. It is quite true that reality seems to be an essentially sys-
tematic whole and that unless we conceive it as such we cannot
understand how its parts hold together. It is also true that Caird
endeavors to avoid the abstract extreme of Spinoza’s philosophy
and of the Upanishads of India. Yet it is equally certain that his
own system tends to be a metaphysical monism in which finite ex-
istence is secondary to the thoughts of divine absoluteness and

That this tendency is not a mere matter of language or of

by means of which Caird elaborated his viewpoint. The first is a
conception of reality as organic. For him the universe was an
organic whole and partook throughout of that remarkable inte-
gration which distinguishes an organism. Such a view leaves little
room for belief in the distinctiveness or relative independence of
the ‘parts’ of reality, for every organism we know is an interpene-
trating and self-acting whole. Its parts are woven into a single
fabric of being, and the actions of the parts are activities of this
being. An organic view of reality, in fact, is necessarily panthe-
istic. It vests all determinations in the ultimate Being in which all
parts are integrated and as the expression of which they always
act. Of Caird’s particular idea of the organic nature of the real
this is still more decidedly true. It leaves still less room for finite
distinctiveness and relative finite freedom. ‘‘By the organic unity

expressed in, its life, and therefore there is not in it the possibility
of the supreme reconciliation of intelligence with itself.”® The unity
of the world, accordingly, is to be conceived in terms of the unity
of self-consciousness, and this is a higher type of unity than that
of the ordinary organism. Such a view is, therefore, strongly and
abstractly pantheistic.

Let us consider, however, Caird’s other conception, that of
self-consciousness itself. It is in the light of this conception that
he defines an organism, and in this fashion he expounds his idea
of the ‘principle of unity’. Self-consciousness, we have noted, is an
essentially abstract idea, but we must consider Caird’s specific usage
of the term more fully. In this connection, Caird makes Kant’s
philosophy his starting point, but his pre-suppositions are those
of Hegel and to the latter he is wholly loyal. Kant’s definition of
existence in terms of ‘position’ he interprets in Hegelian fashion
to mean that existence is always existence for a self’ and that,

At this point, in fact, Caird made a most disastrous leap, so
far as finite individuality is concerned. His concept of a cosmic
Self-consciousness is wholly at variance with the idea of separate
finite consciousness. For if finite consciousness is just part of an all-
inclusive consciousness, how can it have that distinctiveness which
it seems to possess? Conscious intelligence, most certainly, may
involve different organizations of factors in all of its parts, and
that fact may seem to give to finite minds an especial importance
and dignity. These minds, though, are but parts or manifestations
of the Absolute. It is the absolute Mind which ramifies in these
divergent ways. The Absolute is Universal Intelligence. There is
no intelligence which is not part of that Intelligence. Finite minds
are neither separate nor self-determining. Does this not imply a
view of reality as an intellectual Substance quite comparable to
the abstract Substance posited by Spinoza? For “reality”, we must
remember, “‘lies,—not . . . in the mere individual taken by itself”
but in the “principle of relation, itself,—1in the universal which
differentiates or particularises itself and yet is one with itself in
its particularity.”

It seems evident, indeed, that Caird has given to rationality
a pre-eminence which reduces everything to a single self-manifest-
ing thought principle. Everything that exists exists for and as an
expression of intelligence. Matter has no permanence “‘in its own
right, but as the necessary attribute of the object of consciousness
as such.” The world is the manifestation of intelligence, and the
key to nature is the key of mind. Nothing can be conceived, in
fact, which is not the self-manifestation of thought. Thought must
have opposites and differences from which to move, but the struc-
ture of reality is itself the product of rational thought. Mind
which furnishes the key to reality and which provides the synthesis
by which everything is brought into an inclusive relation, appears
twice in its own synthesis. It “appears once as one of the oppo-
sites, and again as the unity which transcends the opposition.’’*
Is not reality reduced to an intellectual substance in which certain
things inhere as landmarks of universal thought? God, it is true,
is more than thought. Like the finite consciousness he finds an
object in opposition to Himself, but that object exists as the neces-

The universe is simply ‘such stuff as thoughts are made of’, and

synthetic process. On this basis, therefore, there appears to be no
opportunity for a just recognition of finite selfhood. Everything is
submerged within universal rationality. The hypothesis that the
unity of the universe is the unity of self-differentiating intelligence
commits Caird to a monism in which the finite consciousness must
lose all distinctive reality. ‘he self-manifestations of thought can-
not be essentially different from the self-manifestations of substance.
And if the philosophy of a universal substance must be entitled ‘pan-
theism’ because it submerges all individuality, the metaphysics of
universal thought deserves the same label. The fact that thought
is somewhat characteristically self-conscious, and thus suggestive of
personality, is no outlet from this conclusion. For an absolute Self-
consciousness which engulfs all finite selves destroys the very type of
distinction it was intended to preserve. It becomes an abstract, rath-
er than a concrete, universal. And this tendency toward abstraction
seems even more marked when we recall that ‘self-consciousness’ is
but the form, and not the content, of all consciousness.

If, however, there is any uncertainty that Caird’s philosophy
tends to be a type of abstract pantheism there can be no doubt that
so far as his thought is concrete it is a form of concrete pantheism.
Reality is throughout the self-expression of the Absolute. God, as
the principle of self-conscious unity, is also the principle of self-de-
termination in the universe. Such a principle in a most highly inte-
grated system leaves no room for self-determination by the parts of
the system. It may indeed be true that this principle does not oper-
ate “like a law imposed upon a foreign matter, for its only matter
is itself.’’** Yet finite beings could not on this basis be considered in
any degree self-determining except in so far as they are identified
with the Absolute. In that case, they would cease to be finite, and
reality would consist of millions of Absolutes, a wholly illogical con-
ception. As Caird himself states the matter, “in determining,” the

itself in them.” ‘The Absolute is all-embracing, and there is noth-
ing which is not part of itself and its direct self-expression. If,
therefore, we could overlook the marked tendency toward an ab-
stract monism in Caird’s philosophy, we should still be left with a
completely deterministic though concrete instance of pantheism.

Now it must be admitted that Caird did not recognize this
predominantly pantheistic trend in his thought. He considered, in
fact, that the idea of a concrete universal provided against the logi-
cal submergence of finite individuality. Whether it is possible for
such a conception to do justice to individuality is not our concern at
this point. What we must contend is that Caird’s particular state-
ment of this idea was too rigidly monistic to allow for the reality of
finite selves. He did, to be sure, make an especial effort to protect
the genuine existence of finite beings within the system of universal
intelligence by positing the idea that the whole is in each of its parts.
Yet this idea simply leads to a paradox of finite selfhood whereby
the finite individual is implicitly absolute, and thus there is an incon-
ceivable plurality of Absolutes, or else the finite being is just a pe-
culiar concentration of the Absolute as such and nothing in itself.
The monistic emphasis of Caird is not at all consonant with the first
alternative, and the second is inevitably pantheistic. Let us, never-
theless, consider this idea in greater detail.

What then did Caird mean by the idea that the whole is in
every part? It was “the idea of the world as an organic system
whose centre lies in a self-conscious intelligence” and which “reveals
in every existence a centre of self-individualising energy.’** A
‘world in which the central principle is a self-determining Being,
while, in one aspect of it, it seems to be a unity in which no room is
left for difference, in another aspect of it breaks into an infinite
number of fragments each of which seems to be centered in itself
. . . it is a universe in which ‘every thought is truth, and every
particle of dust an organization;’ a macrocosm made up of micro-
cosms, which is all in every part.’’"

How can all be in each of its parts and yet be the whole of
which they are the parts? Caird claimed that it was as microcosms
within a macrocosm that this could be the case. It is conceivable
that one organism may exist within another, as in the instance of
the microscopic germ which subsists within a living body. This mi-

a case of Jonah in the belly of the whale, a situation which is in-

not fundamentally different. The unborn being undeniably partakes
of his mother’s nature, but only partially so. The mother does not
actually indwell her young. Nor is the latter integral to its parent.
A macrocosm made up of microcosms, accordingly, is an inconceiv-
ability. It cannot be an integrated totality and functional unity such
as Caird posits in his hyper-organic view of the universe, and hence,
this explanatory figure appears out of harmony with the central
emphasis of his thought. We can see, to be sure, that an organism
is substantially and actively present in all of its parts, but these lat-

they such, they would not be semi-independent realities such as finite
selves are regarded. We are compelled, therefore, to try to think
of this idea, that the whole is in its parts, in other terms.

According to Caird, self-consciousness is the true organic type.
The ordinary organism is only partly organic. This, most certain-
ly, is taking extensive liberty with terminology, but in self-conscious-
ness he recognized a much greater integration than in a living body.
The former, therefore, he thought to represent a higher organic
category than the latter. In self-consciousness, the world stands
over against the subject and is the necessary condition for the sub-
ject’s recognition of itself. The world, also, is a spatial-temporal
unity. Objective existence is thus an integrated whole, and it is also
organic to the self whose experience of it is necessary to his ex-
perience of himself. Thus self-consciousness represents the highest
conceivable integration of existence. The finite consciousness em-
braces significant wholes of selfhood and the world. The absolute
Consciousness embraces all reality, including all finite selves and
their worlds. The former are microcosms, the latter, a macrocosm.
The former exist within the latter, and the latter is individualized
in the former. This, indeed, appears intelligible but, nevertheless,
it is logically incongruous, and for two sets of reasons which we
shall consider.

The first relates to the fact, already presented, that to think
of the Absolute as present in finite self-consciousness is to think of
finite beings as actually or implicitly absolute, in which case they
are not really finite and there is more than one Absolute. If, on

the other hand, we are to think in terms of a single Absolute the
self-individualizations of that Absolute must be mere phases in its
experience or else unreal manifestations. They cannot be relatively
free and distinctively real beings.

McTaggart presented this paradox excellently when he stated
that “it is necessary that in some way or another the whole of the
unity shall be in each individual. ... Yet . . . to suppose that
the unity exists in the individuals as isolated is to destroy the unity.
The unity must be completely in each individual. Yet it must also
be the bond which unites them. How is this to be?’’*® McTaggart
resolved this paradox for himself by recourse to the Hegelian cate-
gory of cognition, of which consciousness is the pre-eminent phase.
He maintained that “if we take all reality, for the sake of con-
venience, as limited to three individuals, A, B, and C, and suppose
them to be conscious, then the whole will be reproduced in each of
them. A, for example, will, as conscious, be aware of himself, of
B, and of C, and of the unity which joins them in a system. And
thus the unity is within each individual”. ‘At the same time the
unity is not in the individuals as isolated. For the whole point of
saying that the unity is for A, is that it exists both out of him and
in him.”’*® It is hard to see, however, how finite selves even from
this standpoint could contain the whole in the sense that the Abso-
lute does. McTaggart, indeed, admitted that the parts do not
contain the whole “in the same way that the whole contains
itself,’’*° and he also recognized that such incipiently absolute selves
as he posited could not be parts of a more comprehending and
integrating personal Absolute. McTaggart’s solution, therefore,
simply shows how utterly inconceivable it is that finite consciousness
can exist as part of an absolute Consciousness and yet be a sepa-
rate consciousness. Either we must think of finite consciousness
being engulfed by the Absolute, giving us an abstract form of pan-
theism, or we must conceive of a multiplicity of Absolutes, a most
ridiculous conception and absolutely out of harmony with Caird’s
Hegelian monism.

the total consciousness of a finite self. The idea has indeed been

itself, which is in every part. The grain of sand is an object for a
self. As such it manifests itself in relation to other phenomena
with which it helps to constitute an objective situation. In this
fashion it implicitly points to these other phenomena and the com-
plete situation. Likewise, it has, by its very nature as object, a
direct reference to the subject for which it exists and also to the
unity which embraces both the subject and the whole content of
objective phenomena. Thus it may be regarded as pointing to, and
reflecting the entire totality of which it is a part. It contains the
idea of the whole within itself. If, then, we think of the finite self
in this same manner, it is but the idea of the whole which i¢ con-
tinually endeavors to articulate. Like the grain of sand, it is for-
ever pointing to the Absolute and appearing to be something in
itself because it reflects the Absolute, but, like a mere symbol or
sign, it is lost in that which it eternally manifests. | |

It may seem that the self-conscious individual, as the true
organic type must constitute a ‘unique’ expression of reality. But
if we take the finite self as the standard and think of that self as a
unity par excellence, then we must either have no place for Caird’s
thoroughly integrated Absolute or the standard must be separated
from the self in which it is found and identified with an all-inclusive
reality, of which the self after all is but a feeble and unreal type.
In the latter circumstance, it is but an aspect of the absolute Con-
sciousness, and finite individuality is merely a reflection of the one
real Individual. We cannot maintain that the whole is really in
every part and that every finite consciousness is a distinctive organ-
ism without dissociating the Absolute into innumerable self-con-
scious centers.. For either the Absolute, as the self-conscious and
self-determining totality of being, includes all consciousness and.
all determinations within Himself, in which case there is no real
finite consciousness, or there is no self-consciousness which is ulti-
mately absolute. The alternative which the main trends of Caird’s
philosophy really represent is that in which the finite individual is
submerged. Obviously he does try to do more justice to the indi-
vidual, but actually all finite existence succumbs before his idea of

the Absolute.

Caird’s view is thus unquestionably pantheistic, and in an
abstract sense. For, if the finite individual is lost in the Absolute,

IV
THEISTIC SUGGESTIONS

The foregoing conclusions do not mean that every ‘absolute’
is implicitly pantheistic. One may believe in the existence of abso-
lutes without being a pantheist. To believe, for instance, in the
constancy of gravitation, of the velocity of light, or even of some
subsistent ends, is to accept a form of absolutism which does not
necessarily equate reality with deity. So long as one does not posit
a single all-embracing Absolute, pantheism is not necessarily impli-
cated. This is particularly true if one recognizes the existence of
variables, as well as constants, in the universe.

One might, indeed, accept the relative supremacy of deity.
One can regard God as ultimate, or essentially so, without beliey-
ing that He is integrally related to all existence. One may conceive

26

of Him as having direct connections with the universe, and actually
indwelling it, without presuming that all reality is organic. God’s
relation to some parts of existence may well be indirect, though
capable of more immediacy than frequently exists. It is possible,
in fact, to conceive of God as approximating ‘the Absolute’ without
being completely such. This view would be practically an absolut-
ism, though theoretically it would be otherwise.

The idea of God as a relatively supreme Being, furthermore,
has an especial significance. In the first place, as already stated,
it avoids the definitely pantheistic implications of complete abso-
lutism. Secondly, it avoids the negative suggestion in the concept
of a ‘finite’ God. ‘The term ‘finite’ emphasizes the limitations,
rather than the essential supremacy, of deity. It is one-sided in its
reference, and its outright adoption, in contradistinction to ‘Abso-
lute’, overlooks the greatness of deity for which philosophical
absolutism stands. There can be no doubt that a God who is not
wholly absolute is in at least some respects finite. And to believe
in the quasi-independent distinctiveness of human personalities is
to recognize some finitude in deity. Yet compared with the finitude
of men, animals, and things, God may be considered absolute. He
may approximate the Absolute without submerging all existence
within his nature. Such a deity would be at once the adequate Ob-
ject of religious trust and worship and the highest principle of
philosophical explanation. Thus one might maintain a view which
for practical purposes could be considered ‘absolutism’ and which
would not be pantheistic. Theoretically, however, it would not be
actual absolutism, and a better name could surely be devised.

The problem of the conception of deity centers, therefore, in
the question as to whether there is a relatively supreme power
which makes for righteousness. An intelligible answer to this ques-
tion depends on the nature of righteousness, and this from the
angle of human life most surely involves the most adequate kind
of human living. Is the most harmonious and complete life wholly
a matter of human achievement, or is it facilitated and fostered
by objective circumstance? The evidence is certainly conflicting,
and we cannot then maintain that God is equally at work through
the entirety of being. The fact that people learn by bitter, and
often ‘evil’, experience is no argument that evil in itself is ulti-
mately good. It is the good in man which enables him to use the
bad for his own enlightenment and discipline. He is clearly worse
for each surrender to the bad. Yet the circumstances of human
lives do inculcate visions of a highest kind of living and move men

27

to actualize these visions. This is often true though men succumb
to lesser ends.

A supra-human righteous Spirit thus attends our lives. We
do not know that it has power beyond our world, but it does seem
native here and so appears to have a fundamental locus in the
universe. Such a Spirit must be relatively supreme. The potency
of human values is most intelligible in this light. Yet this does not
mean an identity of God and being. Nor does it necessitate an
idealistic metaphysic, though this latter is not in question in this
thesis.
