We write at a moment when the postwar liberal international order has effectively
collapsed—from Washington to Caracas, Kiev to Tehran, and beyond.’ The juridical fictions
that once constrained power now persist largely as ornamentation or rhetoric, invoked
selectively by those best positioned to violate them.’ Sovereignty is overridden by
necessity, legality by expedience, and norms by force, while institutions meant to arbitrate
conflict function increasingly as after-the-fact legitimators rather than real restraints. What
presents itself as “crisis” is in fact exposure: the revelation that the order in question
depended less on law than on asymmetries of power temporarily held in check. The present

conjuncture therefore marks not a deviation from the system’s logic, but its consummation

commons itself: resources, labor, land, and information must be withdrawn from regimes

of limitless extraction and re-placed under forms of trusteeship that cannot be dissolved

calls into question not merely particular policies or doctrines, but the deeper assumptions

Political Ontology

Now, political ontology concerns the mode of being presupposed by a political order.° It
asks not how power is exercised, but what must already be the case for power to be intelligible
and operative at all.’ Every political system assumes a prior configuration of reality—what
exists, what endures, what may be transformed, and what may not. Sovereignty, property,
ownership, citizenship, class, gender, violence, and legitimacy are not merely institutional
facts; they are expressions of an underlying ontology that determines whether the world
appears as a field of sacred trusts, finite resources, administrable populations, or disposable
matter. It precedes ideology, law, and economics. It operates at the level where the basic
terms of reality are already decided: what a “person” is, what counts as “nature,” whether
value is intrinsic or exchangeable, whether authority is immanent or transcendent,
whether time is cyclical, progressive, or terminal. Ideologies narrate these assumptions,
laws stabilize them, and economic systems instrumentalize them—but none of these
domains generate the ontological ground they presuppose. Political ontology as we are
defining it here is thus not a belief-system but the silent grammar that makes belief, legality,

ideology, and economic rationality possible at all.

Political ontology therefore defines what entities may act and be acted upon
politically. Every political order draws an ontological boundary around agency and
passivity: who may speak, who may decide, who may own, who may be sacrificed, and what
may be extracted, traded, or destroyed without remainder. Humans may be reduced to labor-
units, populations to statistics, land to resource, bodies to risk-factors, or elements to
commodities—or, alternatively, persons, places, and forces may be treated as ontically

inviolable. These are not ethical choices layered onto a neutral world; they are ontological

decisions about what kinds of beings exist in the political field and what kinds of relations
to them are permitted. And it structures power as a fact of being, not merely of coercion.
Power persists most effectively when it no longer appears as power, but as reality itself. By
shaping what feels “natural,” “inevitable,” or “rational,” a political ontology embeds
domination into perception, habit, and common sense. Coercion enforces order at its limits,
but ontology sustains it from within: it determines the horizons of the thinkable and the
livable, such that alternatives appear unrealistic, utopian, or unintelligible before they are

even contested.

In Bayani terms,” political ontology is not a theory about power but a disclosure of
the rank of being within which power may or may not appear at all. The Bayan begins by re-
situating existence itself: all things are signs (ayat) whose reality is measured not by utility,

exchange, or administration, but by proximity to the Divine Command. To wit,

..lt has not commanded any one of the existential possibilities (mumkinat)’
except through the noesis (ma‘ifat) of its own Logos-Self (nafs) and the
affirmation of the Unicity (tawhid) of the inmost Reality of Its Being; for
everything other than It is a created thing in Its Presence, having been
created by Its Command (amr). Truly, to It belongs the creation and the

Command, from before and after—that is Lord of the worlds! ’°

From this standpoint, politics is not primary; it is derivative. A political order is valid
only insofar as it conforms to the ontological truth of creation as trust (amana), not as
possession. The question is therefore not “who rules?” but “what mode of being has been
assumed as real?”—for any regime that misrecognizes the ontological status of the world
already stands outside legitimacy, regardless of its procedures or laws. This is why, per the

ontological verdict. The Bayan’s political ontology thus can be said to revolve around this

things in the commonly understood modern sense; they are the primordial conditions of
Manifestation (zuhir) through which bodies, lives, and worlds appear. In Marxian terms, it
is the base of Bayani political economy. Thus, to render these elements into objects of

property rather than as divine trusts held in custodianship. In Bayani terms, a system that

Being itself. Such a system is therefore ontologically illegal because its very mode of existence
contradicts the order of creation. The Bayan thus draws an absolute boundary around
political agency. No authority—state, market, or collective—may claim sovereignty over
what God has placed beyond ownership. When earth becomes “real estate,” water a
“utility,” air a “carbon market,” and fire an “energy asset,” the political order has already
crossed from governance into metaphysical transgression. This is not a matter of ethics or
regulation; it is a falsification of reality. The Bayan names this falsification in advance as
negation (nafy)"* and withdraws recognition from any regime built on it. Power that rests on
ontological falsehood is therefore not merely oppressive; it is void. In other words, in this
perspective the structure of the current human world is one built upon and animated by a

metaphysical inversion and so an ontological illegitimacy.

Given this, by implication the Bayan understands domination as something that
embeds itself into the texture of a world built upon Western capitalism because this world
is one animated by negation (nafy) of all first principles. For his part, Ibn ‘Arabi would say
that this is a civilization inordinately imbalanced leaning towards non-existence (‘adam)
than Being (wujiid). When people come to experience the buying and selling of the elements
as normal, inevitable, or rational, ontology has already been colonized and so, in some
sense, negated from proper alignment and balance with first principles. Coercion becomes
secondary and is an inevitable derivative of the misalignment while compliance is achieved
with this misalignment because the world itself has been re-presented as a warehouse of

resources and human beings as units of administration within it—this being the very

inversion of what the Qur’an makes of veridical humanicity as the “vicegerent of God on earth”

rupture. By declaring the Four Elements inalienable, it collapses the metaphysical ground

but the re-emergence of another world—one in which legitimacy flows from ontological

Now, contemporary discussions of political ontology—ranging from Heidegger’s
account of world-disclosure and being-in-common,” through Arendt’s conception of the world
as the condition of politics,’* to Agamben’s analyses of sovereignty and bare life,” Esposito’s
work on community and immunization,”* and more recent anthropological approaches
associated with Bruno Latour,” Philippe Descola,” and Eduardo Viveiros de Castro”’—share
a common intuition: that politics presupposes an ontology. Power, law, and legitimacy are
intelligible only against prior assumptions about life, materiality, subjectivity, and the real.
In these frameworks, political conflict is increasingly understood as a struggle over worlds,
over what is allowed to count as nature, person, resource, or community. Yet in most of these
approaches, ontology remains descriptive or diagnostic. It reveals the conditions under
which political forms arise, but it rarely functions as a criterion capable of invalidating a
political order at the level of Being itself. By contrast, the Bayan does not treat ontology as
a neutral horizon to be mapped, pluralized, or indefinitely negotiated. It advances an
ontological judgment. Existence is disclosed as entrusted manifestation rather than
alienable matter, and this disclosure carries juridical force at the most fundamental level.
Political authority, on this view, does not derive primarily from consent, procedure, or
efficacy, but from correspondence to truth at the level of being. Where much contemporary

political ontology remains post-foundational—exposing hidden assumptions while

suspending ultimate verdicts—the Bayan is unapologetically foundational in a different
sense: its ground is ontological rank, proximity to Reality as such, rather than coherence

within an internally consistent system.

This difference becomes clearest around property and economy. Modern political
ontology, even in its critical and decolonial forms, typically treats ownership,
commodification, and extraction as historical, ethical, or ecological problems.” The Bayan
treats them as ontological errors. By prohibiting the buying and selling of the Four Elements,
it denies that the conditions of manifestation themselves can ever become objects of
exchange. This is not a regulative intervention within political economy, but a refusal of
the metaphysical assumption that renders political economy possible in its modern form.
From within this horizon, capitalist legality is not merely unjust; it is unreal, sustained by
an inversion of first principles. So where contemporary theory often locates politics in
openness, deferral, and perpetual contestation—whether through proceduralism,
pluralism, or post-sovereign negotiation—the Bayan insists on the legitimacy of decision. Not
decision as authoritarian closure, but decision as the refusal to suspend truth indefinitely
within procedural time. The modern administrative order governs by postponement,
dispersing responsibility through process, jurisdiction, and neutrality until judgment itself
becomes unthinkable. The Bayan names the point at which deferral becomes illegitimate.
It does not seek inclusion within existing grammars of legitimacy, nor does it aim to
pluralize them. It displaces them by insisting that a shared world requires ontological
coherence, and that without a decision about what is real, politics collapses into
management. In this sense, the Bayan does not merely participate in contemporary debates
on political ontology; it confronts them with a demand they are structurally trained to

evade.

existentialism, nor does it treat Islamic metaphysics as a decorative supplement to modern
theory. It begins from the conviction that certain intellectual traditions—specifically those
of Suhrawardi (d. 1191), Ibn ‘Arabi (d. 1240), Mulla Sadra (d. 1641), al-Ahsa’i (d. 1826) and

social theory lacks, particularly when confronted with contemporary forms of power that
operate without spectacle, repression, or overt coercion. As such, this specific collection of
essays should be read as a supplement to The Wretched of the Corporate State: A Fanonian Essay
(2025). Thus, what the essays here undertake is a deliberate act of translation into the
language of critique, and one conducted from the side of these traditions rather than from
within the epistemic sovereignty of modern theory. The critical vocabulary is retooled
under the pressure of Illuminationist, Akbarian, Sadrian, Shaykhi, and finally Bayani
ontologies, such that critique no longer presumes the modern subject, economy, or state as
its implicit horizon. In this sense, translation becomes an instrument of reversal: modern
power is rendered intelligible only insofar as it can be measured against ontological

registers that it neither generated nor can fully absorb.

Now, modern existentialism, from Kierkegaard (d. 1855) through Heidegger (d.
1976) and Sartre (d. 1980), frames the problem of existence primarily in terms of
subjectivity: anxiety, choice, freedom, authenticity and alienation.” Its central drama unfolds
between the individual consciousness and a world experienced as absurd, hostile, or
indifferent. This framework was historically indispensable, but it remains bound to a
narrow ontological horizon. It presupposes a flat world of beings, a homogeneous field of
appearance, and a conception of power that must announce itself through prohibition,
violence, or bad faith in order to be recognized as such. As a result, modern existentialism
remains structurally tied to a conception of power that is episodic, confrontational, legible,
and, above all, Anglo-European at the level of experience. In this, power is something that
interrupts freedom rather than something that configures its horizon in advance; it is
encountered as an obstacle, a command, or a falsification, not as an ambient condition that
quietly shapes what can appear as reasonable, actionable, or even thinkable. This leaves
existentialism poorly equipped to account for regimes of governance in which domination

The claim by some that Siyyid ‘Ali Muhammad Shirazi, the Primal Point, the Bab represents

grounded in an unspoken criterion: that a messianic figure must achieve immediate
worldly success—political consolidation, demographic growth, or institutional

theological nor historical in any rigorous sense; it is sociological, and selectively applied. If
this standard is applied consistently, it collapses at once. By the expectations of Second

overthrow imperial rule, did not inaugurate visible justice, and was executed by the state

claims—by contemporary Jewish standards—decisively refuted by events. What later
“rescued” Jesus from the category of failure was not the outcome of his mission, but
centuries of posthumous reinterpretation, delayed eschatology, and eventual imperial
adoption. His validation is retrospective and civilizational, not intrinsic to the historical

What emerges from the ordinances whose ratio decidendi (reason for deciding) we have
isolated above is not a miscellany of pious counsels or marginal ethical refinements, but the
jurisprudential core of the Bayan. These rulings are the points at which the Bayan decisively departs
from inherited Islamic legal paradigms and articulates a new juridical logic grounded in ontological
inviolability rather than sovereign command. Across commerce, movement, dwelling,
correspondence, bodily integrity, and the handling of texts, the same principle is reiterated with
remarkable consistency: no human being may be used, coerced, diminished, appropriated,
surveilled, or instrumentalized without explicit consent, and even consent is circumscribed by a
deeper duty to preserve dignity, modesty, and freedom from harm. What gives these ordinances
their jurisprudential weight is not the severity of their sanctions, but the clarity with which they
identify what counts as injury—often at thresholds far below what classical law or modern positive
law would even register. Seen together, these rulings establish a legal order in which coercion itself
becomes the paradigmatic wrong. Compulsion in travel “even by a footstep,” entry into a house
without permission, imprisonment, bodily alteration “by a hair’s breadth,” the destruction of
books, the withholding or interception of correspondence, the failure to answer another with
clarity, even the act of looking at another’s writing without consent—each is treated not as a minor
infraction, but as a violation of the very conditions under which a soul may appear before God
without grief. The repeated formula of marital prohibition and fixed material penalties is not

incidental; it functions as a juridical marker that these acts rupture the most intimate and socially
foundational bonds. Law here is not primarily punitive or corrective, but protective of ontic thresholds:
the boundary of the body, the home, the word, the page, the path, and the face of the other. In this
sense, the Bayan legislates less for order than for non-domination.

The two Fifth Unity ordinances—permitting commerce only on the basis of known mutual
consent, and commanding the community to “encompass every soul” from birth to death with what
it possesses—are especially revealing. Together they establish the Bayan’s economic axiom:
exchange is licit only where it does not threaten subsistence, dignity, or security, and provision is
not charity but obligation. Commerce is affirmed, not abolished, yet it is radically subordinated to
consent and care. The right to trade is inseparable from the duty to ensure that no soul falls outside
the sphere of provision. This already anticipates the logic of the Ninth and Tenth unities, where
even the smallest subtraction from a person’s body, appearance, or dignity is treated as an absolute
wrong. What modern jurisprudence would separate into contract law, welfare, criminal law, and
human rights are here unified by a single criterion: does this act cause grief to a soul, even minimally,
without right?

When these ordinances are read in light of the 11th Gate of the Ninth Unity, their deeper
coherence becomes unmistakable. That Gate’s prohibition on buying and selling the four elements
does not function as an isolated economic oddity, but as the ontological key to the entire legal
edifice. By declaring the elements—earth, water, air, fire—inalienable, the Bayan denies that the
material conditions of life can ever become absolute property or objects of speculative domination.
Human beings, whose bodies and lives are constituted from those very elements, therefore cannot
be treated as commodities, resources, or instruments either. The ban on elemental commodification
grounds the later bans on bodily harm, coercion, imprisonment, humiliation, and destruction: what
cannot be owned at the level of nature cannot be violated at the level of the person. Law here flows
downward from ontology, not upward from individual sovereign will. Thus, the society envisaged
by the Bayan is neither statist nor anarchic, neither theocratic in the conventional sense nor liberal
in the modern one. Instead, it is theophanocratic: a society structured around guardianship without
domination, authority without coercion, and law as the maintenance of inviolable limits rather than
the extraction of obedience. The Witnesses of the Bayan do not rule by discretion but intervene
only where those limits are breached. Violence, confinement, censorship, surveillance, and
humiliation are not merely regulated; they are rendered almost unthinkable by being placed
beyond the pale of legitimate action. The animating ontology is one in which existence itself—
lighted, entrusted, and transient—demands modesty (haya’) as the supreme political virtue. In that
sense, these ordinances are not peripheral rules but the juridical skeleton of ametaphysics of non-harm,

What becomes striking when the specific Bayanic ordinances we have isolated above are
read alongside Ibn ‘Arabi’s seventeenth chapter of the Bezels of Wisdom (“The Bezel of the Wisdom of
Existence in the Word of David”) is that both corpora converge on a shared reconfiguration of law away
from acquisitive sovereignty and toward gifted responsibility, even as they operate in very different
registers. Ibn ‘Arabi’s Davidic chapter insists that true authority—prophetic, juridical, or political—
does not arise from kasb (acquisition), merit, or contractual exchange, but from wahb: pure divine
bestowal. David’s khilafa/vicegerency is not earned, bargained for, or reciprocally compensated; it
is given “minnd fadlan” (a bounty from Us), without counter-claim. This logic of non-reciprocal gift
underwrites the Bayan’s core legal prohibitions: the refusal to commodify bodies, movements,
dwellings, texts, or even elements themselves. What is given by God—existence, form, dignity,
capacity—cannot become an object of coercive exchange without ontological violation. In both Ibn
‘Arabi and the Bayan, law is legitimate only insofar as it guards what has been given, not insofar as it
extracts obedience or surplus. This convergence is clearest when one juxtaposes Ibn ‘Arabi’s
insistence that David’s vicegerency is explicitly khilafat hukm—a deputation to judge “bil-haqq” (in
truth) without following lust or whim—with the Bayan’s relentless prohibition of coercion at the
most granular level: compulsion “even by a footstep,” imprisonment as such, striking “ever,” taking
“a hair’s-breadth,” humiliating the body or altering its appearance, or entering a house or a text
without permission.

In Ibn ‘Arabi, deviation from divine judgment is not framed as juridical error but as
following a movement of the self not authorized by revelation; in the Bayan, the same principle is
juridified as a zero-tolerance regime toward domination. Both refuse to locate authority in force,
violence, or administrative command. Instead, authority is legitimate only when it aligns with a
pre-given ontological order in which the human is already under divine care and therefore cannot
be treated as raw material for rule. The Bayan’s sanctions—marital prohibition, fixed
compensations, and the intervention of the Witnesses of the Bayan—map closely onto Ibn ‘Arabi’s
distinction between khilafa zahira (the outward caliphate of the sword) and khilafa ma‘nawiyya (the
spiritual or ontological vicegerency). Where Ibn ‘Arabi argues that killing pertains only to the
outward caliphate born of imagined dual sovereignty (“if there were two gods...”), the Bayan
systematically empties law of lethal force altogether. Killing, striking, imprisoning, even
humiliating are not merely regulated; they are ontologically excluded. The Bayan thus radicalizes
Ibn ‘Arabi’s insight: if all effective judgment in the world is, in truth, God’s judgment by the
sovereignty of the Divine Will, then human law has no warrant to replicate divine power through
violence. Its sole task is to prevent grief from entering the created order—and since all current

It could not be preserved without being betrayed. It therefore developed, under conditions

organization not as hierarchy but as solidarity; remembrance not as mourning but as
mobilization. To remember the martyrs was to refuse closure. To recall their deaths was to
indict the order that killed them—and every order that resembled it such as the Baha’i

Administrative Order.

This is why Red Babism terrifies power. It exposes the lie that religion exists to
legitimate authority. It reveals instead that authority exists, historically, to suppress
Revelation. Here, as it were, eternity belongs to God while history belongs to the devil. As
we said, courts that tolerated Christians, Jews, and Zoroastrians once declared Babis worse
than unbelievers, because the latter did not threaten the metaphysical foundations of rule.
The Babis did. They denied the right of history to declare itself complete, symbolically
foresaking the devil in the process. They denied the right of man-made law to define justice.

They denied the right of power to speak in God’s name.

Yet hidden history, the Hand of God, is not defeated once and for all. It adapts. It
absorbs. It waits—and then it bursts forth like a broken dam as it did once more beginning

monetization dressed as mourning; making of Divine Revelation into its containment as
institution. Under the sign of survival, the Bayan was declared provisional by Black Babism
