## Abstract

Chapter 8 begins with a basic introduction to Aristotle’s life and work. After a brief outline of the Politics, an account is given of Aristotle’s theory of the nature of human beings and their intimate connection to social and civic life. He claims that since humans are by nature political animals, the state should not be regarded as something artificial but rather as a product of nature itself. Along the same lines, Aristotle rather shockingly argues that slavery is a natural institution since there are fundamental differences in nature among human beings which separate them into categories of masters and slaves. Aristotle examines and weighs carefully the different forms of governmental organization that he is familiar with: democracy, oligarchy, and monarchy. He ultimately argues that each form of government on its own is problematic when it is employed exclusively. By contrast, the best government represents a balance, taking the best elements from each of the different forms of rule. The chapter ends with a consideration of Aristotle’s refutation of the split between nature and human law. It argues that law and regulation should not always be conceived as something unnatural that curbs our freedom and development. On the contrary, we can only develop by means of such restrictions.
