Introduction

Aristotle (384-322 BCE) was an ancient Greek philosopher who profoundly shaped Western
thought on ethics and politics. His works Nicomachean Ethics and Politics are closely linked
treatises that explore how humans should live and govern themselves. In the Ethics, Aristotle
defines the highest good (eudaimonia or human flourishing) and the virtues required to attain it,
while in the Politics he examines how communities (city-states) should be organized to foster that
good life . Aristotle regarded politics as the practical extension of ethics — a “master science” that
guides lawmaking and civic life toward the wellbeing of citizens . This report delves into Aristotle’s
core principles of governance, highlighting his views on the nature of the state, citizenship, types of
government, the role of virtue in leadership, and the delicate balance between individual good and
the common good. Throughout, we draw on Aristotle’s primary texts and esteemed scholarly
interpretations to distill his frameworks and wisdom in these areas.

The Nature of the State (Polis)

Aristotle begins Politics by asserting that the polis (city-state) is a natural and necessary institution
for humans. He famously wrote that “the city-state is a natural growth, and that man is by nature a
political animal” . By this he meant that humans are naturally drawn to live in communities and
only within a political community can they achieve a fully human life. The state arises organically
from simpler associations — first families, then villages — eventually reaching a self-sufficient
community (the polis) that exists for the sake of a good life. As Aristotle explains, while the polis
may originate “for the sake of living” (for mere life and security), it “exists for the sake of living
well” . In other words, beyond mere survival, the state’s purpose is to enable citizens to achieve
virtue and happiness. He even argues the polis is “prior to the individual” in nature, comparing a
person cut off from society to a limb severed from a body . This naturalistic view of the state
underscores that individuals can only flourish when embedded in a just political community
oriented toward the common good.

Practical insights: From Aristotle’s perspective, a healthy state is not an artificial contract but the
natural context for human fulfillment. The purpose of government is to cultivate conditions in
which citizens can live well (virtuously) rather than simply to provide physical security or economic
gain . Modern readers can take from this the idea that a community’s laws and institutions should
aim at the well-being and moral development of its people, not just basic order. Aristotle’s notion
that humans are “political animals” also highlights the importance of civic engagement —
participating in society is part of our nature and necessary for realizing our potential.

Aristotle’s Concept of Citizenship

Because the state is, in Aristotle’s words, “a partnership” of citizens , he gives careful thought to
who counts as a citizen and what the role of the citizen entails. Aristotle defines a citizen (polités)
not by residence or birth alone but by active participation in public life: “a person who has the right
to participate in deliberative or judicial office” in the polis . In the context of the Greek city-state,
this meant the right to take part in the assembly, serve on juries, hold public office, and otherwise
engage in governing . Thus, citizenship for Aristotle is an active status — a citizen is one who both
rules and is ruled in turn, by taking part in collective decision-making. He notably excludes women,
slaves, and manual laborers from citizenship in his ideal, reasoning (by the biases of his time) that
they lacked the necessary leisure or capacity for cultivating virtue and political judgment . While
these exclusions do not align with modern democratic values, they stem from Aristotle’s belief that
political participation requires education and virtue, which he thought some groups could not attain
in his society.

Aristotle draws a famous distinction between the good citizen and the good man (virtuous person).
The two are not always identical. A “good citizen” is defined relative to the constitution (regime) of

a city: a good citizen upholds and supports the laws and values of that city . Because constitutions
differ, the qualities of a good citizen can vary — what makes a good citizen in a democracy (loyalty
to the principle of majority rule) is different from what makes a good citizen in an oligarchy . By
contrast, a “good man” in Aristotle’s sense means a person of moral excellence (virtue) simpliciter,
pursuing the human telos of virtue and happiness . In imperfect regimes, these two standards
diverge: one might be a good citizen yet not a morally good person (for example, a “good” citizen
of a tyranny might do things a virtuous person would not) . Only in the best regime — one oriented
toward virtue — are the good citizen and the good man the same . Therefore, Aristotle says the ideal
citizen in a perfect state is one “who is capable of and intentionally chooses being ruled and ruling
with a view to the life in accordance with virtue” . In practical terms, Aristotle stresses that a stable
polity must educate its citizens in the virtues that support its constitutional order . Good citizens
share a commitment to the common principles of the regime, and this civic virtue underpins
political stability.

Practical insights: Aristotle’s idea of citizenship highlights the value of active civic participation.
Citizenship is not just a legal status but a role involving duties — deliberation, judgment, and office-
holding — in service of the community. Today’s democracies echo this in expecting citizens to vote,
serve on juries, engage in public discourse, etc. Aristotle also teaches that for a society to thrive,
citizens need a degree of civic virtue and education: they should internalize the values of the
political community and be willing to put the common good above narrow self-interest. This has
practical resonance in modern calls for civic education and public virtue. Finally, the distinction
between good person and good citizen suggests that simply following laws isn’t sufficient — a
society should strive to make its laws and institutions align with true virtue so that doing one’s civic
duty also means doing what is ethically right. In an ideal state, personal morals and civic
responsibilities reinforce each other.

In the Nicomachean Ethics, Aristotle argues that a truly happy life for an individual is one of virtue,
and he extends this to the community: the highest good for a society is a virtuous life for its citizens
. Therefore, a ruler’s duty is profoundly moral — politics is “in order to become [citizens] good” . He
writes, “the politician and lawgiver is wholly occupied with the city-state, and the constitution is a
certain way of organizing those who inhabit the city-state” , indicating that a good constitution is
one designed to inculcate virtue and right habits. Aristotle even cautions that political power should
not be given to the young, because governing requires seasoned judgment and self-control that
come only with moral maturity . This underscores his belief that leadership is an ethical
responsibility requiring experience and virtue rather than mere birthright or popularity. In sum,
Aristotle sees no sharp separation between politics and ethics — the best leaders are moral exemplars
who use wise laws and policies to make their citizens better.

Practical insights: Aristotle’s insistence on virtue in leadership translates into the timeless principle
that character matters in governance. For a community to thrive, those entrusted with power must
possess integrity, justice, prudence, and selflessness — traits as crucial for leaders today as in ancient
times. We see this reflected in modern expectations that public officials serve the public interest, not
their own enrichment. Aristotle would advise evaluating political systems by how well they
promote virtue: for instance, do our laws encourage honesty, responsibility, and solidarity? Do our
leaders exhibit practical wisdom and moral fiber? Moreover, his thought suggests that civic
institutions (education, legal frameworks) should be designed to support the moral development of
citizens and leaders alike. Another pragmatic lesson is Aristotle’s advocacy of moderation and

power-sharing to avoid tyranny: even an absolute ruler should heed advisors and law; an elite ruling
class should temper its rule with consideration for the masses; and a democratic majority should
respect minority rights and law. These ideas prefigure constitutional checks and balances and the
importance of a balanced civil society. Ultimately, Aristotle teaches that a polis that forgets virtue
undermines its own foundation, whereas one that nurtures virtue can achieve stability and true

prosperity.

Balancing Individual Good and the Common Good

A recurring theme in Aristotle’s work is the relationship between the good of the individual person
and the good of the community. Rather than seeing these as opposed, Aristotle argues they are
deeply interconnected — and that the common good of the city is “greater and more divine” than the
good of a single person . In Nicomachean Ethics I.2, he reasons that while it is worthwhile to secure
the happiness of an individual, “to secure the good of a nation or a state is a nobler and more divine
achievement” . This is because the state’s good encompasses the well-being of many individuals
and extends over generations. Importantly, Aristotle does not mean that the community’s good
tramples the individual’s; rather, a rightly ordered common good fulfills each individual’s highest
aspirations. In an ideal polity aimed at virtue, what is good for the state is good for its citizens —
laws and institutions guide each person toward living well. The individual and the state reach their
telos (end purpose) together.

Aristotle’s view of humans as political animals underscores that one cannot achieve the fullness of
virtue in isolation. Friendship, justice, and other virtues are exercised in society. He notes that a
person who is completely self-sufficient and has no need of others (if such a person could exist)
would be “either a beast or a god” — not an ordinary human . For normal people, living in a
community is both natural and necessary for the good life. Thus, Aristotle balances individual good
and common good by effectively subsuming individual fulfillment within a just political order. The
laws of a good state aim to enable each person to act virtuously; in doing so, they secure the
collective happiness of the city. However, Aristotle is careful to define the “common good” in terms
of the free citizens (excluding at his time slaves, etc.) . This indicates a limitation by modern
standards — our concept of the common good would include everyone in society — but his
fundamental point stands: a polity should be organized such that each citizen’s well-being and
moral development is part of the community’s overarching good.

Practical insights: Aristotle’s alignment of individual and collective good offers wisdom for
contemporary society: policies and leaders should not pit personal liberties against societal welfare
as zero-sum, but strive to create conditions where advancing the common good elevates individual
lives. For example, good education and just laws help each person flourish, and as individuals
flourish in character and productivity, they contribute back to a stronger community — a virtuous
cycle. It’s a reminder that community values and institutions deeply affect personal outcomes.
Furthermore, Aristotle implies that citizens have a responsibility to consider the good of the whole
in their actions (the essence of civic virtue), knowing that their own highest good is tied to the well-

being of their community. His philosophy encourages us to seek a balance between individual rights
and social responsibilities, aiming for a harmony where public interest and private virtue reinforce
each other. In modern debates — from public health to environmental policy — this Aristotelian
principle would urge finding solutions that benefit everyone collectively and enable each person to
live a good life, rather than framing issues as my interest versus yours. In essence, Aristotle’s
politics teaches that we live best when we live well together.

Conclusion

Aristotle’s political thought, as expounded in Politics and Nicomachean Ethics, presents a rich
framework in which the state and the individual are partners in the pursuit of the good life. He
offers a vision of the state as a natural community oriented toward virtue, where citizenship is an
active calling and constitutions are judged by how well they serve the common good. His
classification of governments — from monarchies to democracies — along with their virtues and
vices, provides a diagnostic tool still used today to think about political regimes and their
tendencies. Perhaps most enduring is Aristotle’s insistence that character and virtue are the bedrock
of good governance: just laws, wise leadership, and engaged citizens mutually reinforce each other
in a flourishing polity. While aspects of his context (such as excluding many from citizenship) are
outdated, the core wisdom in Aristotle’s politics — prioritizing the common good, fostering civic
virtue, and moderating power — remains profoundly relevant. Modern republics and constitutional
democracies, in prioritizing rule of law, checks and balances, education, and civic responsibility,
echo many Aristotelian insights. In sum, Aristotle teaches that politics is fundamentally moral: the
aim of the state is not mere life but “living well,” and achieving this requires prudent structuring of
institutions and the cultivation of virtue in leaders and citizens alike . Such lessons continue to
inspire contemporary thought on how we can build communities that enable each person — and the
whole — to thrive.
