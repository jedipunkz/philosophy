PREFACE TO FIRST EDITION.

In 1890 a translation of Father Hettinger's Apology by
Father Bowden of the Oratory appeared under the title of
Natural Religion. The great reputation of the writer drew
attention to the work. Many criticisms appeared and of espe-
cial interest was an article on " Reason Alone " — "A Reply
to Father Sebastian Bowden," in the Fortnightly Review, Nov.
1890, by W. H. Mai lock. The importance of the problem was
brought clearly to mind. Convinced that the existence of God
was a certainty — how present this truth to the mind of the
present day? This volume is the fruit of the thought and study.

The success attending the publication of the work on the
Human Soul constrained the writer to adopt the comparative
method with this treatise also. The subject is heavy and
abstruse in parts. An effort has been made to render the
reading as easy as possible. Hence the illustrations, and
references to modern literature, and relegation of doubts and
controversies to the notes at the foot of the page.

The line of thought worked out in this volume is a depart-
ure from that followed in many treatises on the subject. The
writer takes the idea of God as a fact of consciousness. The
question is not how the idea came to the individual mind.
But are we justified in holding the idea, and what is its con-
tent? Hence an investigation into the grounds of the idea.
Viewed in this aspect, it becomes a study in Psychology.
Now, as the idea of God is not an individual but a universal
fact, the course of investigation is of more than a personal
value. It is a study of the human mind.

Some particular questions, e. g., capability of the mind to
conceive the Infinite, the problem of Personality are not dis-

PREFACE TO SECOND EDITION.

In issuing the second edition of the present work I
take advantage of the opportunity to state more explic-
itly my line of reasoning. Let us suppose that I am
seated in my library on a winter evening surrounded by
a few friends. Some may differ from me on religious
belief; at any rate the truth of God's existence is
called into question. As a Catholic priest I am asked
and expected to express the result of my reflections on
the problem. It will not be enough to say that I was
taught in earliest childhood by a revered mother to say
my prayers, to read carefully selected stories from the
Bible, to learn the beautiful and profound lessons in
the Catechism. This may be interesting and true and
yet may carry no weight whatsoever to the minds of my
hearers as to the objective verification of the belief.
So I take a different starting point. I begin by stat-
ing that I have an idea of God. This I call a psycho-
logical fact. To show that this idea is not my own
peculiar property, I point out that it is a primary
conviction and possession of mankind as proved from
the records of history, especially from the department
known as the history of religion. The belief in God
or the idea of God is therefore a fact of human con-
sciousness not only universal in space, i. e., as possessed
by every nation now inhabiting the earth, but univer-
sal in time also as is proved by the written annals and
monuments of mankind, even by language itself. For
language is the true index of thought and feeling, and

of the words employed in known languages to express

which is common to all men. The other is a more elab-

not learned by teaching; it arises spontaneously from
our rational nature ; the rational man has it by himself ;
it is the knowledge which nature suggests. I point in
the explanation of this to the teaching in Catholic
schools that the human soul is made to the image and
likeness of God; that it is made to know God; that
from the indeliberate exercise of its own faculties by
a kind of natural movement it comes to the knowledge
of God : " Insita et cum natura conserta notitia." This
knowledge is, therefore, vague and confused. Reve-
lation and instruction purifies and makes it more com-
plete. I furthermore maintain that this spontaneous
knowledge of God is distinguished by two important
characteristics: (a) It arises from rational nature by
the use of faculties connatural to all. Hence it is not
an intuition, nor is it the result of a special faculty.
On the contrary, it can be compared to a spontaneous
inference, (b) It is universal with human nature.

The sources of this knowledge are common to all
who have the free use of reason. These sources are
the world of man and of nature.

The consideration of man's being opens up the
spheres of mental and of ethical life. The analysis of
the concept, of the judgment, of the first principles, of
reasoning itself leads the mind to the conviction in the
existence of objective truth. We recognize that the
mind is powerless to change its conceptions at will ; it
finds that the idea is such or such and takes it as it is
found. We are forced to admit the objective reality
of external and necessary truth. This truth pervades
the universe in some wonderful manner. The mind is
under its silent potent sway. Thus the world of ex-
ternal reality enters into the world of thought. The
mind may turn or twist the mass to its own special
ends or purposes. Nevertheless the content is ob-
jectively real, the content comes from without; the
peculiar form and shape only can be ascribed to men-
tal activity. The first principles of Logic and of
Metaphysics are closely akin, and Scientists assure us
that the solar system is ruled by truths and principles
which make up the abstract science of Geometry and
appeal for confirmation to the experimental science of
Astronomy. Thus the mind does not make, it finds
truth. In ordinary and scientific language we speak of
new discoveries, of the bounds of knowledge enlarged
from day to day. The ingenuity of man makes instru-
ments to reach out and apply the truth ; but the truth
itself is beyond his control. In its presence he stands
powerless. Its sway is absolute and universal. It
knows no limitations of time, of space, or of any created
intelligence. This absolute dominion of truth can only
be explained in God. Its basis is not the human mind

The testimony of our moral nature is more obvious
and convincing. In stronger and clearer voice it calls
us to look beyond ourselves to one who prescribes the
fundamental laws and principles of human conduct and
alone is able to satisfy the desires of the soul. The
dictates of conscience, the abiding sense of responsi-

intelligent and great, but just and good.

Looking out into external nature I find grounds for
the validity of my idea of God. The great fact im-
pressed upon the mind by the world without is that all
things change. Spring follows winter and summer fol-
lows spring in orderly succession. Night gives place
to day and day to night in unvarying round. The seed
is planted in the ground, rises in a stalk, flowers and
produces seed again. We change from youth to man-
hood to old age. The disposition to change is inherent
in everything and the fact obtains with the necessity of
the law. In this fact we can distinguish: the thing
which changes, the change itself, the cause of the
change and the marvelous harmony in the changes.
The first leads to a consideration of the contingency
of things; the second to movement; the third to the
analysis of cause; the last to the phenomena of order
and contrivance. In pursuing our analysis on these
lines we draw the facts from the discoveries of science.
The various departments of physical science present
