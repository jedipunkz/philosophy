INTRODUCTORY NOTE

The addresses and messages that follow were prepared for the
Spinoza Tercentenary celebration held by the Spinoza Institute
of America at the College of the City of New York on Novem-
ber 23, 1932, under the chairmanship of President Frederick B.
Robinson, president of the College of the City of New York. The
growing consciousness of the commanding personality of Spinoza
and the increasing recognition of the significance of his philosophy
today, found further confirmation in the fact that over twelve
hundred people attended that celebration.

These papers are not offered as an integrated exposition of
Spinoza’s philosophy. The primary purpose is that of paying
tribute to a transcendent genius, who, while insisting on the right-
ful claims of our intuitive vision, nevertheless, championed the
cause of Reason and the power and dignity of the human in-
tellect.

In response to many requests from those attending the celeb-
ration, the addresses, though designed for a specific occasion, are
now offered in this form in the hope that the interested public
will find this booklet an introduction to his thought and a
stimulus for a more adequate appreciation of his philosophy.

We have appended an illuminating essay on Spinoza and
Judaism by Harry Waton as that paper emphasizes the Jewish
nature and orientation of Spinoza’s thought and shows the identity
of Spinoza’s system with the spirit of explicit Judaism.

The address on the founding of the Spinoza Institute, origin-
ally delivered by Harry Waton on December 18, 1927, is included
in this collection. We believe that the plea “to make the idea of
the philosophy of Spinoza the ruling idea of the life of mankind”
is a challenge to those who have not lost their reason in a world
of chaos and disillusion.

WHY HONOR SPINOZA ?

Dr. I. H. Murask1n

President, Spinoza Institute of America

The Spinoza Institute of America, organized for the purpose
of fostering an interest in and appreciation of the life and phil-
osophy of Spinoza, has cheerfully accepted the obligation and
privilege of arranging, with the kind cooperation of our dis-
tinguished guests, this tercentennial celebration, to do honor to the
memory of that great thinker, whose name this Institute has
proudly adopted—Baruch Spinoza.

That we have grown nearer to a just appreciation of the
greatness of Spinoza is shown by this splendid gathering tonight.
Two hundred years ago, Spinoza was the object of contempt; a
century ago he was regarded generally with indifference, for his
bicentenary was not celebrated; fifty years ago, tardy recognition
came; today the cultured world honors him—more it appears to
me, out of regard for his personality than for any just appreciation
of his philosophy. Be that as it may, progress begins first at the
periphery and in time moves toward the center. We are drawn
first to the man, later to his meditations.

Sitting here for these exercises, the thought must have occurred
to you, “Why have we selected Spinoza from out of the great
philosophical firmament as worthy of this honor?” No doubt his
life and personality fascinate us. The qualities which we admire
in him—his serenity, his exalted religious feeling, his profound
intuition into the human soul are, indeed, qualities that strike a
responsive chord in our very essence.

If we imagine, however, that our selection of Spinoza was an
act of free choice—we entertain a naive illusion. No man accepts
another’s philosophy of life unless the borrower potentially and
implicitly already thinks and feels as the philosopher does. All
a philosopher can do is to bring to fullest development and per-
fection what is vague, crude, imperfect and embryonic in our-
selves. Of course, we are conscious of our likes and dislikes, of

7

our feelings and our thoughts, but as Spinoza observed, we are
not conscious of the cause of such thought and feelings. If we
are drawn to Spinoza, it is because our nature responds to his
philosophy and our mind finds completion in his intuitive and
rational insight. This, however, presupposes that we ourselves
have grown to that degree of maturity to be able to recognize the
power of reason and feel the desire to live by its light. Whether,
in point of fact, we do have that innate capacity and that desire
depend not upon ourselves but upon a power beyond us.

Spinoza tells us that the more reality a thing has the more
attributes belong to it. By the same token, the depth and pro-
fundity of Spinoza’s philosophy is to be judged by the multiform
characteristics that it possesses. A scientific mind, like Einstein
finds in Spinoza, a Spinozistic conception of God and makes as
his own the postulate of determinism. A legal mind like that of
Sir Frederick Pollock, finds in the logical structure of Spinoza,
the configuration of a “habit of mind,” a quickened perception
of the relation of premise and consequence. An intuitive mind
rejoices in Spinoza’s clear seeing of the absolute, infinite and
eternal.

You will readily note that the rounded system of Spinoza is
capable of satisfying our entire nature. We respond only to the
extent of our own apperception. Spinoza long ago recognized this
truth when he remarked that the knowledge we have of a thing
reflects our own nature rather than the nature of a thing.

Our limitation should not conceal the fact that philosophy in
the hands of Spinoza, as well as in the hands of rational human
beings, is comprehensive. Rational thought envisages the ultimate
derived by our intuition, develops the implications of our vision
to explicitness and submits the basic principles to a rigorous
analysis of reason. An enlightened philosophy like that of Spinoza,
does not forget to apply the light which it so painfully and
laboriously gathered to illumine our goal toward the attainment
of a rational pro-social mode of life.

A proper evaluation of Spinoza’s thought would be woefully
inadequate and incomplete if I omitted to emphasize the concrete-
ness of his philosophic outlook. Spinoza postulates as a dictate
of reason that every one should endeavor to preserve his own
being. To preserve one’s own being is a virtue or power. Power,
however, comes from a knowledge of one’s nature—physical,

8

moral, intellectual. The highest power of self-preservation requires
a most adequate idea of the totality of existence, i. e. the idea of
God. This knowledge give us supreme, eternal happiness. In
order to attain it, individually and socially, Spinoza proposes a
method of revealing the true rational nature of man. This method
is designed to free the mind from the vast encrustations of pre-
judices, fictions, illusions and passions. Where in the world
literature other than in Spinoza’s Ethics will you find a more
concrete and explicit account of the emotions and the means for
our liberation from their bondage?

If I were asked to characterize the temper of our age, I could
do no better than to describe it as an age of irrationalism. Our
thinkers, with rare exceptions, deny that our intellect is competent
and trustworthy to know the infinite realities of existence. How
can they pass judgment on reason except by reason itself? It is
one thing to say that our present knowledge is limited, but it is
quite another to deny to the human intellect, the power to know
the ultimate nature of reality. Scientists, who scorn metaphysics,
dodge this problem by asserting that the individual mind in its
selective power has fitted nature into a pattern of law. They do
not tell us how the individual mind got that power. Are the
laws of nature fictions of the mind or are they the relations of
the forms of existence? If fictions, then they are more potent
than the realities themselves. Since, as Spinoza demonstrated, the
order and connection of ideas in our mind agree with the order
and connection of things in the external world, is it not more
rational to say that the adequate perceptions of our mind are
in fact true representations of reality? Spinoza drew the
correct conclusion when he boldly affirmed the power of the mind
to know the ultimate nature of reality. “For,” says Spinoza, “it
pertains to the essence of the human mind to have an adequate
knowledge of the eternal and infinite essence of God.” To be
sure, there is a mystery in existence, but that mystery is life itself.
This, as Croce has shown, is an eternal problem for thought. But
this problem would not even be a problem if thought did not

eternally solve it.

The concreteness of Spinoza’s thought and the confidence
placed by Spinoza in the potency of human reason must be
stressed emphatically as an antidote to the irrationalism, ob-

9

scurantism and all forms of esoteric supernaturalism that are now
current.

Can the study of Spinoza’s philosophy stem the tide of
irrationalism ?

Contemplating the diseased state of present society, the answer
seems to be that our efforts to arrest the disintegration of society,
of which irrationalism is its reflex, are likely to be ineffective and
feeble. The creeping intellectual stupor, the economic and material
dislocation, the distempered nerves that have infected our spiritual
outlook with irrationalism and paralyzed it with a presentiment
of decline—these symptoms tend rather to disparage faith in
human reason, and even threaten to engulf Spinoza’s philosophy
in the whirlpool of irrationalism. Sentimental and perturbed
souls may insulate themselves in mystic cults and imagine that
the cash nexus between man and man thereby ceases to be a fact.

Mankind now faces a dilemma. It cannot rise intellectually
and morally save as it overcomes its prejudices and anti-social
feelings and it cannot cast off its ingrained individualism—save
as it rises to the perception of a rational ordering of existence.
The consciousness of this dilemma adds poignancy to irrationalism.
But like all perplexities, this difficulty can be overcome only in
time, for time is the great solvent of prejudices and illusions.
Only in time does our true rational nature assert itself.

Ernest Renan, in his beautiful oration at the unveiling of the
Spinoza monument at the Hague on February 21, 1877, said:
“Humanity indeed advances on the way of progress by prodigiously
unequal steps. The rude and violent Esau is out of patience with
the slow pace of Jacob’s flock. Let us give time to all. We may
not indeed permit simplicity and ignorance to hinder the free
movement of the intellect, but let us not either interfere with the
slow evolution of less active intelligences.”

To have foreseen the destiny of the human race in the eventual
realization of the life of Reason, is to the great credit of Spinoza
and to the everlasting glory of Judaism which has enshrined this
ideal in the consciousness of men.

Even if we should fail in our generation to realize the ethics
of Spinoza, there is consolation to be found in having striven to
attain that ideal. Consider the benefits gained: There comes to
us that joy in passing to a higher perfection, and while our
suffering is not eliminated, our sorrows lose their poignancy as their

10

mental darkness disappears, our relations to our fellow-men become
more tolerant as we realize the difficulties in the way of salvation;
we cast off our illusions and discover that the world no longer
holds any terror for us; our wisdom indeed becomes the wisdom

of life and not a meditation upon death. For what more can
we hope?

SPINOZA, APOSTLE OF FREEDOM

Pror. Isaac Husik

Professor of Philosophy, University of Pennsylvania

Three hundred years ago tomorrow Baruch Spinoza was born
of Jewish parents in the city of Amsterdam. At the age of 24 he
was excommunicated by the Jewish Congregation of his native
city for giving expression to heretical opinions. At the age of 44
years, 2 months and 27 days he died of consumption in the city of
The Hague, mourned by a handful of intimate friends. His bio-
grapher, John Colerus, Minister of the Lutheran Church at The
Hague, writing in 1705, some 28 years after Spinoza’s death, begins
as follows: “Spinoza, that philosopher whose name makes so
great noise in the world, was originally a Jew.” The noise he
made in the world at that time gave him notoriety rather than
fame. He was disowned not merely by his own people, but by
the world as well. The theologians and philosophers of the day,
in Holland, France, Germany, and England, vied with each other
in condemning and rejecting his teachings in terms not exactly
polite. Even those who had a lurking sympathy for him dared
not avow it in public, so that the oldest life of Spinoza, written
by one who knew him, in the year of his death or the year fol-
lowing, has remained anonymous and was not published until
1719. “It is surprising,” he says, “that one should have to conceal
himself in order to write their life (i. e. the life of great men), as
if he were about to commit a crime; especially is this so if these
great men have made themselves famous by views that are unusual
and unknown to common souls.”

And as a matter of fact Spinoza was all but completely
neglected for about a century after his death. To be sure, he was
not forgotten, but he was misinterpreted or misunderstood and
hence was condemned as an atheist or as a materialist. And it was
not until the days of Jacobi and Lessing and Goethe in Germany
and of Coleridge in England—men who (Jacobi excepted) were
not professional philosophers, but who achieved fame and influence

12

in the world of literature—that Spinoza began to receive his due,
and his influence has been growing to this day.

Im view of this peculiar history one may ask, what sort of man
was this Spinoza whom everybody shunned and condemned?
Was he a despot who tyrannized over his subjects? Was he an
industrial magnate who fed on the labor and the sweat of his
poor workmen? Was he a man of undue intellectual ambition
who. delighted in belittling his rivals to put himself forward as
alone worthy of a hearing? Was he arrogant in his attitude to
his fellows, or dishonest in his dealings with them?

No, he was quite the contrary of all this. He was poor and
did not bewail his poverty; he was satisfied with little. Above all
he did not want to be beholden to any one for his support and
declined the gifts of his friends and admirers. He worked for
his living and used his leisure for meditation. He valued his time
and his freedom so much that he declined an offer of a Professor-
ship in the University of Heidelberg for fear it would curtail the
one and encroach upon the other. In his dealings with his fellows,
the simple minded as well as the learned, he was friendly, affable
and modest. He lived the most simple kind of a life and took no
active part in politics. Even his enemies could not find anything
in his conduct to his discredit. His biographer Colerus, who had
nothing but abhorrence for Spinoza’s doctrines, was bound to
testify to his exemplary character.

“The particulars of his sickness and death,” Colerus writes,
“have been variously reported, and have occasioned several con-
testations. "Tis said, Ist, That during his sickness he took the
necessary precautions to avoid being visited by those whose sight
would have been troublesome to him. 2dly, that he spoke once
and even several times these words, O God, have mercy upon me,
miserable sinner. 3dly, that they heard him often sigh, when he
pronounced the name of God. Which gave occasion to those who
were present to ask him whether he believed, at last, the existence
of a God, whose judgment he had great reason to fear after his
death? And that he answered them that he had dropt that word
out of custom. ’Tis said 4thly, that he kept by him some juice
of mandrake ready at hand which he made use of, when he per-
ceived he was a dying; that he drew the curtains of his bed
afterwards, and then lost his senses, fell into a profound sleep
and departed this life in that manner. 5thly, that he had given

13

express orders to let nobody come into his room when he should
be near his end. And likewise, that finding he was dying, he
called for his Landlady and desired her to suffer no Minister to
come to him; because he was willing to die peacefully and with-
out disputing, etc.

“I have carefully enquired into the truth of all those things
and asked several times his Landlord and his Landlady, who are
alive still, what they knew of it: But they answered me at all
times, that they knew nothing of it, and were persuaded that all
those circumstances were mere lies.” And he proceeds to deny
all the allegations, some of which are indeed harmless enough.
“Nobody heard the words which it is said he spoke, O God, have
mercy upon me, miserable sinner: Nor is it likely that they should
come out of his mouth, seeing he did not think he was so near
his death. . . . His Landlady and the people of the house knew
nothing of his ordering to send away the Ministers that should
come to see him, or of his invocating the Name of God during
his sickness. Nay, they believe the contrary, because ever since
he began to be in a languishing condition, he always expresst, in
all his sufferings, a truly Stoical constancy; even so as to reprove
others, when they happened to complain, and to shew in their
sickness little courage or too great a sensibility.”

But Spinoza, despite that he was a man of exemplary virtue
and charity, committed the one sin against the holy Ghost, which
made all else of no account. He believed in freedom and acted
on his belief. He was a nonconformist in thought. Gifted with
an original mind, he used it according to its own laws and fol-
lowed its lead to its ultimate consequences, which were different
from the beliefs and opinions of his contemporaries.

And yet he was no anarchist. He believed in Government and
was loyal to the authorities of his own state. While he pleaded in
his writings for freedom to think and to express his thoughts, he
nevertheless believed in the right of the State to regulate and
control the actions of its citizens and he concludes the Introduction
to the Tractatus Theologico-Politicus as well as the Treatise itself
with the identical words:

“I have written nothing which I do not most willingly submit
to the examination and judgment of my country’s rulers, and... .
I am ready to retract anything which they shall decide to be
repugnant to the laws or prejudicial to the public good. I know

14

that I am a man and, as a man, liable to error, but against error
I have taken scrupulous care, and striven to keep in entire ac-
cordance with the laws of my country, with loyalty and with
morality.”

This was no mere gesture on his part, for he was no coward.
He was ready to face a raging mob who had lynched a friend of
his, John de Witt, and to speak his mind to them at the risk of
meeting with a similar fate; and if he did not do so, it was be-
cause his Landlord had locked the door and he could not get out.

What were the ideas of Spinoza which were regarded as so
dangerous?

They concern God, man and the State. God to Spinoza is not
a person residing in the heavens and from that height ruling the
world of nature and of man, for an ultimate purpose that He
alone knows. God can be loved, but he can not love in return. |
God and the whole order of nature are the same. Man is a part
of this order, and the better he understands it the freer he is.
The passions of man, the cause of so much unhappiness and
misery, become purified and an aid to happiness by reason and
understanding. To understand is to forgive, nay to understand
is to realize that forgiveness is not necessary. Man makes the
mistake of judging the world as a whole by reference to his
welfare. He assumes that God made the world for his benefit
and hence looks for purposes in nature with relation to his desires.
Good and evil are to him those things which give him pleasure
and pain. Hence he is puzzled by the evil in the world and speaks
of the problem of evil for which he invents fanciful explanations.
The true answer is to deny the problem. There are no purposes
in the world as a whole, hence nothing in the world is either good
or evil in the absolute sense. Man is but a small part of the
universe and the universe was not made for him. Hence there is
no problem of evil.

To be sure, it is the part of the wise man to make his life as
pleasurable as possible and to avoid pain. But this end is not
accomplished by indulging the passions, but by rationalizing them.
Man’s chief good is his understanding, and to understand that
certain things are inevitable is to take away their sting. Also the
cultivation of the understanding, and seeing things under the
aspect of eternity, as Spinoza expresses it, leads to intellectual love

of God.
1

Things are inevitable; physical phenomena as well as the
order of thoughts in the mind are determined by causes going
way back to infinity. In this sense there is no freedom either to
think or to act. But this is not the kind of freedom that Spinoza
is talking about. God, he says, is free because he acts from the
necessity of his own nature, not determined from without. Man
is free if he acts from the necessity of his nature, which is his
understanding.

The metaphysics of Spinoza is too difficult to understand or
to try to explain in a few minutes, but fortunately it is not
necessary to enter upon such abstract and complicated details to
appreciate something of Spinoza’s spirit. He appealed, as we have
seen, to poets and literary men before the philosophers began to
cultivate him. His life is something we can all understand and
admire, and Spinoza, himself, was not a mere theorist. He had
lost his anchorage when he realized that the things most men
strive for are not worth while. Hence he was looking for some-
thing stable, permanent and eternal with which he could identify
himself. If he could find this, he was safe and would be saved in
the religious sense. He found this eternity in God as the sum
of all reality, of which man is a part in so far as the eternal part
of him is concerned, namely, his understanding.

He was anxious, however, to guide others too to make the
same discovery, realizing that if they did, the world would cease
to be a vale of tears and we should all enjoy peace and happiness.

The popular conception of religion and the Bible, and the
theologians’ and statesmen’s conception as well, were a stumbling
block in his path. Hence he endeavored to enlighten them. The
Bible was looked upon in his day as literally the word of God in
every part, every expression, every word and letter. Hence all
science and philosophy had to be reconciled with the Bible if
they were to receive any cultivation by religious men. The result
was that the interpretation of the Bible, far from giving an ex-
planation of its meaning, became a method of mental acrobatics,
trying to find therein meanings which were never dreamed of.
Spinoza was one of the first to contend that the Bible must be
explained from itself, and that one must not assume in advance that
it is perfect in all respects, but must examine it first without
prejudice and thus arrive at conclusions.| This Spinoza proceeded
to do and came to conclusions that were novel in his day. These

16

were that the Bible was never intended as a textbook of a
theoretical order, teaching those matters which science discovers
by its own methods. Hence we must not assume that the writers
of the biblical books were, as scientists, superior to their con-
temporaries. Scientific errors in the Bible do not detract from its
value in teaching virtue, obedience, and charity. This stumbling
block gone, thought would be free. And Spinoza further proceeds
to analyze the state and law and government. Here he admits
theoretically that stable social life is not possible unless every mem-
ber of the State gives up some of his individual likes and dislikes
and submits himself to the governing power, whose right extends
as far as its power. But he points out at the same time that if
the sovereign desires to retain his power and maintain the State in
equilibrium he can do nothing better than grant freedom of
thought and speech to all the citizens.

Spinoza sacrificed all that most people hold essential to happi-
ness for the sake of his peace of mind and for the sake of freedom
to think his thoughts and live his life as he thought it should be
lived. Some of us assume that he suffered through the sacrifice
and we sympathize and revere his memory because we all regard
sacrifice as a noble virtue. I do not think this is a true reading
of Spinoza’s character. This would reduce him to the level of
the ordinary man who is on occasion capable of sacrificing some-
thing he holds dear for a higher cause. The importance of Spinoza
lies in the fact that he was a pathfinder. He tried to show in
his life and in his writings that he was not living a life of sacrifice,
that the things he chose to live without were not worthwhile and
that he had reached an insight and lived on a level where he was
happy and cheerful precisely because he had given up those things
most of us strive for. He caught a glimpse of the eternal and
there found peace.

LIVING IN ACCORDANCE WITH THE PHILOSOPHY
OF SPINOZA

Dr. GasrieL R. Mason
Principal, Abraham Lincoln High School

In reading the life of Spinoza, and in studying his meta-
physics, the question sometimes arises whether this wonderfully
beautiful and this majestically serene philosophy can be made the
practical guide for our lives in these modern times. This bold
query I wish to answer with an equally bold and categorical “yes.”

Of course, it will be no easy matter to influence our thought
and actions of 1932 by the teachings of our blessed sage born in
1632; but then, as Spinoza well says, all things excellent are as
difficult as they are rare.

To make the philosophic principles of Spinoza part and
parcel of ourselves, it will first be necessary to agree with him
as to the goal of human existence. Like many hedonists, Spinoza
also taught that in this life we should seek happiness; but this
is not to be obtained by courting riches, fame, position, power
or sensual pleasures. These are will-o’-the-wisps. When we
pursue these phantoms, they always escape us and leave us dis-
appointed, discouraged and disillusioned. Rather let us substitute
for these a life directed by the authority of reason. Through
reason we are enabled to see that Substance (Spinoza’s name for
the all-pervasive nature or the all-absorbing God) is the only
permanent value in a world of accidental, ephemeral and changing
things and thoughts. Therefore let us intellectually love God
and thereby attain peace of mind, tranquility of soul and joy of
heart.

In addition to this fundamental dictum, we must, if we
are to live in accordance with Spinoza’s philosophy, declare our-
selves agnostics. We must not anthropomorphize our God. We
must not limit Him or qualify Him with human traits of a
magnified character. Such a view, in which we humbly and
sincerely confess our ignorance, helps us, stoic-like, to get rid

18

of superstitions and fears, and permits us to taste of happiness
supreme, eternal and unending.

We must also subscribe to the dictum of determinism. When
events in this world happen, they occur for good and sufficient
reasons, following the scientific law of cause and effect. Every
cause must have an effect; every effect must have a cause. Events
are, therefore, inevitable. Constituted as the world was, things

THE HISTORIC SIGNIFICANCE OF SPINOZA’S
PHILOSOPHY

By Harry Waton
Author of The Philosophy of Spinoza

Spinoza contemplated existence in the aspect of eternity, and
he dwelt in eternity. In eternity there is neither time nor space.
With relation to eternity, the time when and the place where
Spinoza was born are of no consequence; of consequence is the
relation of Spinoza himself to eternity. To honor Spinoza, his
relation to eternity must be revealed.

Eternity is not a state of rest and death, but is absolute life
and infinite activity; it is the conscious activity of the Absolute
in accordance with an eternal and infinite order. The Absolute
goes through eternal and infinite cycles, and this constitutes the
eternal history of existence.

The Absolute is absolute thought and infinite light. To
bring out the world, the Absolute differentiates himself into life,
will and love. He projects his infinite will, which becomes infinite
extension. This infinite extension constitutes the infinite attributes
of the Absolute. The infinite will, moving from the centre of
the Absolute to the periphery of infinite extension, moves from
absolute explicitness and supreme perfection to complete implicit-
ness and imperfection—from infinite light to darkness. Then the
Absolute projects his infinite love, which Spinoza calls the In-
finite Intellect of God. The Infinite Intellect comprehends
infinite extension, integrates it, and clothes it in infinite forms.
Extension comprehended by form becomes a reality. Thus are
brought out the stars, the solar systems and all the infinite realities
of existence.

Life appears on the earth’s surface; at first in the form of
primordial cells, then life integrates the cells and brings out an
infinite series of forms of life, one higher than the other, until
man is brought into existence. Man starts as an animal, but
his predetermined destiny is to transcend the phenomenal aspect
of existence. The Infinite Intellect takes man in hand, raises

25

him ever higher, until he attains in succession to explicit reason,
self-consciousness and the intellect. The intellect is the highest
state of man. When man attains to the intellect, he perceives
that his intellect is part of the Infinite Intellect of God; he then
consciously and explicitly identifies himself with the Infinite In-
tellect and contemplates existence in the aspect of eternity and
infinitude. He then partakes of the infinite joy with which God
contemplates Himself, and this constitutes the infinite love of God.
The partaking of this infinite joy constitutes the intellectual love
of God.

Thus we see the eternal cycle of existence in all its infinite
aspects. Creation is not from the centre of the Absolute towards
the periphery of extension, but is from the periphery of extension
to the centre of the Absolute; it is from implicitness, darkness and
imperfection towards explicitness, light and perfection. While
the will of the Absolute moves from infinite light and supreme
perfection. to darkness and imperfection, the love of the Absolute
moves from darkness and imperfection to infinite light and
supreme perfection. Viewing existence in the aspect of this eter-
nal cycle of creation, we see how out of chaos and darkness
emerged creation; we see the dawn of light and the appearance
of life; we see the infinite forms of life following one another,
rising ever higher towards light and consciousness, until man ap-
pears and leads all existence towards infinite light and supreme
perfection. The ladder of evolution stands on the periphery of
extension, but its top reaches the centre of the Absolute. The
messengers of the love of the Absolute ascend by this ladder,
reach God, and bring down the divine light and knowledge.
From time to time a Prometheus, a Moses, a Buddha, a Jesus,
came to the fore, ascended to the Absolute, and brought down
to mankind divine light and knowledge. These immortals saw
God face to face, but they were not privileged to reveal to man-
kind what they saw in a manner clear and explicit. The reason
for this was that mankind was yet in a state of implicitness and
semi-unconsciousness. Hence, these immortal messengers of light
and knowledge were compelled to convey their messages to
mankind in a language of vague symbols, which mankind never
understood adequately.

Spinoza was the first man who, like Moses, saw God face to
face, and who was privileged to give to mankind in a language

26

clear and explicit the divine light and knowledge which he brought
down from the Absolute. The reason for this was that, by the
time that Spinoza appeared, mankind had already attained to
some degree of self-consciousness and explicit reason, and were
capable of receiving the divine message in its clear light. Philo-
sophers do not write systems of philosophy, but the Infinite In-
tellect speaks to mankind through the mind of the philosophers.
Viewing Spinoza in this aspect, we can perceive and appreciate
the historic significance of his appearance in existence. Spinoza
is significant, not in the sense of ordinary history, but in the sense
of the history of eternal existence itself. In him we see the stage
of progress made by the Infinite Intellect through mankind in its
movement towards the Absolute.

The ancient Rabbis said: He who has learned from his com-
rade a chapter, a sentence or even only one word, must honor the
comrade. The teacher is the greatest benefactor to mankind.
As Emerson says: He that feeds men serveth but few; he serveth
all who dares be true. The teacher that dares impart to mankind
light and knowledge is, indeed, one that deserves most honor.
We, who for years have been the pupils of Spinoza, and who for
years have enriched our mind with the light and knowledge
radiated by the Infinite Intellect through the mind of Spinoza,
and who have all along honored Spinoza by a zealous devotion
to the study and spread of his philosophy, have availed ourselves
of the privilege to honor Spinoza in this manifest manner. We
rejoice in the privilege which we have deserved. Your presence
here, and your participation in this privilege, show that you are
worthy to be identified with Spinoza. May this occasion inspire
you to take up the study and spread of the philosophy of Spinoza,
so that this philosophy may become a “lamp unto the feet and
a light unto the path” of mankind.

Physical dialectic is one thing and metaphysical another. Na-

ultimate nature of things, though it has often proved that sup-

We know today that the key to the understanding of Spinoza

On the other hand, when one has gone to the end of the Ethics

The doctrine of Free Will is one of the most thorny problems
of philosophy. Many philosophers, not being able to see
more clearly than the great mass of mankind, are loath to abandon
this grand doctrine of Free Will which they consider to be in
line “with the dignity and importance of man,” as the high-sound-
ing phrase usually goes. By taking this view, however, they re-
move man from the universal causality permeating all beings and
things. They thus consider him, in the words of Spinoza, as being
“outside the sphere of nature,—as a kingdom within a kingdom.”

Already the simple consideration that everything that is or
takes place cannot—if we are not to assume a miracle—originate
in itself or come out of nothing, but must, on the contrary,
acknowledge a cause for its existence and its functions—already
this simple and cogent consideration does away as with one sweep,
with all pretensions to and arguments for a Free Will, for that
non-existent pet-child of man’s imagination and vanity.

But, one might say, does: not our consciousness affirm the
existence of a Free Will? Do we not feel daily, in many of our
acts, that we can act as we want to, and that we are consequently
free? Isn’t our own consciousness dependable enough?

The answer to this is that it is not dependable; that mere con-
sciousness of a certain state apart from reason constitutes no proof
for the actuality of that state. Witness our many optical illu-
sions. We have, furthermore, the consciousness attested to by all
our senses that the earth is at complete rest and we have no sensible
consciousness at all of the complexity and rapidity of motion with
which the earth, and we with her, swing about in space. To rely

Sy is

on the consciousness-argument is, therefore, to rely on something
that is unreliable. Numerous other examples might be given which
would still further illustrate this obvious fact.

It is not denied, however, that we have the consciousness of
will. But the consciousness of Free Will is not the same as Free
Will itself. The two should not be confused. If a stone, says
Spinoza, while continuing in motion, were endowed with con-
sciousness it “would believe itself to be completely free, and
would think that it continued in motion solely because of its own
wish. This is that human freedom which all boast they
possess, and which consists solely in the fact that men are con-
scious of their own desire, but are ignorant of the causes whereby
that desire has been determined. Thus an infant believes that it
desires milk freely; an angry child thinks he wishes freely for
vengeance, ... a drunken man thinks, that from the free decision
of his mind he speaks words, which afterward, when sober, would
like to have left unsaid. — — — As this misconception is innate
in all men it is not easily conquered.” *

It is claimed, especially by those who look at the practical
side of things that, to deny Free Will is to accept a fruitless,
barren, and pernicious doctrine. It will be seen, however, that far
from being so, the denial of Free Will has proven and will yet
prove to be of immense practical significance to our daily life.
Happily for us we now deny the doctrine of Free Will in many
aspects of our life without perhaps knowing that we do so. Our
educational practices, for instance, have changed for the better
in the last few years precisely because we have consciously and un-
consciously abandoned more or less the idea of Free Will.

“Free Will,” translated into educational practice, means in-
effectual preaching and moralizing, useless do’s and dont’s and
“you mustn’t do this,” and “you oughtn’t do that.” The world
has been preached to for hundreds of years from every possible
angle and with the most idealistic words without any noticeable

volves affirmation or negation.” This means that in the very idea
that we have of an object there is included whether we do or
do not afhirm it as regards its truth. Also whether we approve
or disapprove of it with regard to our personal interest. What we
know to be good for us we cannot help desiring, and the op-
posite is true of what we know to be harmful to us. Willing,
then, is included in the knowing. We might with equal right
say that knowing is included in the Willing, since it is above
all the element of pleasure or displeasure in an object that deter-
mines our deliberations and our choosing or rejecting it. As
Spinoza expressed it: men’s “decisions of the mind are nothing

62

save their desires.” Knowing and Willing, then, clearly coincide.
“Will and Intellect are one and the same thing.” (Ethics II, Prop.
49, Corollary).

Another important criticism of the denial of Free Will comes
from those who confuse determinism with fatalism. If everything
is predetermined to happen in a certain manner, they argue, then
there remains nothing for us human beings to do but to put our
hands on our laps and to allow Fate to take care of everything.
Striving, then, has no sense, thought is useless, and action be-
comes paralyzed.

The fatalistic viewpoint, however, is very crude and super-
ficial. It fails to take account of the obvious fact that with human
nature there are already given all those many forces, desires and
appetites which determine one to act in the way one usually acts,
so that everything pretty nearly remains as it is. It is predeter-
mined or determined that man, for instance, shares most of his
appetites with the animal: that he will get hungry, that he will
have to have food, that he will procreate his kind, etc. Fatalism is
thus seen to be shallow and flat, just because it really rejects true de-
terminism as it is found in the reality of nature and because it
assumes a determinism that nowhere exists.

In practical matters, as was mentioned before, our outlook
on life is as a rule deterministic. We act in accordance with the
causes and effects of things, and we think in causal relationship.
“Ratio siva causa,” says Spinoza. Cause and effect become “ground
and consequence” in rational and logical thought. Fortunately,
in many instances, as we already saw in the case of recent educa-
tional practice, we tend to disregard the doctrine of Free Will
in spite of our belief in it. Our attitude towards criminals, to
give another example, has also undergone a profound change in
recent years. This change is due mainly to our realization that,
in the greatest number of cases, criminals do not become crimin-
als out of their own free will, but that they become so due to the
determining force of environment and circumstances, and we
realize that, if these circumstances had been different, the indivi-
dual might not have become a criminal at all. In our juvenile
courts, in our reformatories for the young lawbreaker, in our more
humane prisons where attempts are made to teach their inmates
a useful trade, and to impart to them, in general, a more
social attitude towards their fellowmen; and, in that which ie

63

now called mental hygiene, we give visible and practical expres-
sion to this Spinozistic, deterministic view that human thoughts,
emotions and actions are not free, but that they “follow from the
same necessity and ability in nature as other individual things.”

It would lead us too far, however, to discuss here either the
many other advantages that the denial of Free Will bestows or
might bestow upon the individual and upon society, or the equally
great disadvantages and the physical and mental harm that the
assumption of Free Will brings in its course. Think only of the
innumerable, and often entirely useless suffering that pangs of
remorse bring to human beings because of what they believe were
“avoidable” mistakes or false steps that they have made in their
lives. Believing in the power of their Free Wills there is always
that question ceaselessly gnawing at their hearts: “Why didn’t
I act otherwise?”

Free Will is also implied as Brunner points out in our
“Morality,” which, as he uses the term, is the antithesis of ethics
and ethical conduct. By presupposing Free Will and by mis-
understanding the egotistical nature of man, our morality becomes,
as Brunner has shown, a malignant tumor in our social life into
which it brings immeasurable harm, discord, haughtiness, recipro-
cal hatred and cruelty.

Enough, we believe, has been said to show that, as Einstein
remarked, “Man is not free in his thinking, feeling, and doing,”
but is “just as causally determined as are the stars in their motions.”

Free Will is a non-entity. The inexorable laws of causality
that permeate the infinite universe, also determine all things on
this our earth, including man, and for this reason, also, the entirety
of man, his physical as well as his psychical being.

WHY A SPINOZA INSTITUTE?
An address delivered by
Harry Waton

on the occasion of the founding of the Spinoza Institute
of America, December 18, 1927

We are a group of men interested in the philosophy of Spi-
noza. And your presence here indicates that you, too, are
interested in this philosophy. Otherwise, you would not have
been here now. In the philosophy of Spinoza, then, we have a
common interest. This common interest can and should be made
the basis for a joint endeavor to realize what would become a
common aim, namely, to make the idea of the philosophy of
Spinoza the gniding idea in the life of mankind. This is a dar-
ing and most ambitious aim—an aim, which though meritorious,
seems to be impossible of realization. Indeed, how can this aim
be realized? We are but a small group of men with limited means
at our disposal, and mankind is so crudely materialistic. How can
we undertake to transform mankind from crude materialists into
noble idealists? You will therefore regard us as utopians, vision-
aries and dreamers. Yet, we are neither utopians, visionaries, nor
dreamers, but we are realists in the fullest sense of the term. It
is because we are realists that we have undertaken to realize this
aim. This sounds paradoxical, yet, it is true. That you may per-
ceive this truth, it is necessary to consider the following:

The realization of an aim—whatever be its nature—is organ-
ically and indissolubly bound up with history. It is, in fact, an
organic part of history. History appertains to the essence and
existence of mankind. The understanding of history should,
therefore, be man’s supreme concern. Yet, men entertain the
most naive views about history. Some believe that history is
entirely made by men; that men can make their own history
as they please, and therefore, can realize any aim they want.
Such naive notions about history can be entertained only by men
that never seriously endeavored to realize any aim in life. These
men do not know what it means to realize an aim, and therefore

65

do not know what history is. There are others, more rational, who
believe that, though history is made by men, yet men do not
make their own history out of the whole cloth; that they make
their own history out of the given material conditions and in
accordance with the natural order of things. These men, in tak-
ing up any aim, will first make sure that the material conditions
require this aim, and that it can be realized in accordance with
the natural order of things. Yet, experience shows that these
men, notwithstanding their great knowledge, profound wisdom
and penetrating insight into things, never realized their aims;
that the results of their endeavors were usually different from
what they had expected, and often even contrary to what they
had anticipated. This should have caused rational men to sus-
pect that there is in history something more than material condi-
tions and a natural order of things.

Now, what is the truth about history? History is neither
made by men, nor is it determined by the material conditions,
nor does it proceed in accordance with the natural order of
things. History is not a natural process. It is a transcendental
process. History is made by destiny—a power that transcends
infinitely the power and wisdom of men, and transcends the
material conditions and the natural order of things. Out of the
material conditions and upon the basis of the natural order of
things, destiny makes through mankind history in accordance
with its own purpose. In the realm of history, man can at most
propose, but it is in the hands of God to dispose. Since men do
not make their history, and since the realization of an aim is
organically and indissolubly bound up with history, it is not in
the power and knowledge of men to determine beforehand,
whether a given aim will be realized or not. And history shows
that aims, which at first seemed to be impossible of realization,
were realized, while aims, which at first seemed to be easy of
realization, were never realized. For this reason, we are not con-
cerned about the question, whether the aim before us can and will
be realized or not. We are concerned about something else. We are
concerned about our duty—the duty dictated to us by our conscience
and reason. Our conscience and reason dictate to us the plain duty
to ourselves and others to endeavor to realize this aim—leaving
to destiny to show us whether this aim is to be realized or not.
By the fruit ye shall know. We shall know later by the results.

66

Now, whether mankind will ever clearly perceive what their des-
tiny 1s, whether men will ever understand what role the material
conditions play in history, and whether they will ever discover
the truth about the natural order of things, or not; these are
questions which must be left to future generations to answer.
For the present we have another guide in life. We have an im-
mediate and certain knowledge of what our conscience and
reason dictate to us, and the dictates of our conscience and
reason are the most certain and trustworthy guides in life. There-
fore, in guiding ourselves by the dictates of our conscience and
reason, we are more thoroughly realists than those that guide
themselves by the material conditions and the natural order of
things. Not we are utopians, visionaries and dreamers, but they
are utopians, visionaries and dreamers.

Being realists, we have initiated a movement for the realiza-
tion of the aim dictated to us by our conscience and reason, and
that aim is, to make the idea of the philosophy of Spinoza the
guiding idea in the life of mankind. The first step in the realiza-
tion of this aim is the creation of an organization. We intend
to call this organization, The Spinoza Institute of America.
And we shall ask you to join this organization, and help us
carry on the work before us. But, before you can and will de-
finitely decide to join this organization, we must answer several
questions that will naturally present themselves to you. The
questions are:

First, what reason is there for this movement? Secondly,
what will be the nature and function of the Spinoza Institute of
America, which we intend to create? And, finally, what will
the idea of Spinoza’s philosophy accomplish? These questions I
will answer in the order stated.

And, first, what reason is there for this movement? The
reason arises out of the character of our age. We live in an
age that is not merely materialistic, but is crudely materialistic.
Materialism in itself is both rational and necessary. Materialism
recognizes a universal condition to the existence of living beings;
including man. Whatever may be the ultimate nature of life
and its final destiny, in its bodily manifestation it depends upon
the material means of life and must function in a material world.
‘This is true of man as of other living beings. It is true that not
on bread alone does man live, but it is equally true that without

67

bread man cannot live. To live, man must have the material
means of life, and principally food, clothing, and shelter. These
material means of life are necessary, not only that man may live,
but also that he may satisfy his intellectual and spiritual require-
ments and make further progress. Mens sana in corpore sano.
That man may have a sound mind, he must have a sound body;
and that he may have a sound body, he must have the proper
material means of life. All intellectual and spiritual progress
presupposes a corresponding material progress, and the realiza-
tion of the highest ideal is possible only upon a proper material
basis. By taking thought, man cannot add a cubit to his intel-
lectual and spiritual stature. To raise himself intellectually and
spiritually, man must at the same time also raise his material
foundation. Since this is a universal condition to the existence
and progress of mankind, mankind should consciously recognize
this condition and explicitly endeavor to comply with this con-
dition. The conscious recognition of this universal condition to
the existence and progress of mankind constitutes materialism.
Materialism is, therefore, both rational and necessary.

But far different is the case of crude materialism. Crude
materialism postulates that on bread alone does man live. This
is an illusion. Life is more than meat, and man is more than
body. Whatever biologic kinship there may be between man and
the beast, man is essentially different from the beast. The essence
of man is reason. It is reason that differentiates man from other
living beings. Take away from man his reason, and he becomes
worse than a beast, and then must perish. A beast can maintain
itself as beast, because that is its nature; but man cannot main-
tain himself as a beast, because that is not his nature. Man can
destroy himself, but he cannot change his nature. Noblesse
oblige. The nobility of position in which man was placed de-
volves upon him the duty of nobility of conduct. Man was given
reason that he should use it, and live in accordance with its light.
Reason is the essence and the life of man. Reason should there-
fore be man’s supreme concern. And, while endeavoring to
secure the material means of life to satisfy his bodily needs, man
should at the same time endeavor to secure the spiritual means
of life to satisfy his reason. But crude materialism ignores reason
and disregards its intellectual and spiritual requirements. Crude

materialism postulates that on bread alone does man live. Crude
materialism is, therefore, an illusion.

Due to certain causes, which I shall presently point out,
mankind abandoned itself entirely to crude materialism. Men now
concentrate all their attention upon the material means of life,
and direct all their endeavors towards the acquisition and ac-
cumulation of material wealth, at the same time ignoring reason
and disregarding its intellectual and spiritual requirements. Men
became mere mechanical automatons, indifferent to all intellectual
and spiritual values. This is true, not only of the uneducated,
but equally true also of the educated; and this is true, not only
in the realm of action, but also in the realm of thought. Modern
science and philosophy became crudely materialistic. Modern
science and philosophy seek to interpret existence and life in
terms of matter and motion, without at the same time knowing
what matter and motion really are. In becoming crudely mater-
ialistic, modern science and philosophy lost their spirit. Whereas
previously science and philosophy were inspirations to mankind,
now they depress the spirit of mankind. Modern science and
philosophy are cynical and pessimistic. They take, in sober earnest,
the cynical and pessimistic ideas of old Koheleth. They say with
Koheleth, for that which befalleth the sons of men also befalleth
the beasts; even one fate befalleth them; as one dieth, so dieth
the other; yea, they have all the spirit, so that man hath no pre-
eminence above the beast; for all is vanity. And this has become
the universal attitude of mankind. Even in those cases in which
cultured men do recognize a distinction between man and the
beast, and do seem to appreciate the intellectual and spiritual
values, they appreciate them, not because they are conducive to
intellectual and spiritual growth, but because they can be exploited
tor material purposes. They pursue science, art, literature and
philosophy, not that through the exercise and discipline of their
reason they may acquire a mastery over themselves and over the
conditions of existence, but that they may relapse into a Nzrvanic
ataraxia, that they may lull themselves into an intellectual and
spiritual sleep. Now, man has not only a human nature, which
he cannot change; but man has also a human destiny, which he
cannot evade. To live in accordance with his nature and to realize
his destiny, man must perpetually endeavor to rise to ever higher
intellectual and spiritual perfection. But the moment man set-

And now we come to the final question. What can the idea
of the philosophy of Spinoza accomplish? It will acccomplish
what is necessary to our aim. The impending world crisis will re-
sult from the racial, national and class divisions of mankind and
their perpetual struggles against one another. It is necessary, not
only that mankind be brought back to reason, but also that man-

by the idea of the philosophy of Spinoza. To perceive this, we
must go to the root of the matter.

St. Paul said: We know in part, and we prophesy in part; but

done away. Mankind were divided into antagonistic races, nations
and classes because they knew only in part and they prophesied in

can be accomplished only by reason.

There was a time when reason was at one with itself. The
light of reason was yet undifferentiated. Reason then compre-
hended all functions of mankind, the material, intellectual and
spiritual. But a time came when this had to change. Reason, like
any other reality of existence, had to go through a process of evolu-
tion. Now, the two essential characteristics of evolution are differ-
entiation and integration. Reason began to differentiate its func-
tions, and in time became distinct and separated from one another.
Since reason is the essence of mankind, the distinct functions of
reason found their embodiment in distinct portions of mankind.
Some limited themselves to the material functions, others limited
themselves to the intellectual functions, and others limited them-
selves to the spiritual functions. Each portion of mankind then
began to know only in part and prophesied only in part. Now,
the essece of any reality of existence is the endeavor to persist in
its individual existence. But, as Spinoza says, determinatio est
negatio. The self-assertion of any reality implies a negation of other
realities. The self-assertion of each portion of mankind implied
a negation of the other groups of mankind. This resulted in a
struggle among the various portions of mankind against one an-
other. Hence the history of mankind is nothing else than the con-
tinuous struggle among races, nations and classes.

Since reason was the cause of all this division and struggle
among men, reason must overcome that division and struggle.
Now, as I stated before, the essential characteristics of evolution
are differentiation and integration. At the same time that the
members of an organism become differentiated in form and func-
tion, they also integrate. After the functions of reason have be-
come differentiated, they must now become integrated. And, since
reason is the essence of mankind, this integration of the functions
of reason must bring about an integration of mankind. This is
now taking place. This manifests itself in various ways. On the
one hand, we have the great international labor movement, whose
historic aim is to abolish all racial, national and class divisions of
mankind and all antagonism and struggle. On the other hand, it

and classes, and shall consolidate the whole human races into one
historic reality. This universal endeavor to unify the human race is
but the manifestation of the process of unification that goes on
among the functions of reason. It is the work of reason itself.

Now, reason speaks through ideas. An idea differs essentially
from a truth. Truth is the agreement between our cognitions
and the realities of existence. There can therefore be as many
truths as there are realities in existence. Now, if the realities of
existence had always remained the same in form and in their
interrelations our cognitions agreeing with them would always re-
main true. But the realities of existence perpetually change. The
cognitions that agreed with the realities before they changed no
longer agree with them after they changed. It follows, therefore,
that eternal truths are impossible. Nevertheless, there are eternal
truths, and these are ideas. Now, an idea is not only eternal, but
it is also universal. Take, in illustration, the idea of evolution. This
idea tells us that everything in existence perpetually changes, but
the law of evolution itself never changes. This is an eternal law,
that is, it is an eternal idea. Again, while there may be as many
truths as men have cognitions that agree with the realities of ex-
istence, there is only one idea of evolution. This idea is eternal
and comprehends all realities of existence.

Now, the idea of the philosophy of Spinoza is an eternal and
universal idea. It is the idea of reason itself. Spinoza’s philosophy
is not a synthesis of truths, but is the revelation of an eternal and
universal idea. This idea comprehends all functions of reason and
comprehends the whole human race together with the whole of
existence. Now, an eternal and universal idea is not the product
of an individual mind, nor even is it a product of the collective
mind of the human race. Such idea is the product of universal
reason, the reason that pervades the whole universe and determines
all processes of existence. Spinoza was only the means through
whom universal reason spoke to mankind. Until now, this idea
gestated in the minds of individual thinkers. Now the time has ar-
rived for this idea to come out into the world and function through
the whole human race. And, by identifying ourselves with the eter-
nal and universal idea of the philosophy of Spinoza, we are in fact
identifying ourselves with universal reason itself. Our aim is not

of history and of movements. Mankind lost faith in religion and
former ideals and ideas. To regenerate mankind, we must first
project an idea, which shall be the essence of the new movement

Universal reason is the destiny of human existence and his-
tory. It is destiny that speaks through us. Since this is the work

The Philosophy of the Kabbalah

By HARRY WATON

A ae Kabbalah is the esoteric doctrine of the Jews, which came
down to us from the most ancient times, and which embodies
the numerous systems of thought crystallized by the universal mind
of mankind. The Kabbalah is a trinity of soul, body and garment.
The garment consists of religious practices whose aim it is to bring
man into harmony with God and the order of existence. The body
consists of metaphysical ideas about God, existence and man. The
soul consists of a philosophy that reveals the purpose of creation,
the genesis, order and destiny of existence, the nature and destiny
of man, and shows the dependence of the phenomenal practical
world upon the transcendental noumenal world.

The Kabbalah is embodied in three principal works: Sepher
Yetziroh, Zohar and Tikune Zohar, and numerous commentaries
on these three standard works. Since these works had been closed,
most Kabbalists concerned themselves only about the body and
the garment of-the Kabbalah, wholly neglecting its soul. For this
reason, the Kabbalah is known to be but a mystical doctrine that
seems to have no relation to the phenomenal practical world, and
no bearing upon the earthly existence of man.

In this book, for the first time, the philosophy of the Kabbalah
is revealed, and now it clearly appears that in universality and
sublimity the Kabbalah transcends all other systems of thought
crystallized by metaphysicians and philosophers, and in depth and
essence it surpasses all sciences, modern as well as ancient. What
the modern scientists are seeking was long since discovered by the
Kabbalists, and what the modern relativists are vainly endeavoring
to solve the Kabbalah solved long ago.

Besides bringing to light this profound, sublime and universal
philosophy, the author of this book has another and higher aim,
and that is to reveal the nature, course and destiny of history.
Science is great, philosophy is high, metaphysics is sublime; but
greater, higher and sublimer than all is history. Science serves the
immediate practical purposes of man, philosophy satisfies the re-
quirements of his reason, and metaphysics gratifies the demands
of his intuition, but history comprehends the whole man, and
serves him in all his aspects. Man’s existence and destiny are bound
up with history. In this book, for the first time, an idea of univer-
sal history is presented, in the light of which the book of history
is revealed, so that one can read future history as well as past
history with perfect understanding.

THE PHILOSOPHY OF SPINOZA
By HARRY WATON

If Einstein, as President Butler said, is the monarch of the
mind, then Spinoza is the monarch of the intellect. In the realm
of the intellect—the union of the highest intuition and the most
explicit reason—Spinoza has no equal. His philosophy is the pro-
foundest and sublimest system of intuitive and rational thinking
that the human mind has crystallized. Spinoza’s philosophy was not
only the product of great and persistent mental effort, but it was
principally a direct and clear seeing into the Absolute, Infinite,
Eternal and Universal; a profound and clear comprehension of the
essence, nature and order of existence, and a penetrating insight
into the nature of man and the human mind. For this reason
Spinoza’s philosophy cannot be understood by mere study: one
must bring to the study of this philosophy a high intuition and
explicit reason, and one must spend many years in intimate com-
munion with the mind of Spinoa, to be able to grasp and under-
stand the infinite and sublime thoughts and ideas which he has
embodied in his philosophy.

Since the days of Spinoza, most of his works were translated
into many languages, and many books were written about his
philosophy; but no one has made an attempt to reveal to mankind
the philosophy itself. The present work is the first attempt to open
the works of Spinoza, and to reveal to mankind his philosophy.
He, to whom philosophy is not merely an amusement and an or-
nament, but to whom it is “a lamp unto his feet and a light unto
his path,” will find the present work of great use and value. It
will help him to enter into the sacred realm of the profoundest
and sublimest thoughts crystallized by the universal mind of man-
kind, and it will enable him to commune with Spinoza and un-
derstand his philosophy.

The present work contains a new translation, exposition
and elucidation of the two works of Spinoza: De Intellectus
Emendatione (The Freeing of the Intellect) and Cogitata Meta-
physica (Metaphysical Reflections). The text is explained, the
significance of the profound ideas is brought to light, and the
essence and aim of the whole philosophy are pointed out. This
present work will prepare the student to take up the study of
The Ethics—the greatest achievement of the human intellect. The
author of the present work has spent many years on the study of
Spinoza’s philosophy, and the present work is the product of
profound reflection on the problems of existence. It is for the
student of the present work to judge of its merit. But, whatever
be the judgment, there can be but one opinion, namely: it is the
first work of its kind in the realm of philosophy.
