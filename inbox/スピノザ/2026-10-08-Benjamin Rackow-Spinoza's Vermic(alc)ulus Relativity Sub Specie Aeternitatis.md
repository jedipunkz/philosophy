I. Introduction

I would like to propose that in the well-known but cryptic Letter XXXII to Henry Oldenburg, at the
time the secretary of the Royal Society of London and an associate of Robert Boyle’s, and which
consists of an extended metaphor about “a worm in the blood”, Baruch Spinoza anticipates the
conceptual principle of general relativity almost three centuries before its formal elaboration by
Einstein. The letter’s standard reading, originating with Gilles Deleuze, takes it to be an articulation of
the relationship between imagination and reason, which correspond to the whole and the part in
dialectical thought, which it certainly partially is;* Ilaria Gaspari interprets it as a reductio ad absurdum
in which the worm is read as a pure example of reason without imagination, and not meant to be
analogous to human perception at all—a difficult position to maintain given that in the letter Spinoza
states that the “little worm would live in the blood, in the same way as we live in our part of the
universe”—but there is some value in this reading nonetheless, as will be seen;* Filip Buyse does
propose a physical interpretation, related to the spontaneous synchronization of clock pendulums
observed by the physicist Christiaan Huygens, one of Spinoza’s associates, but reads into it only an
expression of the principles of classical mechanics and claims that it represents a curious exception to
the remainder of Spinoza’s metaphysics;* Richard Mason, in one of the few Anglospheric treatments of
the text, reads it as an articulation of Proposition 28 of part 1 of the Ethics, which states that there is
necessarily an infinite chain of causes for the existence of each thing, something like a form of the
prime mover argument.* That this letter concerns not only the internal nature of thought but also
anticipates conceptually the discoveries of relativity and Noether’s symmetry theorems, and
furthermore is to be considered in perfect continuity with the metaphysics later formalized in the
Ethics, will be demonstrated in the following.

The letter begins with a description of a small worm living in the circulatory system of some organism;
from its perspective, this circulatory system comprises the whole universe, and it knows nothing except
its constitutive corpuscules (“the lymph and chyles”, one can see here the organic outgrowth of this
metaphor from Spinoza’s livelihood as a microscope lens grinder). Although the blood can be
considered as a single fluid, the worm from its perspective can only see the movement of the individual
corpuscules which constitute the blood and so takes each particles to not be merely a partial view of the
greater fluid turbulence, but a self-contained object which “upon encountering another, either rebounds
or communicates a part of its motion”. This introduces the dialectical antinomy of the part and the
whole: considering the blood as a single entity, the movement of individual corpuscules is determined
by the general flow and so are not independent unities acting of themselves; and conceiving of each
part as a independent unity as the worm necessarily does, ignorant of the nature of the blood, these are
perceived as independent bodies that follow the laws of Cartesian mechanics. The context of the
correspondence is precisely a discussion of Descartes’ physical theories, as noted later in the letter;
although certainly the metaphor of the worm functions, as Deleuze and Gaspari maintain, as a kind of

This mistranslation is a likely source of some of the confusion surrounding the text, at least in the
Anglosphere, since it seems to foreclose the discussion of the whole and the part and demarcate the
preceding metaphor from what follows. A more faithful rendering of the Latin

“in this way the blood is apprehended as a part, not as a whole. Of the part and the whole I have

spoken of in the preceding”.®

This refers the metaphor back to the introduction of the letter where he states his general definition of
the part and the whole (translation mine):

“By the coherence of parts, therefore, I understand nothing other than that the laws or nature of one part
so accommodates itself to the laws or nature of another such that they contradict each other as little as
possible. Concerning the whole and the parts, I consider things to be parts of some whole to the extent
that their natures accommodate each other so that, as far as possible, they mutually agree; but insofar

as they are mutually discrepant, to that extent each forms an idea distinct from the others in our mind,
and consequently is considered as a whole, not as a part.”

Far from foreclosing the discussion of the whole and the part, this reinscribes the blood as being a part
in the part-whole dialectic. The distinction between part and whole is something that exists not
intrinsically in things themselves, but in the way in which the experiential world (or extension as
Spinoza calls it) is apprehended by the intellect; the corollary of Spinoza’s statement that “only in
relation to our imagination can things be called beautiful or deformed, ordered or confused” is that only
in relation to our imagination can the distinct parts of the undivided whole be taken as unities
themselves. Having first established a metaphorical system in which the blood represents the universe

“All things at once” is the literal translation of omnibus simul, and ratione is the singular form of ratio.
These slight distinctions aren’t trivial; the invariance of motion doesn’t reside in the bodies themselves
under the consideration of the faculty of the imagination, as Spinoza might put it, even in the form of
an infinite set, but in the unitary category of “all things at once”, i.e. the universe considered as a
singular unfolding in its temporal dimension, though the sum total of these bodies also evidently
constitutes this totality. The schema of the transition is as follows: the worm is transposed, explicitly,
onto our perspective as finite subjects peering out from a corner of the universe; and the blood is
transposed, explicitly, onto the concept of the universe as a whole. The metaphor is then not a
meaningful demonstration purely in itself, as Gaspari holds, but is being deployed by Spinoza as a
means of preparing the reader for this conceptual leap in which the important point is that we
necessarily have as little a conception of the totality of the universe as the worm does of the blood, and
that our experience of its modes, which seem to be self-contained, coherent wholes, are equivalent to
the corpuscules seen by the worm; they are in reality part of the general flow, and only distinguishable
insofar as they disagree with other parts of it. The above passage’s emphasis on temporal unity refers
not to specific modes but to the prior concept of substance as such, which converts the sentence from
one that could be read as (merely!) one of the earliest statements of an energy conservation law in
Western thought (i.e. the same amount of energy always exists in the universe) into something much
more radical. Insofar as there is in every moment the preservation of the same relation of movement
and rest, there exists therefore in the universe a total symmetry, and this symmetry consists of the idea
that all phenomenal differentiation, the domain whose correlates are the concern of classical mechanics,
is subtended by a preserved underlying unity.

This being Spinoza’s intended meaning is supported firstly by the phrase that bodies are “mutually
determined to exist and operate in a certain and determinate proportion” (translation mine, the existing
translation substitutes “fixed” for determinate but the duplication is present in the original Latin and

fixed gives a totally different valence to the statement) being already a formulation that extends beyond
classical mechanics, since here even the existence and proportion and not merely the operation of the
objects is determined dynamically; secondly, from the schematic transposition of the worm metaphor
by which he introduces this more abstract statement, establishing the idea of the unknowable whole;
and thirdly by this underlying unity being Spinoza’s preoccupation in the Ethics and the fundamental
ground of his philosophy in general. The universe is to be conceived of as a single, flowing unity like
the blood (albeit infinite) whose parts are determined according to the evolution of the whole and not
only their mutual interactions, or rather these are two sides of the same unfolding, in extension and
intellect, if such interactions are understood to be covariant rather than obeying the billiard ball-like
motion of the sanguineous corpuscules.

That this should be the basis of a physical theory is not a particular heterodox claim, since it’s more or
less universally accepted that a unified theory is required to reconcile relativity and the empirical
patchwork of quantum field theory, and that the ground for such a theory likely consists of a higher-
order unitary generator; but what is being proposed here is that this unity is not to be found in some
further elaboration of a geometric or algebraic object, but in the negative concept of unity itself (in the
sense of being unencompassable by the finitude of our minds except as a negation) which is precisely
the notion of Spinozist substance. That is to say that in 1665 Spinoza was already subjecting classical
mechanics to the critique later completed by Einstein and Noether in the early 20" century that inverts
the relative epistemic positions of univocity and phenomenal differentiation. In relativity, spacetime
curvature emerges from the invariance of the speed of light; in Noether’s theorems, conservation laws
emerge from invariance under transformation. These are not only mathematical formalism: the
unspoken common ground of all invariance is a presumed metaphysical unity. The vermiculus treated
earlier (not antiparasitically but sympathetically, since we are no other than such a thinking worm) is a
metaphorical representation of the assumptions of classical mechanics: there exist independent bodies
insofar as they are grasped by us as unities in our subjective experience of the world, and through
sensorial and intellectual perception (these corresponding to the attributes of extension and thought in
the Ethics) we map the correlations of the movements to each other in their various interactions, as in
Cartesian/Newtonian mechanics (or quantum mechanics for that matter, which inherits all the
functional assumptions of classical mechanics under the veneer of a supposedly different ontology,
insofar as its still consists of the laws of corpuscules, however vaguely defined these are). What is
being proposed by Spinoza is an inversion of the part and whole; instead of macro-scale existence
emerging from the aggregate of fundamental interactions, which is the (usually) unstated metaphysical
ground of quantum theory, these fundamental interactions emerge instead from the relation of the part
to the whole, and the evolution of a system comes about as a result of the necessary coherence of this
totality, which is the metaphysical ground of general relativity and gauge symmetry, and hence this
philosophical perspective is merely an excavation of the already operative logic within these.

evolution it is exactly with respect to the coherence of omnibus simul that the curvature of spacetime

II. Theory

Firstly, the question that emerges logically from the preceding is that if the fundamental unit of analysis
is an unchanging, undivided whole, why would there then be any motion or differentiation of spacetime
at all? This was Hegel’s (and Leibniz’) objection to Spinozist univocity, that it collapsed all distinction
into a mystical whole that foreclosed contradiction and inquiry. Spinoza’s answer is found in the
sentence immediately following his postulate of univocal symmetry, which also confirms that this
univocal symmetry is what was being proposed just prior:

“Hence it follows that each body, in so far as it exists as modified in a particular manner, must be
considered as a part of the whole universe, as agreeing with the whole, and associated with the
remaining parts. As the nature of the universe is not limited, like the nature of blood, but is absolutely
infinite, its parts are by this nature of infinite power infinitely modified, and compelled to undergo
infinite variations.”

This is an adequate response from a theo/logical perspective, one that prefigures the strain of cosmic
infinitude that seems to have permeated the consciousness of the 19" century, grasped by Boltzmann,
Blanqui and Nietzsche, and which can be summed up by the énoncé of that greatest of our
contemporary Spinozists, Eiichiro Oda: Everything that you can imagine exists! (Ethics Part 2, Prop.
VII: “The order and connection of ideas is the same as the order and connection of things.”). However,
we are peering out from our finite instantiation of this undivided infinitude, attempting to derive a
specific insight into the precise nature of the unfolding of G-d (that is to say, physics), which
necessarily must exist in some local sense since natura naturata (nature natured) must also unspool
itself according to the immanent causes of natura naturans (nature naturing), and not merely the
abstract logical terminus of substance monism, and hence we will require something a little more
specific than this statement, logically correct but ultimately lacking in any inductive power. But first,
outside of the indirect empirical evidence for the univocity of existence, which has as its phenomenal
form the invariance of the speed of light and consequently relativity, why should we take this postulate
as the ground floor of our intellectual edifice at all? In order to proceed we can a priori assume the
metaphysical singularity of substance according to the reasoning that Spinoza provides in Part 1 of the
Ethics, which as will be demonstrated consists of a formal proof that its various detractors, such as
Leibniz and Hegel, did not succeed in dispensing with, and in doing so re-derive a functional physical
theory.

Spinoza’s proof of the singularity of substance is as follows: firstly, he defines substance as that which
is prior to its modifications. The modifications are what we take as the things of the world; substance is
being defined as the negative unity of these modes. This is a proposition often cited as flawed for its
circularity, given that it relies on the earlier definition of modes and substance (Defs. iii and v).
However, we can derive these definitions from Axiom 1, which is a simple tautology and not a
metaphysical postulate: “Everything which exists, exists either in itself or in something else.” There are
two possibilities here:

1) Let A be an existent, and let B be the absolute set of existents except A. In this case the axiom holds,
because A cannot belong only to arbitrarily defined set C, since any such set C also belongs to B; and in

perceives as the essence of a substance (Def. iv).? Why must A and D be disjunct? If A and D share

both A and D), and A and D are therefore defined as the absolute sets that do not include themselves,
then A and D are part of the Russell set, the set of sets that do not include themselves, since the number
of these substances can be extended infinitely to E, F, G... As is well known, this set involves an
internal contradiction (if it’s part of itself, it isn’t part of itself and vice-versa) and cannot exist. This
leaves only the case where B is only a subset of one or neither of A or D, since in this case at least one
(or infinitely many) of these substances would no longer be the absolute set that doesn’t include itself
but be conceived purely through itself as a free substance. This is the last remaining logical possibility,
a final redoubt in Spinoza’s proof, and the one corresponding to Leibniz’ vision of a universe composed
of monads perfectly synchronized by G-d at the beginning of time. The Russell paradox wasn’t spelled
out by Spinoza but implicitly undergirds Axiom V and the corresponding Props. IV and V; these are

misinterpretation: if two substances possess the same attribute or modification, they would be capable
of limiting one another, and hence cannot form distinct substances. From this method also follows
Leibniz’ monad that allows for a multiplicity of substances that are disjunct. The set theoretic argument
is included to avoid the possible confusion that what is being considered is substance as its attributes or

substance, and that this argument is a logical one as well as a physical one, i.e. the contradiction holds

so there is only one substance.

From Spinoza’s proof emerge several corollaries that will be of use in deriving a physical theory from
substance monism:

Axiom 1: Uniqueness

This follows from the preceding demonstration; there can only exist a single substance.
Axiom 2: Indivisibility

This follows from the preceding demonstration and Axiom 1; to divide a substance posits the existence
of a second that splits it or forms its lacuna. Spinoza derived this corollary in Prop. XII.

Axiom 3: Inviolability

This follows from the preceding demonstration and Axioms 1 and 2; since substance is singular and
indivisible, it cannot contain any disjunction, or negate or overlap itself.

Axiom 4: Locality

This follow from Axioms 1, 2 and 3. The inviolability and indivisibility of substance is the sufficient
reason for the existence of locality as such, since without locality it would be in a state of universal
overlap or superposition and negate itself. And so if there is a unique substance, it cannot have but a
single, invariant speed of propagation. The idea of non-local correlation contravenes this, and also
introduces an implicit division in the nature of substance and so cannot exist. The question of how the
specific magnitude of c arises (which is only a measure relative to our perception in any case) is
distinct from its logical requirement given the uniqueness of substance, and whose invariance emerges
necessarily from its metaphysical unity.

III. Results

I freely admit that the above contradicts more or less the entire orthodoxy of physics, and if I were to

Non-Bohmian Pilot Wave, Bell Equality and Entanglement

We have derived the logical impossibility of nonlocality in the preceding Axiom 4, which brings us
headlong into conflict with Bell’s theorem, perhaps the most exhaustively verified result in modern
physics. This is not to claim that there is any mistake in the experimental verifications of Aspect and
Zeilinger among others, nor that there is an error in Bell’s derivation. However, there is, as with von
Neumann’s earlier proof of non-locality, a flawed assumption contained in the posing of the question
itself. The often elided fact that Bell was himself a believer in Bohmian mechanics explains why such a
question had to be posed; rather than being a pillar of support for the Copenhagen interpretation, as
frequently been portrayed, Bell was attempting to buttress the position of non-local hidden variable
theories, of which Bohmian mechanics precisely is one. This nonlocality emerged as an artefact of the
time-foliation approach inherent in the Schrödinger equation adapted by Bohm to articulate his vision,
since there being no definite, classical trajectory to a particle, the evolution of the system has to be
considered de novo at every moment; and likely also as a result of the general belief in the
impossibility of local hidden variables even prior to Bell’s theorem, owing to Grete Hermann’s 1936
critique of von Neumann’s 1932 no-go theorem not being widely known until Bell himself
rediscovered it decades later. However, if there indeed exists a definite history to this trajectory and the
associated wave (as a real physical phenomenon and not merely an abstraction, which therefore also
corresponds perhaps to Feynman’s path integrals) then this nonlocality is unnecessary. That the

Copenhagen interpretation and the standard quantum formalism represents a metaphysical absurdity
follows from the logic of the derivation of substance monism, and which emerged, in my opinion,
largely from the misapprehension of the double slit experiment. Instead of diving into the weeds and
attempting to show this immanently, it will be simpler to provide a coherent alternative hypothesis, one
that dispels both the cumbersome nonlocality of Bohmian mechanics and the idealism of the
Copenhagen interpretation, while in a sense representing a unification of their respective one-sided
epistemic positions.

IV. Conclusion

Given that this theory is more or less just a reinterpretation of the existing formalism and observations
to make them consistent with the necessity of substance monism, j’ignore if it has any practical
applications. My purpose in following this line of inquiry was only to show that things are at once very
simple and irreducibly deep; as Alain Badiou says, the difficulty of mathematics is that they are in a
sense too simple (n.b. not easy or facile) relative to the sinuous panoply of existence. While the
assumed multiplicities of physics finish by reproducing the world in the image of Benjamin’s
homogeneous empty time, the reduction to univocity of our understanding paradoxically brings us back
to the multitude of the world in its unique unfolding, and reorients us to its intellectual and ethical
demands. It is this reversal in philosophical thinking that marks the transition from classical to modern
physics, already articulated more or less explicitly by the early 20" c. developments of general
relativity and Noether’s work on invariance, and not the contemporaneous conjuration of the realm of
quantum indeterminacy and nonlocality. Whether or not Spinoza erred in stating the impossibility of
deriving a unified theory, since according to him such a thing would require knowledge of the totality
of Nature, consider that his metaphysical vision, which at the very least must be confronted by any
physicist as a formal challenge to the prima facie existence of fundamental multiplicity, forms only the
succinctly established foundation for the derivation of his ethical and political thinking; and since this
foundation is inseparable from the ethics and political thinking it entails, then I hope there will be some
value in staging this philosophical intervention.

LETTER XXXII
To the most Noble and most Learned Man, Henry Oldenburg, from B. D. S.
Reply to the Preceding

Most Noble Sir,

That you and the most Noble Mr. Boyle kindly encourage me to philosophize, I give the greatest
thanks; I indeed, as far as the slightness of my talent allows, proceed as much as I can, meanwhile not
doubting your help and benevolence.

When you ask what I think concerning the question which turns on this: that we may know how each
part of Nature agrees with its whole, and in what way it coheres with the others, I think you are asking
for the reasons by which we are persuaded that each part of Nature agrees with its whole and coheres
with the rest. For to know how they actually cohere, and how each part agrees with its whole—this I
said in my previous letter I do not know; because to know this would require knowing the whole of
Nature and all its parts.

I shall therefore attempt to show the reason which compels me to affirm this; yet first I wish to warn
that I do not attribute to Nature beauty, deformity, order, or confusion. For things cannot be called
beautiful or deformed, ordered or confused, except with respect to our imagination.

By the coherence of parts, therefore, I understand nothing other than that the laws or nature of one part
so accommodates itself to the laws or nature of another that contradict each other as little as possible.
Concerning the whole and the parts, I consider things insofar as they are parts of some whole to the
extent that their natures accommodate each other so that, as far as possible, they mutually agree; but
insofar as they are mutually discrepant, to that extent each forms an idea distinct from the others in our
mind, and consequently is considered as a whole, not as a part.

For example, when the motions of the particles of lymph, chyle, etc., mutually accommodate each other
in proportion to their size and figure so that they are plainly in mutual agreement and all together
constitute one fluid, to that extent only are chyle, lymph, etc., considered as parts of the blood; but
insofar as we conceive the lymphatic particles to differ from the particles of chyle in respect of figure
and motion, to that extent we consider them as a whole, not as a part.

Let us now imagine, if you please, a little worm living in the blood, which is capable of distinguishing
by sight the particles of blood, lymph, etc., and by reason of observing how each particle, upon
encountering another, either rebounds or communicates a part of its motion, etc. That worm would
indeed live in this blood as we live in this part of the universe, and would consider each particle of
blood as a whole, not as a part, nor could it know how all the parts are moderated by the universal
nature of blood and are compelled to accommodate themselves to one another, as the universal nature
of blood demands, so that they mutually agree in a certain proportion.

For if we imagine that there are no causes external to the blood which would communicate new
motions to the blood, and no space external to the blood, nor any other bodies into which the particles
of blood could transfer their motion, it is certain that the blood would always remain in its state and its
particles would undergo no other variations than those which can be conceived from the given relation
of the motion of blood to lymph, chyle, etc. And thus the blood would always have to be considered as
a whole, not as a part.

But because very many other causes exist, which moderate the laws of the nature of blood in a certain
way, and are in turn moderated by the blood, hence it happens that other motions and other variations
arise in the blood, which follow not solely from the relation of the motion of its parts to each other, but

from the relation of the motion of the blood and of the external causes to each other simultaneously. In
this way the blood is apprehended as a part, not as a whole. Of the part and the whole I have spoken of
in the preceding.

Now since all bodies of Nature can and must be conceived in the same way as we have here conceived
the blood—for all bodies are surrounded by others, and are mutually determined by each other to exist
and to operate in a certain and determinate proportion, while there is always preserved in all things at
once, that is, in the whole universe, the same relation of motion to rest—it follows from this that every
body, insofar as it exists modified in a certain way, must be considered as a part of the whole universe,
must agree with its whole, and must cohere with the others; and since the nature of the universe is not
limited, like the nature of blood, but is absolutely infinite, therefore its parts are moderated by this
nature of infinite power in infinite ways, and are compelled to undergo infinite variations.

But with respect to Substance, I conceive that each part has a closer union with its whole. For as I
formerly attempted to demonstrate in my first letter, which I wrote to you while still living at
Rijnsburg, since it is of the nature of Substance to be infinite, it follows that each part pertains to the
nature of corporeal Substance, and can neither be nor be conceived without it.

You see, therefore, in what way and why I hold that the human Body is a part of Nature. As for the
human Mind, I also hold that it is a part of Nature; namely because I maintain that there exists in
Nature an infinite power of thinking, which, insofar as it is infinite, contains within itself objectively
the whole of Nature, and whose thoughts proceed in the same way as Nature—that is, as its ideal
correlate.

Next, I hold that the human Mind is this same power, not insofar as it is infinite and perceives the
whole of Nature, but insofar as it is finite, namely insofar as it perceives only the human Body. In this
way I hold that the human Mind is a part of a certain infinite intellect.

But to explain and demonstrate all these things accurately here, along with what is connected to this
matter, would be too lengthy a task, nor do I think you expect this from me at present. Indeed, I doubt
whether I have sufficiently grasped your meaning, and whether I have answered something other than
what you asked—which I would like to know from you.

As for what you then write, that I hinted that almost all of Descartes' rules of motion are false: if I
remember correctly, I said that Mr. Huygens thought this, and I affirmed that no other rule is false
except Descartes’ sixth rule, regarding which I said I thought both Huygens and Descartes were in
error. On this occasion I asked you to communicate to me the experiment which you performed in your
Royal Society according to that hypothesis; but I judge that you are not permitted to do so, since you
say nothing in reply about this.

The said Huygens has been and still is entirely occupied with polishing dioptric lenses. For this purpose
he has constructed a machine, quite a fine one, in which he can also turn grinding dishes. But what he
has achieved with it I do not yet know, nor, to tell the truth, do I greatly wish to know. For experience
has sufficiently taught me that spherical lenses are polished more safely and better by free hand than by
any machine. Concerning the success of the pendulums and the time of his departure for France, I
cannot yet write anything certain. [...]

Voorburg, 20 November 1665.
With all affection, yours,
B. de Spinoza.
