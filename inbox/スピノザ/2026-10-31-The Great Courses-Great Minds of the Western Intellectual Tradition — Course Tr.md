Prologue

There is a temptation, when surveying the history of Western
thought, to imagine it as a succession of great names arranged in
chronological order. One begins with the Greeks, proceeds through
Christianity and the Middle Ages, encounters the Renaissance and
the Enlightenment, passes through the ideological conflicts of the
nineteenth century, and arrives at the intellectual fragmentation of
the modern world. The result can resemble a museum tour, a
sequence of famous figures and influential books displayed behind
glass, admired for their historical significance but disconnected from
the concerns of contemporary life.

This course invites a different perspective.

The history of ideas is not primarily a history of doctrines. It is a
history of questions. Philosophers, theologians, scientists, political
theorists, economists, and social critics may disagree profoundly with
one another, yet they often participate in a common conversation
extending across centuries. The participants change. The vocabulary
evolves. Historical circumstances transform the context. But the
deepest questions persist. What is ultimately real? How can human
beings know the truth? What makes a life good? What is justice?
Does history possess meaning? Is there a God? What can reason
accomplish? What are the limits of freedom? How should human
beings live together?

These questions are older than any civilization now existing, and they
remain as urgent today as when they were first posed.

The eighty-four lectures that comprise Great Minds of the Western
Intellectual Tradition follow the evolution of this conversation across
more than two and a half millennia. The course begins with the
earliest Greek philosophers, whose bold attempts to understand

nature through reason rather than myth established many of the
assumptions underlying Western inquiry. It concludes with
contemporary debates concerning language, science, tradition,
liberalism, ideology, and the future of modernity. Between these
points lies one of humanity's most remarkable intellectual journeys.

The story begins in the ancient Mediterranean world. The Pre-
Socratics ask what the universe is made of. The Sophists challenge
assumptions about truth and justice. Socrates turns philosophical
attention toward the examined life. Plato explores the nature of
reality, knowledge, and_ political order. Aristotle develops
comprehensive frameworks for understanding ethics, politics, logic,
and the natural world. The Hellenistic schools confront the problem
of human happiness in an uncertain world. By the end of antiquity,
the foundations of Western philosophy have been laid.

Yet the classical tradition is only one of the two great sources from
which Western civilization emerges.

Alongside Athens stands Jerusalem.

The biblical tradition introduces a radically different intellectual
orientation. Instead of beginning with philosophical speculation, it
begins with revelation, covenant, history, and faith. The God of the
Hebrew scriptures is not an abstract principle but an active presence
who enters into relationships, makes promises, judges actions, and
guides historical events. The encounter between the classical and
biblical traditions becomes one of the defining events in the history
of ideas. The Christian Age witnesses the gradual synthesis of Greek
philosophy and Christian theology through thinkers such as
Augustine, Aquinas, and countless others who sought to reconcile
faith and reason within a unified vision of reality.

For nearly a thousand years, this synthesis provided the intellectual
framework of Western civilization.

Yet no intellectual framework remains unchallenged indefinitely.

The Renaissance reopens the classical inheritance and encourages a
renewed confidence in human creativity and inquiry. The
Reformation fractures religious unity and transforms conceptions of
authority. The scientific revolution alters humanity's understanding of
nature. New methods of observation, mathematics, and
experimentation reveal a universe governed by discoverable laws
rather than inherited assumptions.

The result is the emergence of the modern world.

Thinkers such as Bacon, Descartes, Hobbes, Locke, Spinoza, and
Leibniz attempt to reconstruct knowledge upon new foundations.
Their efforts culminate in the Enlightenment, an age animated by
extraordinary confidence in the power of reason. If human beings
can understand nature, perhaps they can also improve society. If
inquiry can free us from ignorance, perhaps it can also free us from
oppression. The Enlightenment becomes one of the most influential
intellectual movements in history, shaping modern conceptions of
science, politics, rights, education, and progress.

Yet every intellectual triumph generates new problems.

The nineteenth century inherits the achievements of the
Enlightenment while confronting realities that challenge its
optimism. Industrialization transforms economies and_ societies.
Nationalism reshapes political identities. Secularization alters the
cultural landscape. Philosophers respond by constructing vast
ideological systems intended to explain history and human destiny.
Hegel, Marx, Comte, Kierkegaard, Schopenhauer, and Nietzsche offer
competing visions of modernity, each attempting to make sense of a
world undergoing unprecedented change.

The twentieth century, in turn, subjects those visions to scrutiny.

Psychoanalysis reveals hidden’ dimensions of the _ self.
Phenomenology investigates consciousness. Existentialism confronts
freedom and meaning. Analytic philosophy examines language and
logic. New approaches emerge from the recognition that certainty is
more elusive than previous generations believed. At the same time,
world wars, totalitarian movements, technological revolutions, and
social transformations force intellectuals to reconsider the
assumptions underlying modern civilization itself.

The final part of the course examines these crises of modernity. The
debate is no longer simply about what Is true but about how human
beings can sustain meaningful forms of knowledge, morality,
freedom, and political order in a world increasingly aware of its own
complexity and contingency. Contemporary thinkers confront
questions that would have been recognizable to Plato, Augustine,
and Kant, yet they do so under historical conditions those earlier
figures could scarcely have imagined.

This continuity is one of the central themes of the course.

The history of Western thought is not a sequence of disconnected
eras. Each generation inherits questions from its predecessors,
modifies them, challenges them, and passes them forward. Plato
remains present in Augustine. Aristotle reappears in Aquinas. The
debates of the Reformation influence the Enlightenment. Nietzsche
shadows much of twentieth-century thought. Ideas rarely disappear.
More often, they are transformed.

The purpose of studying this tradition is not merely historical. These
lectures do not ask us to memorize doctrines or revere intellectual
authorities. They invite us to participate in an ongoing conversation.
The thinkers we encounter are not relics of the past. They are
interlocutors. Their questions remain our questions because the
fundamental problems of human existence have not vanished.

We still wonder about truth and meaning.

We still struggle to reconcile freedom and order.

We still debate the relationship between faith and reason.
We still ask what justice requires.

We still seek happiness, knowledge, purpose, and wisdom.

The Western intellectual tradition offers no final answer to these
questions. Indeed, one of its greatest lessons may be that final
answers are rarely available. What it does offer is something more
enduring: a record of humanity's most sustained effort to understand
itself and its world through disciplined reflection.

That effort begins with wonder.
It continues through disagreement, criticism, revision, and discovery.
And it remains unfinished.

The journey that follows is therefore not merely an exploration of the
past. It is an invitation to join one of the longest and most
consequential conversations in human history.

Part I: Classical Origins

Preamble

There is a particular kind of intellectual courage required to ask a
question you cannot answer — to sit with uncertainty and treat it not
as a failure of nerve but as the very beginning of wisdom. That
courage is the common thread running through the twelve lectures
that make up this opening part of Great Minds of the Western
Intellectual Tradition. From the bold, sometimes reckless speculations
of the earliest Greek thinkers to the disciplined skepticism of a
Roman physician writing at the edge of a dissolving empire, Part |
traces the long and restless arc of classical philosophy in its first and
foundational flowering. It is a journey that begins, appropriately
enough, with the question of what a journey through philosophy
even Is.

The introductory lecture wastes no time in confronting that question
directly. Philosophy, we are told, may be understood in at least three
ways: as the rational pursuit of perennial truths, as an historical
discipline whose questions and methods shift from era to era, and as
something older and more irreducible than either of those
descriptions — the love of wisdom, born of contemplative wonder.
This triple characterization is not merely an academic throat-clearing.
It is a genuinely useful compass. As the lectures unfold across seven
parts and eighty-four installments, the listener will find that
sometimes philosophy looks like a rigorous scientific program,
sometimes like a political manifesto, sometimes like a_ spiritual
autobiography, and sometimes like all three at once. Part | introduces
the full range of what philosophy can be by letting the ancient world
demonstrate it in practice.

The story begins not in Athens but along the trade-soaked coastline
of lonia, in cities like Miletus where the convergence of cultures
made received mythology feel insufficient. The Pre-Socratics —
Thales, | Anaximander, Anaximenes, Pythagoras, Heraclitus,
Parmenides, and Zeno — were the first thinkers in the Western
record to redirect the fundamental questions of existence away from
divine agency and toward material and logical explanation. What is
the world made of? How does change happen? Is motion even real?
These are questions that modern readers may associate with physics
textbooks, but the Pre-Socratics pursued them with an almost
religious intensity, and the tension between reason and revelation
that they first opened up has never fully closed. Pythagoras, in
particular, reminds us that the line between mathematics, mysticism,
and music was once a great deal thinner than we tend to assume. His
conviction that numbers undergird the cosmos is not merely a
historical curiosity; it is a founding intuition of modern science, one
that Galileo would invoke two millennia later when he declared
mathematics the language of the universe.

The Sophists arrive next, and they arrive as something of a
provocation. The very word has been used as an insult for so long
that it takes a deliberate act of historical generosity to restore them
to their proper dignity. They were, in their time, the world's first
professional intellectuals — teachers of rhetoric, political analysis,
and the close examination of human custom and law. Their great
contribution, which Plato resented precisely because he recognized
its power, was the discovery that most of what human beings call
justice, morality, and natural law is in fact convention, constructed
rather than given, variable rather than eternal. The Melian Dialogue,
as Thucydides records it, is the Sophists' most chilling exhibit:
Athenian generals informing a small neutral island that justice is
simply the will of the stronger, and that nature has always been so.
This is uncomfortable reading. It was meant to be.

Plato's three lectures occupy the gravitational center of Part |, and
rightly so. His response to the Sophists — and to Socrates’ execution
at the hands of a democratic state — was not merely philosophical
but almost theological in its intensity. The theory of Forms posits that
behind the changing, unreliable world of appearances there exists a
realm of unchanging truth accessible only to the mind's eye. The
allegory of the cave gives that doctrine an unforgettable mythic
shape: most of us live chained in darkness, watching shadows on a
wall and mistaking them for reality. Education, in Plato's vision, is the
painful process of turning around and climbing toward the light. His
political philosophy in the Republic follows directly from this
metaphysics: if truth is knowable, then those who know it should
rule. The philosopher-king is not an arrogant fantasy but the logical
conclusion of Plato's deepest premises. His psychology adds a third
dimension, dividing the soul into rational, spirited, and appetitive
parts whose proper ordering mirrors the ideal ordering of the just
city. What makes Plato so inexhaustible — and why one philosopher
called all of Western thought a series of footnotes to him — is that
his reach is total. He addresses being, knowledge, beauty, love,
justice, death, and the nature of the self, and he addresses them all
together, as parts of a single, interlocking vision.

Aristotle is, in a sense, Plato's greatest gift to posterity, and also his
greatest critic. His three lectures here cover metaphysics, politics, and
ethics. Where Plato pointed upward toward eternal forms, Aristotle
pointed outward at the world of growing, changing, purposeful
things. His four causes, his doctrine of potentiality and actuality, his
insistence that form is always embodied in matter — these are the
tools of a man who wanted philosophy to account for oak trees and
animals and human cities, not just for abstract triangles in a realm
beyond the sky. His ethics of virtue as a mean, of happiness as the
activity of the rational soul in accordance with excellence, and of
friendship as essential to the good life, remains among the most

livable moral philosophies ever articulated. His politics, with its
advocacy for a broad middle class and its suspicion of concentrated
power, anticipates concerns that will echo through every subsequent
century.

The final three lectures of Part | step out of Athens and into the wider
Greco-Roman world, where philosophy meets the demands of
empire, civil war, and personal survival. Epicureanism and Stoicism
both emerge as responses to a world in which the city-state no
longer offers protection or meaning, and in which the individual
must construct a private ethics adequate to a very large and
indifferent universe. Cicero's eclectic synthesis — blending Stoic
duty, Epicurean pleasure, and Aristotelian political realism —
represents Rome's most generous intellectual achievement, a
philosophy built not for the schoolroom but for the active life of a
statesman who knew that certainty was rare and that the best one
could do was choose wisely among imperfect options. And Sextus
Empiricus, writing at the far edge of this tradition, asks the question
that every previous thinker had tried to suppress: what if we simply
cannot know? His answer — that we should trust experience,
conform to custom, suspend judgment, and keep inquiring anyway
— is not the counsel of despair. It is, in its own quiet way, a form of
courage.

Part |, then, is not merely a survey of ancient philosophy. It is an
initiation into a conversation that has never stopped, conducted by
minds whose questions remain, two and a half millennia later,
stubbornly, beautifully unresolved.

Lecture 1: Introduction
Professor Darren Staloff

What is Philosophy?

In this lecture, we seek to set the parameters and framework for the
birth of philosophy in the ancient world — first among those
pioneers of ancient Greek philosophy, the pre-Socratics, then among
the famed classical Greek thinkers Socrates, his student Plato, and
Plato's student Aristotle, and then in the Hellenistic and Roman
period, a period characterized by syncretism.

But first, it is probably useful to ask a straightforward question: what
is philosophy? What do we mean by philosophy? What kind of a
discipline is this?

Philosophy can be characterized in a number of different ways. We
can describe it as the rational pursuit of truths — truths conceived as
answers to perennial and eternal questions. This is one way of
thinking about the various sub-disciplines of philosophy.
Metaphysics, or ontology, is the study of being, or the ultimate
nature and structure of the universe: what is out there, and how does
it relate to everything else? Ethics is the study of the nature of the
good life and the nature of the good itself, of value. It is obviously
closely associated with political philosophy, which examines the
nature of political life and seeks to characterize such public virtues as
justice, equality, and fraternity. Aesthetics examines the nature of the
beautiful and its embodiment both in nature and in works of art.
Epistemology examines the question of knowledge — we might
think of it as the theory of knowledge — and specifically attempts to
characterize the nature of truth, science, and rational inquiry, while
endeavoring to offer criteria for each. Philosophy of mind, or

philosophical psychology, is the study of the workings of the psyché,
the soul or the mind, analyzing its functions and mechanisms. And
natural philosophy attempts to offer a rational account of the natural
world, both organic and inorganic, again with an eye toward rational
explanation.

But that is only one way of thinking about philosophy. We could also
describe philosophy as a historical discipline. As such, it would be
subject to change over time, which means that different epochs are
concerned with rather different issues. Post-Platonic philosophy, for
example, was concerned with metaphysical and ethical issues —
those were really the core of its concentration and focus. Early
Christian philosophy was interested in theology and metaphysics: the
relationship of the divine to the human and God's working out of his
providential plan in time and space. Modern philosophy, by contrast,
has been concerned far more with epistemological issues —
questions about the nature of science, truth, and rational inquiry —
as well as with political considerations, as publics have emerged to
become important political forces.

On yet another level, we might observe that philosophical questions
which prove tractable give us a very interesting insight into the
history of the discipline. Once such problems prove tractable,
amenable to fairly straightforward solutions, they often cease to
remain philosophical. The natural philosophers Galileo and Newton,
for example, were so successful in their endeavors that the subject
they created became a separate discipline — the one we know as
physics, and in_ particular mechanics. Similarly, the political
speculations of many _ post-Machiavellian theorists laid the
groundwork for the new disciplines of political science, sociology,
and even economics. And the phenomenological analyses of post-
Humean philosophers — that is, philosophers who examined the
phenomenological or immediate experience we have in our mental

life — gave rise to the modern discipline of experimental psychology.
The principle of association and the notion of stimulus and response,
which form the very core of modern experimental and behavioral
psychology, all trace their lineage back to philosophy, just as
existentialism and the philosophy of mind combined to give birth to
modern psychoanalysis.

One might therefore think of philosophy as the historical study of
intractable problems, such that as problems become tractable they
cease to be philosophical. This requires us to be aware that different
epochs not only concern themselves with different issues, but frame
those issues differently. In the ancient world, for instance,
metaphysics was organized around the issue of hylomorphism — the
relation of form or structure to content or matter — and that
question dominated the thought of both Plato and Aristotle.
Christian metaphysics, by contrast, was organized around the
relation between the fallen world of man and the realm of the divine,
giving rise to a very different way of framing the same fundamental
questions about what exists and what the universe is made of.
Modern metaphysics, in turn, has been organized around dualisms
— the dualism of mind and body, and most importantly the dualism
of the empirical and mechanistic versus the ideal or super-sensible.

We might similarly note that the mind of the ancient Greeks — nous,
as it were — was characterized by its participation in universal
concepts. What made something a mind was its ability to grasp
universals. This stands in stark contrast to our post-Cartesian
understanding of mind, which is not defined by its ability to
apprehend universals and universal concepts but rather by its
experience of particular phenomenological states — immediate
feelings which are never universal but are always particular and
individual to the person who has them. States like pain, doubt, or
pleasure are unique and in some sense ineffable, because each

person has_ exclusively privileged access to their own
phenomenological states. So even a topic like the philosophy of
mind changes dramatically over time as people think about mind in
fundamentally different ways. Plato's mind was not only subject to
universals but was itself universal; one could speak of nature as
having nous or mind. One cannot do that within’ the
phenomenological framework of Descartes.

The political philosophy of both Plato and Aristotle similarly
presupposed that political association was natural for man. Man is a
zoon politikon, a political animal. That was an essential part of human
nature; one would never find men outside of political associations.
Contrast that with modern social theorists such as Hobbes or Locke,
who assume instead that the natural state of man — the so-called
state of nature — is an isolated one in which each individual lives on
his own prior to political association, and that the motivations of
such individuals are always self-interested rather than directed
toward the good of the polis as a whole.

This also means that philosophy and its practitioners occupy
different cultural and social roles in different epochs, which is, | think,
very important. Socrates and Plato, for example, were both social and
cultural critics who took an extremely adversarial stance toward the
cultural and political authorities of their time. Both rejected the
teachings of religious authority — principally that of Homer — and
also criticized the existing democratic practices of Athens. Medieval
philosophers, by contrast, generally found themselves in the employ
of the Church. They were generally churchmen or university
professors teaching at universities associated with the Church, and as
a result philosophy was conceived to be, in the words of Aquinas, the
handmaid of theology. Both philosophy and the philosopher thus
occupied a very different social role than in the ancient world. In
modern philosophy, we find again that philosophers tend to be

critical — offering critiques of society, or trying to serve as cultural
referees who judge the veracity, tenability, and legitimacy of various
discourses and disciplines by deploying criteria of truth and
rationality to assess the various arts, humanities, and sciences.

But — and here | think is the third way we must think about
philosophy — philosophy is also certainly what its very name implies:
to translate from the Greek, the love of wisdom. In this sense,
philosophy is ultimately born of contemplative wonder and a sense
of awe toward the nature of being and the universe. As such,
philosophy both expresses a posture toward being, a sort of
metaphysical stance, and seeks to fulfill both spiritual and existential
needs, as well as purely rational and explanatory roles. Ancient
philosophy thus begins with a combination of rational curiosity and
reverence — the same combination found in its precursors, the
famed gymnosophists of the ancient subcontinent, the naked forest
speculators whom we call yogis to this day, and whom many scholars
have argued were in fact precursors to Socrates.

The Pre-Socratics: The World of Objects

Let us turn now to the first epoch in the birth of philosophy in the
ancient and classical world — that of the pre-Socratics — who truly
represent the birth of Western philosophical speculation, a
speculation that began in the wider Greek diaspora.

The first phase of pre-Socratic thought was devoted to
understanding the world of nature — the natural world, what we
might describe as the world of objects. The lonians, for example,
sought to understand the natural world by reducing its multiplicity to
a finite number of entities, beginning with the four classic Greek
elements — earth, water, air, and fire — and then attempting to
reduce even those to still simpler entities: Thales with water,

Anaximander with the unlimited, and Anaximenes with air. They thus
sought to answer a fundamental metaphysical question: what is the
universe made of? What exactly is out there?

In contrast, the Pythagoreans sought to reduce the same material
realities to a simpler scheme, but in their case the scheme consisted
of mathematical objects. That reduction of material objects to
mathematical entities highlighted the ways in which mathematics
helps us model our experience of the natural world. It is, of course,
not coincidental that philosophy begins this rigorous mode of
rational discourse in a culture which had mastered deductive
mathematics, both in Euclidean geometry and in the work of
Pythagoras himself.

Heraclitus and Parmenides — the great opponents on the
metaphysics of the post-Pythagorean epoch — addressed not the
question of what the universe is made of, but rather the question of
change and continuity. How does change occur? Is change even
possible? In approaching these questions in an abstract and logical
way, it was really they who helped give rise to metaphysics as a
logical discipline, a discipline grounded in abstract reasoning. Both
the dynamism of Heraclitus and the monism of the Eleatics would
have far-reaching influences on subsequent thinkers across the
centuries. Nietzsche, for example, drew heavily on Heraclitus for
philosophical inspiration, deeply moved by Heraclitus’ sense of flux,
chaos, and strife in the universe. There are also very deep and
obvious affinities between the monistic cosmology of Parmenides
and the monistic determinism of Baruch Spinoza in the seventeenth
century.

The Pre-Socratics: The World of Subjects

The second phase of pre-Socratic thought turned from the analysis
of the world of objects, or nature, to the study of the human world
— the world of subjects. This shift was in part the result of the
profusion of doctrines about nature, each of which was equally
persuasive and each of which contradicted the others. Such a
profusion of equally persuasive but mutually contradictory doctrines
tends to produce skepticism, and that skepticism about the
possibility of reliable knowledge of nature caused thinkers to redirect
their inquiry toward the human and social world.

Now, both Heraclitus and the Pythagoreans had in fact offered
ethical doctrines, though of a radically different character. But the
truly critical figures here are the Sophists, because the Sophists
represent the first full-blown engagement with the problems of
politics, ethics, and human sociability. Their analysis was empirical —
they studied constitutions and examined political practices in a
realistic fashion — and their empirical analyses of political motivation
and power formed the basis for subsequent political science as well
as subsequent political philosophy. Particularly important was their
discovery of the role of convention and custom, as opposed to
regular mathematical law, in human affairs. This finally forced serious
thinkers to recognize that many of our political and social values are,
in fact, culturally determined and conventional — varying from place
to place and time to time.

The Classical Greek Philosophers

This brings us to the high point of the ancient world: the
development of the great classical Greek philosophers — Socrates,
his student Plato, and Plato's student Aristotle — all of whom lived
or taught in Athens, and all of whom drew heavily on the pre-

Socratic traditions, as well as on each other's teachings, to construct
what are really the first full-blown philosophical systems, particularly
in the thought of Plato and Aristotle.

Socrates

Socrates' great breakthrough and most important contribution was
the development of ethical rationalism — the belief that people act
on the basis of what they think is good. On this view, there is no
perversion of the will, which means that immoral action is the result
of a failure of knowledge rather than a failure of character. Socrates
also taught that ethical knowledge was teachable.

The Sophists had maintained that morality and political order were
based on convention, and were therefore obviously relative to those
conventions. Socrates rejected these skeptical conclusions. He
insisted instead that ethical truths were absolute and demonstrable,
and his model for this was geometry: through carefully defining
ethical terms and grasping their essence, we could arrive at final and
fixed moral truths. And as mentioned, Socrates held that these truths
were not only rational but teachable — a doctrine with profound
implications for the role of the philosopher. If one can teach virtue,
and if the only thing preventing virtuous behavior is a lack of
knowledge, then the philosopher, as ethical teacher, takes on a
critical and indeed primary role within the polis as instructor and
censor of politics and morals.

Socrates further taught that all people act on the basis of their
beliefs, and thus that the problem of morality is really a problem of
knowledge, not of will — a position quite antithetical to the later
Christian tradition. This was summed up in his famous expression: to
know the good is to do the good. This made ethical philosophy the
sine qua non of a just and stable social and political order, and

placed philosophy at the very center of Athenian cultural life, at least
in philosophy's own self-understanding.

Plato

Plato injected his teacher's ethical rationalism into a remarkable
overarching philosophical synthesis. Plato's metaphysics were
designed to address the problem of change raised by Heraclitus and
the Eleatics, and his doctrine of forms, essences, or ideas was clearly
intended to divide natural objects into, on the one hand, their
structure or essential nature — what he called the form — and on
the other hand their material content. This helped resolve the
problem of change, because the forms were definable and therefore
eternal and unchangeable, whereas it was the material content of
things that was subject to change.

For Plato, this also had profound epistemological implications.
Precisely because the forms were definable and eternal, they were
the subjects — indeed the only proper subjects — of rational
knowledge. Changes arising from the material principle, by contrast,
cannot be reduced to abstract definable terms, and therefore, Plato
argues, we can never have a truly scientific understanding of change
in the physical world. Plato used this dualism of form and content to
argue further that the forms of things were in some sense
metaphysically distinct from and prior to the material objects that
participated in them. Although all the objects we encounter are a
mixture of form and matter, the forms actually exist independently in
some metaphysical realm and possess greater reality — because they
are more knowable and eternal — than the particular things that
participate in them. In this way, Plato offered the first full-blown
dualist ontology: the view that beyond our own realm of sense and
appearance, there exists another realm, another world of eternal and
unchanging truth.

Plato's ethical and political doctrines were informed by his adoption
of Socratic ethical rationalism. Since for Plato politics is simply ethics
writ large — summed up in that wonderful formulation in the
Republic — Plato assumes that philosophy or rational inquiry is
capable of generating the same kind of absolute certainty for the
polis that it can generate for the ethical life of the individual. This
means that Plato's political thought is not only descriptive, as had
been the case with the Sophists, but prescriptive: it holds out a
model or blueprint which we are ethically obliged to try to bring to
realization. And it is that prescriptive understanding of political
philosophy that would become the source for much of the
subsequent idealist tradition in political speculation.

If we look at the Republic in particular, we find doctrines with a long
legacy in that idealist tradition. Plato was the first to critique private
property as a source of social injustice — a form of communism in
the original sense. He was the first to argue for the equality of
women insofar as they participate in mathematical rationality — an
early feminism. And he argued, ultimately, for the rule of
philosophers within the state and the culture.

Plato's psychology drew on his metaphysics in being again based on
a dualism of soul — or form — and body, or material content. But
Plato further analyzed the psyché into three distinct faculties or
functions: the rational or calculative part of our minds, which
engages in mathematical analysis; the spirited part, associated with
courage and our sense of ego; and the appetitive part, that which
desires. Each of these corresponds to a different social class in the
Republic, and the Platonic ideal is one in which a healthy soul is
governed by the rational faculty, with the aid of the spirited or ego-
bearing will, over the base animal appetites. This scheme would have
a very long history indeed. One might think of the Christian notion of
each soul being tempted by demonic urges corresponding to Plato's

appetitive part, or guided by angels corresponding to the intellective
and moral part. One might equally compare it to Freud's tripartite
analysis of the mind into a superego — much like Plato's intellective
part — an ego corresponding to the spirited part, and the id
corresponding to the appetitive or libidinous part.

Aristotle

Plato's student Aristotle adopted the overall approach of his mentor
but criticized some of his central doctrines on what we would call
realistic grounds. Aristotle rejected the metaphysical separation of
form and content, arguing that forms exist only in their participation
in actual things — that objects, though admittedly composed of
form and content, do not license the inference that there exists a
separate world of forms themselves. Aristotle resolved the problem
of change not through Platonic hylomorphism but through his own
doctrine of entelechy, which held that natural objects have natural
ends or potentials toward which they tend unless interrupted. The
acorn under the right conditions becomes the oak; the child under
the right conditions becomes the adult. Even inanimate objects tend
to rest unless moved, and all objects tend toward their natural level.

Aristotle further posited a fourfold causal nexus — formal, material,
efficient, and final causes, which will be explored in subsequent
lectures — that would have an immense impact on European
thought all the way to the scientific revolution. It was an analysis that
synthesized metaphysics with the science of his day, and Aristotle
was quite a scientist. For him, the soul is the final cause of motion in
organic matter — it is the entelechy of the person. He identified four
forms of souls, the ultimate form of which is the pure actuality of
God, the prime mover, who sits atop the chain of being.

Aristotle also criticized the conclusions of Plato's political and ethical
philosophy, again on realistic grounds. He rejected the communal

arrangements of his teacher as impractical and offered instead an
empirical account of the various forms of states and the factors —
social and economic — that determine them, rather than what he
considered an illusory quest for one single best and true form of
government. Rather than rule by philosopher-kings, Aristotle inclined
toward the republican rule of a broad middle class. In many ways,
Aristotle as the great realist is the founder of modern realistic
political thought, as well as of the realistic tradition in practical
metaphysics and epistemology.

Finally, Aristotle also held that justice and virtue are not pure
Sciences mathematically demonstrable in the way Socrates and Plato
had taught, but are rather the result of practical judgment, or
phronesis. One learns the truth of political association and how to
live one's life well not through abstract reasoning, but through
practical life and through learning from experience.

The Hellenistic and Roman Epochs

The final epoch — that of the Hellenistic and Roman worlds —
inherited these classical doctrines and incorporated them into its
own philosophical perspectives. Many historians have noted that this
epoch produces relatively little in the way of original systematic
philosophical speculation. What the late Hellenistic and Roman
thinkers did instead was draw on classical philosophy in a syncretic
fashion, weaving together as many different strands as possible to
create syntheses that would help them navigate their cultural worlds.

The Stoics and Epicureans, for example, tried to give meaning and
direction to ethical and spiritual life without recourse to traditional
mythologies. Both attempted to provide rational models of the good
life — ones that did not depend on religious beliefs. The Stoic ideal
of a life lived according to nature, drawing on Socratic teachings,

projected a rational order on the cosmos and demanded of its
adherents both reverential piety and the self-discipline of a life lived
by the dictates of reason. The Epicureans, by contrast, sought the
good life in the Aristotelian pursuit of cultivated pleasures — not, it
must be stressed, the pursuit of crude carnal hedonism, but rather
pleasures of the mind, pleasures that produce __ intellectual
satisfaction. A very Aristotelian doctrine, in fact.

Similarly, thinkers like Cicero and Polybius practiced a philosophical
eclecticism, selectively drawing on elements of different philosophies
to construct their own unique worldviews. Cicero, for example,
combined Socratic and Stoic conceptions of virtue and duty with
Aristotelian political philosophy — that practical, realistic analysis of
existing regimes — to produce a political credo for late Republican
Rome.

Skepticism emerged in the Hellenistic and Roman period as a result
of the profusion of metaphysical and philosophical doctrines that
had accumulated by that time. Skepticism led to a return to the
quest for merely practical certainty, as we see in figures like Sextus
Empiricus. But it also led to an openness to extra-rational sources of
cosmology and metaphysics — to an openness to more traditional,
perhaps more _ faith-based and emotionally grounded
understandings of the world, and to participation in meaningful
Spiritual existence. This is reflected in the epoch by the rise of
mystery cults, of which Mithraism was one of the most famous,
though ultimately the most successful, of course, was Christianity.

In a sense, the rise of skepticism brings the trajectory of ancient
philosophy full circle. Ancient philosophy began with a kind of
skepticism: the Milesian lonians engaged in philosophical disputation
and the construction of a science of nature partly because they had
been subjected to a variety of different religious interpretations of
the world, which led them to be skeptical of those religious

interpretations. The subsequent profusion of metaphysical and
rational philosophical doctrines produced, in turn, a skepticism about
rational disputation itself. And what we find is that the skepticism of
classical philosophies — which were themselves intended to resolve
the first, religiously-induced skepticism — gives way to a new age of
faith: the Christian epoch.

Lecture 2: The Pre-Socratics — Physics and
Metaphysics

Professor Louis Markos

Introduction: The Birth of Philosophy

In this lecture, we shall witness the exciting birth of philosophy in the
speculations and systems of the pre-Socratics. We shall explore how
these philosophical forerunners shifted the focus of learned thought
from religious questions of who and why to scientific questions of
what and how, and, by so doing, kicked off an ongoing dialogue that
still continues 2,500 years later.

Now, we call them the pre-Socratics for two reasons. The first is
chronological: they precede Socrates. But the second, more
important reason is that the pre-Socratics — and there are about a
dozen of them — laid down a philosophical framework that Socrates,
and later Plato and Aristotle, both developed and countered.

When did the pre-Socratics live? They cover a span of about 150
years, from roughly 600 BC to about 450 BC. Where did they live?
Unlike Socrates, who lived in Athens, the pre-Socratics hailed first
from the lonian, or Asia Minor, coast — what is today the western
coast of Turkey — and some of the later pre-Socratics came from the
eastern coast of Italy and the island of Sicily. This geographical
aspect is vital, | believe, because it places the pre-Socratics along
major trade routes and thus exposed them to a plethora of foreign
ideas and cults. This was particularly true of the founders of pre-
Socratic thought: Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes, all of whom
lived in the lonian city of Miletus, and who are therefore called the
Milesians. Perhaps growing up in the bustling markets of Miletus,
where every conceivable religious system was advocated, inspired

these three men to come up with ideas and answers that were more
pluralistic and less tied to cultural norms. They lived in a place fertile
with new ideas, and | think that inspired them to move beyond
tradition and to explore genuinely new possibilities.

From Who and Why to What and How

To guide their new search, Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes
formulated a new set of questions. Prior to the pre-Socratics, in the
eighth and seventh centuries BC, Greek poets like Hesiod — who
lived around 700 BC — attempted to account for the present state of
man and nature by tracing the origins and actions of the gods. In his
great work the Theogony, which means the birth or origin of the
gods, Hesiod presents himself as called by the Muses to explain two
things: first, why things are as they are, and second, by what divine
agency — or who — these things came into being. In other words,
Hesiod is interested in religious questions and supernatural answers.

Along came the Milesians — Thales, Anaximander, and Anaximenes
— and they rejected Hesiod's supernatural religious orientation, with
its questions of why and who, in favor of a more naturalistic, scientific
approach. The Greek word for nature is physis, and we therefore call
these men physisists, or physicists, because they were interested in
natural, physical, material explanations. Their interest was not in who
created the world, but in what the universe was made of. Not in
supernatural religious answers, but in material, physical questions —
not who, but what. Likewise, they were interested not in why, but in
how things come into being and pass away. They were not looking
for philosophical or religious purpose; they were looking for material,
physical processes.

At first, you might think | am saying that the pre-Socratics were anti-
religious or atheistic. Not necessarily. It is not so much that they were

anti-religious as that the gods played no significant role in their
materialistic systems. The Bible begins with the words in the
beginning, God. For the pre-Socratics, by contrast, the origin of all life
is not God, but stuff, or matter — which means that even if the gods
do exist, the gods, too, are products of this stuff, this primal matter.
In this sense, one might argue that the pre-Socratics were the true
founders of the modern spirit of evolutionary inquiry: they were
looking for physical, material answers to the questions of what we
are and what this earth is made of, and they were doing this 2,500
years ago.

The Four Elements and Qualitative Pairs

All the pre-Socratics shared a common belief that the material
building blocks of life are composed of and expressed through four
elements — earth, air, water, and fire. Today we know that none of
those are elements in the modern chemical sense, but the Greeks
considered them the four fundamental elements, and they believed
that everything in the universe is composed of some combination of
these four.

In addition to the four elements, the Greeks posited a series of
qualitative pairs — pairs of opposing qualities — including hot and
cold, dry and moist, rare and dense, and light and dark, among many
others. They believed not only that everything was formed from
these elements, but that everything could be described as either hot
or cold, dry or moist. To give a feel for how the pre-Socratics
imagined the world, consider how they mixed together the elements
with their qualitative pairs. They conceived of the universe as
composed of four levels: the bottom-most level of earth, which is
cold and dry; above that a layer of water, which is cold and moist;
then a layer of air, which is moist and hot; and finally, the uppermost

level of fire, which is hot and dry. That picture gives a useful sense of
how the pre-Socratics understood the structure of the world when
they turned their scientific minds toward it.

The Milesians

Both the Milesians and Pythagoras sought to posit a first principle —
a single originary substance that could account for the cosmos. The
Greek word for this first principle is arché — as in archaeology,
meaning beginning or origin. Many of the early pre-Socratics wanted
to find a single physical origin, a first principle that could be the
beginning of everything.

Thales

The first person to do so was Thales, and for that reason any book on
the history of Western science or Western philosophy will always
begin with him. It is worth noting that for the pre-Socratics, science,
philosophy, and religion are essentially the same thing; it is not until
Aristotle that we begin separating those three domains.

Thales was a keen observer of the natural world. He accurately
foretold a solar eclipse in 585 BC, he measured the Egyptian
pyramids, and he cornered the market on olive presses by predicting
a large olive crop. An astonishing man, he was considered one of the
Seven Sages of the ancient world. Thales was not entirely original —
many of the measuring techniques he employed he learned from the
Egyptians, either through direct contact or through those who knew
them. But what makes Thales, rather than the Egyptians, the founder
of science and philosophy is that he took ideas which in Egypt were
purely practical and pragmatic and made them abstract and
scientific. He derived principles rather than merely practical
techniques.

The best illustration of this distinction is Pythagoras and the famous
theorem that bears his name. Pythagoras noticed that Egyptian
builders knew that if they had three strings measuring three, four,
and five units in length and arranged them into a triangle, they
would obtain an exact right angle of ninety degrees. What
Pythagoras did was to take that practical observation and turn it into
a universal theorem: in any right triangle, regardless of size, whether
on earth or in the heavens, the square of the hypotenuse will always
equal the sum of the squares of the other two sides. The move from
practical technique to universal philosophical and scientific theorem
is precisely what Thales accomplished for the study of nature.

Turning his analytical mind toward the nature of the universe, Thales
proposed that the first principle, the arché, of all things is water. You
might think, what is the great insight in that? But the reason Thales is
the founder of science and philosophy in the Western world is not
because he chose water. The real reason we consider him the
founder is that, in making that choice, Thales asserted two things:
first, that there ts an origin of all things, and second, that that origin
can be determined through observation. In other words, Thales
established a scientific method — an approach for analyzing and
discussing the world. He is the founder not so much because of his
answer, but because of his method.

Anaximander

The next Milesian, Anaximander, was a pupil of Thales. Anaximander
advanced his master's position by pushing the first principle further
back — not to water, but to something deeper and more primal than
any of the four elements. He realized that the arché cannot be one of
the four elements, because if it were, it would end up canceling out
the opposing elements. If water — which is cold and moist — is the
first principle, it will suppress everything that is dry and hot. So the

first principle cannot be an element, or it will dominate and distort
everything.

Instead, Anaximander proposed that the first principle must be
something earlier and more fundamental — something amorphous.
This he called the unlimited or the boundless, which we might
describe as a kind of primordial cosmic soup out of which the four
elements and all of nature gradually separated. In this way,
Anaximander moves considerably closer to what we now think of as
evolutionary reasoning.

Anaximenes

The third Milesian, Anaximenes, might at first seem to take a step
backward, since he argued that the first principle was air — which Is,
after all, one of the four elements. But | think what is happening here
is that Anaximenes was no longer primarily interested in the what
question; he was interested in the how question. His real contribution
to pre-Socratic philosophy was to offer an explanation not for what
the first principle is, but for how all the elements could be generated
out of it.

Whatever the first principle may be, we need to answer the question:
how can one thing become many things? How can a first principle
separate out into the four elements and generate all of matter?
Anaximenes' answer begins with air, and he proposed two opposing
processes. Through rarefaction — a thinning and expanding process
— air transforms itself upward into fire. Through the opposing
process of condensation, air transforms itself downward into wind,
then cloud, then water, then earth, and finally stone. By one of these
two processes, a single element could literally become all the others.

With hindsight, we might note that Anaximenes would have done
better to start with water, which already exists in three states — ice,
liquid water, and steam — and as three physical phases: solid, liquid,

and gas. It would have been more intuitive to explain rarefaction and
condensation using water. But he used air. Hindsight, as always, is
everything.

Pythagoras

Pythagoras is a name all of us recognize, and he too was a pre-
Socratic, also interested in the first principle. But Pythagoras offered
a more elaborate, quasi-religious solution to the problem of origin,
proposing that the real source of everything is numbers. As he
expressed it: all is number.

Many people know that Pythagoras was among the first to identify a
direct relationship between numerical ratios and musical harmonies
— he found that certain mathematical ratios produce certain chords,
octaves, fifths, and thirds, and he was not only a mathematician but a
musician. But what many people do not realize is that Pythagoras did
not stop there. He reasoned that just as numbers underlie music, so
they are the very stuff and pattern of the universe — that beneath
everything in the cosmos there is some kind of numerical ratio.

Pythagoras’ faith that the entire natural world must display numerical
order was so strong that he would sometimes indulge in great leaps
of logic to accommodate it. When he observed the heavens, for
example, he counted nine heavenly spheres. But that was
unsatisfactory, because Pythagoras and his followers considered the
number ten to be a mystical, almost magical number — they would
arrange it like bowling pins and treat it as sacred. So Pythagoras very
unscientifically posited a tenth sphere, which he called the counter-
earth. You cannot see it, but it must be there, because the magic
number demands ten.

Pythagoras was also a religious leader, and the path from
mathematics to religion runs through his doctrine of the music of the

spheres. He believed that as those ten planets revolved in the
cosmos, they rubbed against one another and produced a beautiful
celestial music. Our physical, earthly ears cannot hear that music,
however — the only way to perceive it is to purify the body, mind,
and soul. And so Pythagoras founded a religious cult, and young
men gathered around him to learn and practice spiritual disciplines
that would so purify them as to allow them to hear that celestial
harmony.

Pythagoras further believed in reincarnation. He held that the soul
was immortal, and that until it achieved an inner harmony or
attunement to the greater harmonies of the cosmos, it would be
doomed to return to earth in a series of incarnations — coming back
again and again until it reached a state of perfection. For Pythagoras,
that perfection was a kind of mathematical harmony: when my soul is
attuned to the soul of the cosmos, when | not only hear the music of
the spheres but enter into it, | am at last ready to leave behind the
earth and all its physical matter and ascend to the heavens.

Pythagoras’ system combines philosophy, religion, and science, and
he has therefore had a profound influence across all three fields. He
paved the way for Plato's theories of the transmigration of the soul;
the medieval obsession with numbers — seen throughout Dante's
Divine Comedy, for instance — goes back to him; and Galileo's
celebrated claim that mathematics is the language of the universe is
pure Pythagorean thinking.

Heraclitus and the Eleatics: Change Versus
Permanence

So far we have seen how the Milesians and Pythagoras answered the
questions of what and how. But the later pre-Socratics confronted
another related question: is the true nature of reality singular or

plural? Fixed or changing? | want to close with one pre-Socratic,
Heraclitus, who believed it was plural and changing, and then two
pre-Socratics — Parmenides and Zeno, both of whom came from
Elea in Italy, and are therefore known as the Eleatics — who believed
that the nature of reality was singular and fixed.

Heraclitus

Heraclitus possessed one of the richest, most enigmatic intellects of
the ancient world. All of his speculations were recorded in cryptic
aphorisms that have influenced such thinkers as Blake, Nietzsche,
and T.S. Eliot. Still, his basic theory is clear and is best expressed in
his well-known saying: you can never step in the same river twice. In
other words, Heraclitus believed that all of nature is in constant flux.
Every time you go to a river, it is a new river, because It is continually
changing. According to Heraclitus, the only constant in the universe
is change itself.

He believed that a perpetual yet creative strife existed between the
elements and their qualitative pairs — a universal dance in which one
element becomes another and one pair becomes another. It is a
wonderful, beautiful image of ceaseless transformation. Heraclitus
often used the twin images of the bow and the lyre to express how
harmony can arise out of opposing, seemingly destructive forces.
Think about the way a bow bends: the bend should snap it, but
instead it gives the string the proper tension to send the arrow flying.
Likewise, the bend of the lyre allows it to produce its beautiful
harmony. So everything changes, and stability is in fact founded
upon unending change.

Parmenides and the Eleatics

Most of us find Heraclitus’ vision intuitive enough — of course things
are always changing. Let us look, then, at the Eleatics, beginning with

Parmenides, who argued the exact opposite: that the nature of reality
is one and unchanging.

In what may be a parody of Hesiod, Parmenides presents himself in
his work as called by the goddess of truth to discern between true
knowledge and mere opinion. True knowledge, he argued, rests on
nature (physis) and is apprehended by speculative reason. Opinion,
by contrast, rests on custom (nomos) and is perceived through the
senses. Nature is fixed and unchanging, Parmenides held, and our
reason apprehends that fixity and eternality. Custom, on the other
hand, is always changing, and our senses are deceived by that
change.

While opinion tells us that things change and move, reason dictates
that this is an illusion. Parmenides argued two things. First, that
being is perfect and complete and therefore cannot change — being
is in a perfect state, and any change would diminish it. Second, that
non-being does not exist: there is no such thing as nothing, no such
thing as the void. And if there is no empty space in the universe,
there is nowhere for being to move. He believed the universe
consisted of a single, eternal, perfect, and complete mass — a system
known as monism. Heraclitus, by contrast, believed in pluralism: that
there are many things, and that those things change ceaselessly and
eternally.

As for why being must be eternal, Parmenides reasoned that if being
came into existence at some point, it could only have arisen out of
non-being — and non-being does not exist. Nothing can come from
nothing. If being exists, it must always have existed: perfect,
unchanging, eternal.

Counterintuitive as this position is, Parmenides' contemporaries did
not merely find it puzzling — they ridiculed it. Can't you see that
there are many things, and that they change? Parmenides had a

brilliant pupil named Zeno who rose to defend his master's monism
— and he did so in a unique and fascinating way. Zeno defended
monism not by arguing for it directly, but by demonstrating in a
series of paradoxes that a belief in change and pluralism, when taken
to its logical endpoint, yields results that are even more absurd. This
method of attacking a philosophical position by exposing its latent
absurdities is known as reductio ad absurdum, and it remains a
standard tool of philosophical argument to this day.

In his best-known paradox, Zeno exposes the self-contradictory
nature of movement by proving that Achilles, though twice as swift
as his opponent, could never catch an opponent given even a ten-
foot head start. When the race begins, Achilles runs the ten feet —
but by the time he arrives, his opponent has moved five feet ahead.
Achilles runs those five feet — but now his opponent is two and a
half feet ahead. And so on, infinitely. Reductio ad absurdum: if you
believe in change and movement, you are forced to the ridiculous
conclusion that Achilles can never catch his opponent, even though
he is twice as fast. Many have poked holes in this paradox, and
rightly so, but it was enormously influential, and the method of
reductio ad absurdum that Zeno pioneered is still wielded regularly in
philosophy today.

Resolution: Plato's Two Worlds

The philosophical impasse between Heraclitus the pluralist and the
Eleatics the monists sent shockwaves through Western philosophy.
The remaining  pre-Socratics — Empedocles, Anaxagoras,
Democritus, and Leucippus — all set as their goal the reconciliation
of change and continuity, of pluralism and monism. There is
unfortunately no time to examine each of them here, but if you read
them, look for that unifying project.

Let me close instead with Plato, because Plato, | believe, arrived at
the fullest and most satisfying answer to the pre-Socratic riddle.
Plato said that the reason we have both monism and pluralism is that
we actually have two worlds. Up in the realm beyond the senses is
the world of being, eternal and perfect and unchanging — the world
that monism correctly describes, and that can only be apprehended
by the speculative reason. Our world, by contrast, is the world of
becoming: it is pluralistic, it changes, it decays, and it dies, and it is
perceived through the senses. Plato believed that our senses mislead
us, and that the only true knowledge is knowledge of the world of
being. In this way, Plato caps off the pre-Socratic tradition by
offering a sweeping resolution to the great tension between
permanence and change that had driven pre-Socratic thought from
its very beginning.

Lecture 3: The Sophists and Social Science
Professor Jeremy Adams

Introduction: Reassessing the Sophists

Sophistry is generally considered, in modern vernacular English, a
bad thing. To be a sophist is not good; sophism is a defective form of
thought. All of these terms — sophistry, sophism, sophist itself —
designate, for most people who think about philosophical issues, the
wrong way to go. This is, | think, very unfortunate, historically
speaking. The sophists, who gave their name to this whole family of
words, were important, serious thinkers of the fifth and fourth
centuries BC who were given a very bad name by Plato, because his
mentor Socrates considered them intellectually immoral — and
sometimes immoral in other ways as well. This insult survives in our
current English usage, but it is really a misnomer. The sophists were
professional teachers who tried to meet the educational needs of
citizens of the Greek city-states of their time.

And their time was a moment of social change and of economic and
cultural expansion. Athens was the center of the economic life of the
Eastern Mediterranean. Athens itself, with perhaps 100,000 people,
was the largest city in Europe. Athens had recently struggled with the
greatest empire in the world, the Persian Empire, and against all odds
had defeated it and brought it to a very advantageous peace. And
for that reason, as well as others, Athens had become the center of
philosophical thought in the Greek-speaking world.

Many of the sophists came from many different city-states, but most
of them gravitated to Athens for at least part of their adult careers —
certainly the ones we are going to talk about today. These sophists
are in many ways the forerunners of the modern professional

intellectual, whether scientist or humanist. And obviously the
forerunners of such a type must have something good about them,
since the modern professional academic intellectual is supposed to
be a model of how to think and talk and write — or so we like to
think.

The Sophists and the lonian Legacy

One of the most important things about sophist thought is its
connection with the previous legacy of lonian thought, particularly
lonian science. You have already been hearing about the wise men of
lonia — Thales and others — and their broad speculations on all
kinds of topics, which began in the cities of the eastern Aegean, cities
like Miletus and Ephesus. Heraclitus, for example, came from the
royal family of Ephesus and was for a time its king. But after the
Persians took over the eastern shore of the Aegean Sea — which is
of course now western Turkey — many of the thinkers of those city-
States fled across the Aegean to Athens. Athens was the mother city
of many Aegean colonies; it was, for example, the mother of Miletus,
the home of Thales, the first of the seven wise men, and a center of
their general style of thought.

One aspect of lonian scientific teaching was the conviction that the
ethical laws of society — the nomoi, deeply revered by traditional
Greek religion and lawgivers — had some basis in natural science
and inhered, in some sense, in the nature of the world. The sophists
partly disagreed. To the sophists, most if not all of the myth-based
laws that men _ revered were artificial, merely conventional
agreements. Some laws about human behavior, the sophists felt, did
inhere in nature, in physis, the physical world — but once rationally
analyzed, they turned out to be rather shocking. | will return to this
point when discussing Thucydides’ account of the dialogue between

Athenian officers and the representatives of the oligarchic city of
Melos.

A further instance of the sophists' departure from lonian thought
was their abandonment of the objective analysis of nature in favor of
the subjective analysis of the human being and of human society.
The external world that had fascinated the seven wise men and later
lonian thinkers gave way to the subject — to the human being, to
man, to use an unfortunately sexist term that was entirely the norm
in the extremely sexist society of ancient Greece. Man was then
considered the measure of all things, the center of the cosmos in
every possible intellectual sense.

Now, Thales — born around 615 BC and who, by tradition, died in
546 BC, when the Persians took over the city of Sardis, the key to the
lonian coast — decided that the primal element of the world was
water: water available in so many forms, solid, liquid, and gaseous,
and evidently necessary to life. Thales then went on to explain many
things by reference to this first principle. The method of the wise
men was, of course, deductive: they would begin with a general
premise derived through an insight, formulate it carefully, and then
deduce from it all sorts of further conclusions.

Thales’ pupil Anaximander carried on this tradition. He disagreed
with his teacher — as any truly good pupil should — and decided
that the first principle could not be water. It had to be something
even more fundamental. Because it was so fundamental, it was in a
sense invisible to the human being, not directly accessible to us, and
yet one could discern this primal matter somehow everywhere.
Anaximenes, the pupil of Anaximander, thought the primal element
must be air. And so the speculation continued. Heraclitus was
convinced that everything was constantly in motion, constantly in
flux, nothing stable. Other lonian scientists thought exactly the

contrary and would develop their theories of the universe
accordingly.

Four Key Sophists

By contrast, the sophists of the fifth and fourth centuries BC
preferred to speculate analytically about the inner motives of
individual and group human behavior. Some examples of sophist
analysis that radically challenged traditional opinion are the
teachings of Gorgias, Protagoras, Callicles, and Thrasymachus.

Gorgias

Gorgias was not from the lonian coast. He came from another zone
of heavy Greek colonization — the island of Sicily, from the polis of
Leontini, to be exact. He spent much of his adult life in Athens,
however, and while there he wrote a work that can be translated as
On Non-Betng and Nature. In this deliberately provocative book,
which he clearly meant to be as outrageous as possible, he argued —
and seemed to his readers to demonstrate logically — that nothing
really exists. Whether Gorgias truly held this doctrine, whether we
ought to consider him a nihilist in the formal sense, or whether he
was simply using the argument to expose the absurdity of logical
demonstration, is debatable. He subsequently abandoned
philosophy to teach rhetoric, the art of persuasion. Socrates meets
him in Plato's dialogue the Gorgias as a professor of rhetoric who
would teach the young men of Athens how to be effective in their
political campaigns and, by extension, in their diplomatic and
commercial dealings with people throughout the wider world.

Gorgias argued, among other things, that it was much better to give
oneself fully to certain emotions because those emotions were
fundamentally valid. The dramatic theater was a matter of great
concern to Socrates, who disapproved of it very strongly — he

disapproved of the violent emotions it unleashed. Gorgias said, on
the contrary, that it was far better to be genuinely moved by the
emotions of tragic drama, to be profoundly affected, for example, by
the drama of Agamemnon or of Hecuba, even if one had to cultivate
that response a little, than to show that one had no literary sensitivity
at all. That is exactly the kind of posturing — as Socrates would have
put it — that so outraged Socrates and gave Plato the firm
conviction that sophists like Gorgias represented the worst
tendencies of human thought.

Protagoras

More serious, and in some respects almost a friend of Socrates, was
Protagoras. His dates are very uncertain: some think he was born
around 500 BC, others around 481, and the one surviving tradition is
that he died near the end of the Peloponnesian War, probably
around 411. That gives us a general picture of a man who spanned
much of that crucial fifth century, in which Athens rose to
extraordinary world power by defeating the Persians against all odds,
and then fell by losing a generation-long war with her former ally
Sparta — the great and terrible Peloponnesian War.

Protagoras can be considered the founder of the modern sciences of
linguistics and social science. People are always trying to find a
founder of social science. Thucydides, the great historian, is
sometimes called the founder of social science; so is Montesquieu,
the great eighteenth-century thinker whose division of power into
executive, legislative, and judicial branches was so important to the
founders of our own republic; and so is Machiavelli. Why not go back
to classical antiquity and choose Protagoras? We do not, of course,
have many of his writings, or of any of the sophists' writings. Most of
what we know about them is reported by a major enemy — Plato —
or by more sympathetic writers with whom we will deal later in this
course.

Protagoras was an atheist, though he denied it in public for obvious
political reasons. When challenged concerning his belief in the gods,
he replied that he knew nothing about them and preferred to study
and talk about things he did know something about — namely, the
laws and customs of men, and the language men used to convey
them — rather than the mythic language men used to convey the
stories of the gods, which for most of Greek society formed the basis
of all morality and all statute law. Protagoras especially enjoyed
contrasting law and nature — possibly the most significant sophist
departure from lonian thought and from the general consensus of
respectable opinion in Greece of their time. In pursuing this contrast,
he was led to an ever-sharper sense of the absurdity of most human
traditions and most human beliefs. Socrates reacted sharply against
him, and we have the memory of this in various of Plato's dialogues.

Callicles

Callicles, of whom little is known, argued, according to Plato, that
might is right. It is natural for human beings to be driven by the will
to power — that will to power which would prove so important for
nihilists and realpolitik leaders of European states in the nineteenth
and twentieth centuries, usually the ones we have fought rather than
ourselves, though we have sometimes been accused of it by our own
enemies. Callicles elevated the will to power to a position of high
moral standing precisely because of its naturalness: it expressed, in
his view, the genuine law of nature.

Thrasymachus

All of this is perhaps most bluntly summed up in the figure of
Thrasymachus. Thrasymachus is a character in Plato's dialogue the
Republic, but he was also an actual sophist. He was not himself
Athenian; he came from an island to the east and traveled to Athens

to teach, where he gathered a considerable following among the
young.

According to the Republic, he was a guest at the house of Cephalus,
a kindly and wealthy old merchant in the city of Piraeus, the port of
Athens, whose son invited Socrates, along with two brothers of Plato,
to spend the night, share a banquet, and talk. This banquet, lasting
until dawn, was an extraordinary intellectual occasion — a prolonged
and wide-ranging discussion that produced an extraordinarily long
book, if you believe that Plato was essentially writing up a banquet
that actually took place. Even if he was not, it is a terrific story,
describing an extraordinary kind of intellectual feast.

Socrates debates with various people in the course of this dialogue
about the nature of justice, which is the central issue throughout. The
first of his rivals in debate is Thrasymachus, and as they move toward
the question of justice, Thrasymachus becomes extremely impatient.
He is rude, rough, tough, and confrontational. He declares that what
men call justice is simply the will of the strongest — the strongest
man, the strongest state, the strongest party within a state at any
given point. Whoever wins calls what he has done justice, and that is
all there is to it. Get rid of your illusions.

Socrates dismantles him early in the Republic, not by an appeal to
basic human feeling or to the ancient myths on which the
community is founded, but to logic — by means of an extremely
complicated logical argument which does not convince every
modern reader. It is clear, though, that the kind of thinking
represented most bluntly and crudely by Thrasymachus, with greater
elegance by Gorgias, with greater restraint by Protagoras, and with
comparable bluntness by Callicles, was deeply corrosive to the
patriotic and moral myths that sustained the social cohesion of the
Greek city-state.

The Athenians, for example, required strong belief in the myths of
the goddess Athena and the god Poseidon, and a whole body of
other myths surrounding the founding of their institutions. The
fundamental constitution of Athens was grounded in stories of the
terms the gods had struck with the men who settled under their
protection, and it was traditional for Athenian politicians to invoke
these sacred stories. Once those stories are dismissed as nonsense —
and not only that, but when contrary moral principles are adduced as
the scientific truth — then, at least among the educated citizen class,
the stability of the state is in serious doubt.

This helps explain why Socrates reacted so strongly in favor of a
logical argument for morality. Morality must be established as
something that inheres in nature — not the physical nature the
lonians had been investigating, but the physis of the spiritual world,
the spiritual nature which Socrates and Plato saw as the primary
reality.

The Invention of History

The sophists coincided with more than just the outrage of Socrates.
They coincided with what some call the invention of history. Some
say the Greeks invented history — that is the traditional claim of
Western learning. Others, protesting against what they see as an
excessively ethnocentric Western view, point out that all peoples
have had their histories, and one can certainly find in the ancient
Near East very strong evidence of historical writing among the
Sumerians, the Babylonians, the Assyrians, and supremely among the
Jews, whose historical books of the Hebrew Scriptures are very much
like what we have come to call history in the West — particularly in
the Middle Ages, when Christianity provided a fundamental, if you

want, mythic basis for reality, and the will of God became the primary
motive force in historical writing.

But the notion that the Greeks invented a new kind of history is, |
think, perfectly defensible. The kind of history the Greeks invented
attempted to be analytical about causes — to go beyond the will of
God or the moral qualities of individual important actors, individual
kings and queens and peoples — toward something more systematic
and rational.

Herodotus

The first historian in this tradition is Herodotus, born in 484 BC and
died in 428 BC. He was born in Halicarnassus, another lonian polis in
the eastern Aegean. He moved to Athens for a time, and then to the
polis of Thurioi in Italy, where he eventually died after a tranquil old
age. He wrote a great work called simply Historiae — meaning
"researches." Historiae tended to be researches about geography or
about what we would call politics, and no one had previously
assembled a body of researches anything like the work of Herodotus.

What Herodotus was trying to figure out was the cause of the Greek
victory in the Persian War, which simply did not make any sense in
terms of resources — given not only the vast resources of the Persian
Empire, but also the endless tendency of the Greek city-states to
quarrel among themselves and to divide within themselves. The
great fact was that Athens and Sparta got together. They became
allies against the Persians, and the Spartan army and the Athenian
navy together defeated the world's greatest military power. How was
this possible?

Herodotus goes to work in properly sophist fashion, gathering facts.
What are the peoples of the world? What kind of peoples do the
Persians rule? What sort of people are the Greeks? He then moves
into a kind of scientific psychology — an analysis of the

psychological motivations of the rulers of Persia and of the Greek
world. It is a very broadly speculative work that opens up studies we
today call anthropology as well as political science. | do not know
why we do not call Herodotus the founder of political science; |
suppose he is already called the founder of history, and that one title
is thought to be enough for any ancient Greek.

Thucydides

His work is carried on by Thucydides, whose long book on the
Peloponnesian War is obviously conscious of Herodotus’ work,
though it alludes to it only in very indirect ways. Thucydides’ account
of history is much more precise. It has less to do with mythic stories
— though Herodotus does present such stories as possible causes,
he tends to undercut them at pretty much every turn — and instead
closely analyzes the motivations of the leaders of Athens and Sparta
and their allied states, trying to explain the decisions and accidents
that seemed to him to have turned the tide of history in the
remarkable generation-long conflict that brought his world-
dominant city-state down to defeat and profound humiliation.

It is hard to single out any central or final message of this
masterpiece of historical writing. One can say that Herodotus settles
for a kind of science of psychology, but one cannot focus on any
single theme like that for Thucydides’ Peloponnesian War. It is very
clear, however, that Thucydides thought human behavior was very
complex and very hard to predict — but fascinating — and that its
analysis was the most compelling exercise of human reason, more
compelling than the study of the kinds of topics that Protagoras,
Gorgias, Socrates, or Plato wrote about. Was this a utilitarian science?
Probably so. We get the impression that Thucydides thought
understanding the mistakes of the past was a good way to develop a
sense of how to avoid them in the future. But he does not make this

a formal principle; he presents his material analytically, in his own
terms, and lets the reader draw his own conclusions.

The Melian Dialogue

One of the most strikingly dramatic moments of the Peloponnesian
War, and one in which Thucydides shows his sophist legacy most
clearly, is his account of a debate between the officers of an Athenian
invasion force and the leading citizens of the oligarchic polis of
Melos — a small and strategically insignificant island in the Aegean
Sea in the sixteenth year of the war. This was militarily a very minor
event, but it exemplified for the author much of Athenian behavior,
and it serves as a prime example of sophistic political reasoning.

What happened is this. The Athenian navy arrives off the coast of
Melos. Melos has remained neutral throughout the war. It is a polis
founded by Dorians, and the Melians therefore consider themselves
kinsmen of the Spartans. Accordingly, Melos had not joined the
other city-states of lonian origin that had naturally affiliated with
Athens — first against the Persians, and then in the confederate
empire the Athenians subsequently established. The Athenians had
incorporated other non-lonian city-states into their empire as well,
and they wanted this to become a general principle: moving away
from ethnic and linguistic connection as the basis of their
confederation, they wanted any city-state within their strategic zone
to be part of the Athenian alliance.

They sent against Melos a formidable expeditionary force — thirty of
their own ships, six from the allied polis of Chios, and two from the
allied polis of Lesbos, for a total of thirty-eight ships, carrying 1,200
hoplites, or heavily armed infantrymen, 300 archers, and 20 mounted
archers — all Athenians — plus roughly 1,500 additional hoplites

from various allies. This was a major expeditionary force by the scale
of warfare the Greeks knew at that time.

The Athenian generals — Cleomedes and Tisias, the strategoi —
camped on the territory of Melos and called for a parley. What they
had hoped for was to present the Athenian case to the assembled
citizens of Melos and persuade them that resisting alliance was
simply foolish and would do them no good, whereas alliance with
Athens would mean paying the tribute tax but would secure
Athenian protection in return. The Athenians were accustomed to
making offers one could not refuse, and when people refused them,
they became extremely unhappy — which was precisely the case
here.

Now, Melos, being a Dorian state founded on the Spartan model,
was an oligarchic state; it did not believe in democracy. The oligarchs
who ran things refused to allow the Athenian generals to address the
whole mass of the people. Instead, let the generals deal directly with
them. The Athenians agreed, and the argument proceeded across
what must have been a long and historically decisive day —
historically decisive for the Melians, at any rate.

The exchange reaches a crucial turning point, or rather a crucial
summary, about two-thirds of the way through. The Melians say:

"It ts difficult, as you may be sure we know, for us to oppose your
power and fortune unless the terms be equal. Nevertheless, we trust
that the gods will give us fortune as good as yours, because we are
standing for what ts right against what is wrong. And as for what we
lack in power, we trust that it will be made up for by our alliance with
the Spartans, who are bound — if for no other reason than honor's
sake and because we are their kinsmen — to come to our help. Our
confidence, therefore, ts not so entirely irrational as you think."

The fundamental claim of the Melians at this point is clearly that
honor exists and that kinship exists. They assume that honor inheres
in the moral nature of the universe, and they appeal to traditional
values handed down through the ages, as well as to the founding
myths of their own community — myths stretching back, they
claimed, some 700 years to the twelfth century BC. Many city-states
did maintain long records of kings, councils, and the genealogies of
leading families, so there was a certain amount of genuine research
behind these mythic statements, even if considerable creativity
entered into those genealogies as well.

The Athenian generals answered as follows. Now, of course,
Thucydides was not present. We do not know whether this was
Cleomedes or Tisias speaking. Thucydides populated his history with
appropriate soeeches — speeches of the sort that should have been
made. Whether he had reliable reports from people who were there,
which is entirely possible, or whether he composed the speeches
himself, ultimately does not matter: what this dialogue shows, as do
all the famous speeches of the Peloponnesian War — such as the
Funeral Speech of Pericles — is what Thucydides thinks about the
motive forces of human behavior.

The Athenians say:

"So far as the favor of the gods is concerned, we think we have as
much right to that as you have. Our aims and our actions are perfectly
consistent with the beliefs that men hold about the gods and with the
principles which govern their own conduct. Our opinion of the gods
and our knowledge of men lead us to conclude that it is a general and
necessary law of nature to rule whatever one can. This ts not a law
that we made ourselves, nor were we the first to act upon it when it
was made. We found it already in existence, and we shall leave it to
exist forever among those who come after us. We are merely acting in
accordance with it, and we know that you or anybody else with the

same power as ours would be acting tn precisely the same way.
Therefore, so far as the gods are concerned, we see no good reason
why we should fear to be at a disadvantage."

Note the telling phrase: our opinion of the gods and our knowledge of
men. That distinction between mere opinion about divine matters
and genuine knowledge of human affairs is precisely the kind of
sophistic distinction between what can be known and what belongs
to the realm of myth that Plato will also make — though to entirely
opposite ends — in the Republic. Whether this distinction slipped out
spontaneously in the course of the actual debate, or emerged from
Thucydides' own sophistic sensibility as he wrote, the message is
perfectly powerful either way.

As for Spartan honor, the Athenians continue:

"We must say that we congratulate you on your simplicity, but do not
envy you your folly. In matters that concern themselves or their own
constitution, the Spartans are quite remarkably good. As for their
relations with others, tt can be expressed shortly and clearly by saying
that of all people we know, the Spartans are most conspicuous for
believing that what they like doing ts honorable and what suits their
interests is just."

This is the argument of Thrasymachus: justice is whatever serves the
interests of the stronger. And this kind of attitude, the Athenians
add, is not going to be of much help to the Melians in their quest for
safety.

The Melians, immovable, hold to their beliefs and their hopes for
Spartan assistance. The Athenians therefore isolate the town of
Melos with a wall on the landward side, blockade the harbor, and a
long siege of attrition follows. Eventually the Melians launch a sortie
against the Athenian lines and inflict casualties, and the Athenians
respond in force. The Melians surrender before the walls are actually

taken by storm. All men of military age are killed. All women and
children are enslaved. Melos, in effect, ceases to exist, though it was
resettled later by some survivors, according to one account.

One has to wonder how many of those Athenian officers had been
pupils of Callicles or of Thrasymachus. And what did Thucydides
himself think? It seems perfectly clear that the sophistic distinction
between nature and conventional law — grounded in nothing other
than a strictly rational analysis of human behavior — is operative
right here, in that central and chilling statement about how not just
Athenians, but all men, behave.

Lecture 4: Plato — Metaphysics
Professor Philip Carey

Introduction: Plato and the Western Tradition

Plato is our great mind for this lecture, and it is hard to imagine a
greater one, especially in terms of his influence on the Western
intellectual tradition. Plato is a fourth-century Athenian philosopher
and a student of Socrates, who is also the main character in Plato's
writings, as we will see.

Plato is the founding figure of Western philosophy. There are
philosophers before Plato, but none of them leave us a literary
legacy that becomes the foundation for the rest of Western thought.
Plato's writings are really at the root of the entire tradition of
Western philosophy. The modern philosopher Alfred North
Whitehead has said that Western philosophy is a series of footnotes
to Plato — and that is almost not an exaggeration.

What Plato founds is a form of philosophy we can call metaphysics.
When we get to the postmodern philosophers at the end of this
series of lectures, this tradition of metaphysics — which is at the
heart of Western philosophy — will be raised as both a fundamental
problem and a fundamental resource of Western thought.
Metaphysics is the notion that there is something like true being,
transcendental being, that lies not merely in appearances but
somehow beneath, behind, within, or above the appearances of this
world. That is a deep notion — maybe a false notion, maybe a
problematic notion, maybe a notion we cannot escape even when we
are not entirely comfortable with it. That is what postmodern
philosophers will often think about when they think about Plato: you
cannot escape him.

You cannot escape Plato and still be a Western thinker. And yet this
notion of metaphysics — of a deep, high, or true being — is a
genuinely problematic notion. It is not just an idea that sits quietly in
the Western tradition; it is a source of disagreement, debate,
difficulty, and inspiration.

So Plato founds what we can call metaphysics. The word itself is
Aristotle's. It is very typical of Aristotle, who was a student of Plato,
to give a name to what Plato had already been doing. Plato was
doing metaphysics before there was a name for it; he was on a quest
for true being before Aristotle gave that quest the name
metaphysics.

Now, Plato pursued this quest by writing philosophical dialogues —
something like little plays in which characters discuss philosophical
issues and engage in critical examination of them. The main
character in these dramas is Socrates, Plato's teacher. To understand
this, we need to say something about the setting of fifth-century and
fourth-century Athens.

Socrates and the Sophists

When Plato was growing up in Athens and Socrates was at work, the
educational system in Athens was changing. There had not really
been a formal educational system before. If you were a young man
— and it was only young men whose education was considered in
those days, in what was a very sexist society — you were educated
by your father within the household. The old aristocratic notion was:
it was good enough for me, it will be good enough for my son. |
know what it is to be a citizen of Athens, and | can teach my son
what he needs to know.

But this was changing, because into Athens came an influx of new
professional teachers called sophists — the first professional teachers

the Greek world had known. They taught for a fee, which meant, of
course, that they claimed to possess knowledge worth paying for.
They would seek out young men, get them talking, and try to
persuade their fathers through those young men that they needed to
invest in a proper education.

The kind of education the sophists typically offered was education in
speaking. Athens was an ancient democracy — and imagine a
democracy without television, newspapers, or any form of media
other than the spoken word. Eloquence in speech was the
fundamental form of media power in Athens; it was the fundamental
form of political power. So the sophists offered, for a fee, the skill of
speaking, which was in effect the skill of political power. Do you want
your son to be a great man in Athens? | will teach him how. | will
make him a powerful, virtuous, and persuasive person — someone
who can speak on any subject in public, win arguments, win law
cases, and move the Athenian assembly to war or to peace as his
interests dictate.

For the sophists, then, wisdom meant the ability to speak eloquently
and persuasively. They called themselves sophists because sophia is
Greek for wisdom. They were wise men — or so they claimed — and
they taught wisdom for a fee.

This may seem a peculiar idea: teaching wisdom for a fee. It certainly
seemed peculiar to Socrates, who was a native Athenian rather than
one of these foreigners coming in — for the sophists were all non-
Athenians. Socrates had an entirely different program for young
men. He did not claim to be wise. He did not claim to know all the
answers. He certainly did not collect a fee. Instead, he walked around
with young men and asked more fundamental questions than the
sophists ever asked.

The sophists would teach you how to talk about virtue and vice,
about good and evil, about right and wrong conduct. Socrates
thought that before you could talk responsibly about such things,
you needed to know what they actually were. What Is virtue, after all?
So Socrates would typically ask these basic questions — what is
virtue? Or, taking a particular virtue, he would ask: what is justice?
What is temperance? What is courage? What is piety? And he would
say, | do not know. Now, young man, do you know? Let us find out
together.

More comically, Socrates would sometimes approach someone who
already thought they had the answers — a sophist, perhaps, or one
of the elder statesmen of Athens. Imagine Socrates approaching a
powerful man, someone who has moved large audiences with his
speeches — someone like Pericles, let us say, though the chronology
is a little awkward. Socrates would say: very well, Pericles, you have
been persuading us what the right thing to do is. Tell me — what Is
justice, really? After Pericles gave an answer, Socrates would ask a
further critical question, and the dialogue would go on in that
fashion until Socrates had asked more and more probing questions
and the great man could not hold his position.

The conclusion of these exchanges — in Plato's early dialogues — is
that the big man does not know the answer. He cannot give a clear
account of what something like virtue or justice truly is. These early
Socratic dialogues of Plato end in perplexity, in the recognition of
ignorance.

This has been called the Socratic wisdom — the wisdom of
recognizing that one is not truly wise, the wisdom of not being a
sophist. And the method of question and answer has been called the
Socratic method: a method in which a teacher does not simply
lecture and tell you what is what, but asks you questions, lets you
reply, and then asks more critical questions, so that you discover you

do not know what you thought you knew. At least that is where
things end in the early Socratic dialogues of Plato, and this was most
likely how Socrates himself actually worked.

The Birth of Metaphysics: The Theory of Forms

The question then arises: is the message of Socrates simply the
message of skepticism? At the end of the Socratic dialogues, we
recognize that we do not know what virtue is. Is that the final answer
— that we simply do not know?

Plato, in what are called his middle dialogues — his later literary
productions — came up with the idea that no, we can give an answer
to the question: what is virtue? We can give an answer to the
question: what is justice? It takes considerable work, and it still
requires the Socratic method of question and answer, which Plato
called dialectic — from the Greek word for dialogue. But we can, in
fact, arrive at an answer. We can find the true being of virtue. And
that is when metaphysics is born. That is when the notion of a true
being of things enters the Western tradition in a decisive way.

Plato will say that we can find the form of virtue. This is the famous
Platonic doctrine of forms, which you may also have heard called the
doctrine of Platonic ideas. | will use the word form for reasons | will
explain in a moment.

Virtue itself is a form. The true being of virtue is a form. What we are
asking for when we ask what virtue is — that is a form. We are asking
for an unchanging essence of virtue. Contrast that with the many
different ways of being virtuous: there is more than one way, there is
more than one virtue, and what counts as virtue in Athens might
differ from what counts as virtue in Sparta, and from what counts as
virtue in the modern world. There are many virtues, and what is
reckoned virtuous changes across time and culture. But Plato wants

to know the true being of virtue — virtue itself, the essence of virtue,
the unchanging being of virtue.

In wanting that, Plato was inventing Western metaphysics. And the
true being of virtue, as he conceives it, has two essential features.
First, it is one thing, not many. There are many virtuous acts and
many kinds of virtue, but there is only one form or essence of virtue
— that single thing which governs, gives form to, and confers
identity and nature upon all the many acts of virtue. The one form
forms the many; the one-and-many relationship is crucial for Plato
and for the Platonic tradition. Second, it is an unchanging form. The
form of virtue does not change. Virtue is fundamentally the same
thing now as it was in Plato's time. Some aspects of what we count
as virtue have shifted across history, of course — what makes a good
American is different from what made a good Athenian. But virtue
itself has not changed. Virtue itself is eternal.

So here is this notion of a deep, high, fundamental true being which
is unchanging and eternal — deeper than the changes of the
historical world, and more real than the appearances of history and
culture.

Plato calls this the form. One can also call it the essence, which is a
good term for it. Plato also uses the word idea, though this does not
mean the same thing that idea means today. Nowadays, when we
speak of an idea, we mean something in our heads. For Plato, an idea
is not something in your head. It is something you see — It is literally
the Greek word for what is seen. But he means something seen with
the mind's eye, not the eye of the body. The form of virtue is
something you will never see with your physical eyes; it is something
you can only see, as it were, with your mind. That is why he calls it an
idea: it is an unchanging essence which is not located in the physical
world and not located in your mind, but located metaphorically
above us in an unchanging world, which we will get to. In order to

avoid the confusion between Platonic ideas and modern ideas-in-
our-heads, | will use the term Platonic forms.

In summary, then, Platonic ideas or forms are true being — the
unchanging essence or nature of things in this world, especially of
ethical virtues. We apprehend these things with the mind's eye, not
the body's eye.

Mathematics as an Analogy

Let me suggest an analogy that may make this easier to follow. Think
about mathematics. Mathematics is very different from virtue, | know,
but for Plato they have something important in common. What Plato
is doing, | think, is in some sense comparing ethical thought about
virtue and justice to mathematical thought about triangles or
addition.

Consider a triangle. Think about a mathematics professor drawing a
triangle on a chalkboard. The professor is going to go on to prove
some theorems about this chalk triangle — the Pythagorean
theorem, for instance. But wait. That is a slight misstatement, is it
not? Because the professor is not really proving a theorem about the
chalk triangle. If you tried to measure the angles of a chalk triangle,
you would get something close to 180 degrees, but very probably
not exactly 180, because the lines are not perfectly straight and the
angles are not perfect. In a sense, the chalk triangle is not a real
triangle.

Which raises the question: what is a real triangle, then? The real
triangle we learn about in a mathematics class is not something you
can see with the eyes of your body. You will never see the real
triangle about which the Pythagorean theorem holds. Except — you
will see it with your mind's eye. Think of what happens in a
mathematics class when you are struggling to understand a proof,

and then suddenly it comes to you: aha, | see it, | get it. That kind of
seeing is the kind in which you really know what the mathematics
teacher has to teach. And that kind of seeing is also, Plato thinks, the
kind in which we come to see the form of virtue and justice.

This means that if you are a Platonist, you have to come to terms
with the idea that the things you see with your physical eyes are not
the truly real things. They are not exactly illusions, but they are not as
real as the real triangle. The chalk triangle is there; it is real in some
sense. But it is not the real triangle, and what we want to know about
is that real triangle — the thing you can only understand with your
mind's eye. And here again is metaphysics: we are looking at
something deeper than what appears on the surface, deeper than
anything you can see with your eyes, deeper and more true than the
world of appearance.

Plato's Vocabulary

Plato develops a set of technical vocabulary for marking the
distinction between the true being of the forms and the sensible
things of the physical world. Let us run through it briefly.

The true triangle — the one you can really know and see with your
mind's eye — is something we have knowledge of. That is the kind of
knowledge you attain in a mathematics class. The kind of
acquaintance you have with things you can see with your eyes is
mere opinion. Imagine trying to work out the Pythagorean theorem
by actually measuring chalk or pencil triangles. You would arrive at
an approximation of the theorem, which Plato would call opinion,
not knowledge. Knowledge is when you truly understand the
theorem — when you can say, aha, | see it, it is absolutely clear, it is
crystalline and certain. That is real knowledge. Whereas the sort of
acquaintance you acquire through sensory experience is not really

knowledge at all; it is merely opinion, of the sort you would form by
measuring a collection of imperfect drawn triangles.

Another feature of Plato's vocabulary concerns the relationship
between the chalk triangle and the real one. The chalk triangle is an
imitation, an image, a reflection, a shadow of the real triangle. That
cluster of metaphors — imitation, image, reflection, shadow — is one
Plato loves to use to describe the relationship between true being
and appearance. He sets the world of being against the world of
becoming, which is his term for the world of change.

The chalk triangle, as it were, imitates the real triangle; it is an image,
a reflection, a shadow of it. Get used to the idea that what you see,
touch, taste, and feel is only a shadow of the real thing, which you
see with your mind's eye. The real thing is unchanging and therefore
belongs to being — Being with a capital B — as opposed to
becoming, the world of change. The true triangle belongs to being,
not becoming. It is also what Plato calls intelligible, as opposed to
sensible. The word sensible, for Plato, derives from the senses: it
means anything you can perceive with your five senses — anything
visible to the eye, audible to the ear, tangible to the touch, anything
you can smell or taste. Those things belong to the world of
becoming, the world of opinion, the world of shadows. Whereas the
thing you truly understand with your mind's eye is intelligible —
from the word intellect or intelligence, which in Greek means to
understand. The intelligible thing is the thing that you understand.
Plato sometimes compares it to visibility, speaking of something
visible to the mind's eye, but that is clearly a metaphor: you do not
see the true triangle with your body's eye.

Once you have this vocabulary in hand, you begin to get the feel of
Plato's talk about true being and of why it is so very different from
what you see, taste, or touch — what we ordinarily call the real
world. | can bang on the podium, and that is real enough. But for

Plato, that is not ultimate reality. Why should you think that
something you can knock against is the ultimate reality? Plato wants
to know about ultimate reality, and it takes some adjustment to think
about a reality more real than what we normally call the real world.

The Allegory of the Cave

Let me describe an allegory, a symbolic story that comes from Plato
himself, which will help you grasp this metaphysics of true being that
goes beyond the world of appearances. This is the Allegory of the
Cave, and it comes from Plato's Republic.

Begin by imagining yourself in a dark cave. You are not merely in a
dark cave, however; you are tied up, chained in such a way that your
head cannot turn. You are looking straight ahead at a wall, and what
you see on the wall are shadows. The shadows are cast by a fire
burning behind you. You cannot turn around to look at the fire, nor
at the objects casting the shadows. What casts the shadows, it does
not matter too much, but let us say cardboard cutouts of things: a
cardboard cutout of a triangle, a cardboard cutout of a tree, and a
cardboard cutout we might label virtue. You are looking at the
shadows of these things on the wall.

That is a strange picture, says Plato — but it is us. It is where we are
right now. You are looking at, say, a table or a chair or a video screen,
and those are all shadows. What would it be like to look away from
them at something real?

That is hard to imagine when you have spent your whole life chained
up, looking straight ahead at the shadows, convinced that those
shadows are the only real thing — what many people call the real
world, the practical world, the world of money and power and things
that matter.

For Plato, that is not the real world, but you cannot see this until
something changes. What must change, metaphorically, is the
following. First, you have to be liberated — unchained. The image of
freedom and liberation is crucial here. Second, you turn around. The
Greek word for turning is translated into Latin as conversio, from
which we get our word conversion. This is a conversion experience, a
turning away from the darkness toward the light. Third, you must
climb. The cave is underground, and you must ascend — and this
ascent is the image of the soul's journey from a world of appearance
and illusion to a world of true being.

So: liberation, conversion, and ascent. You climb up and you emerge
into the light — the real light, the light of day, the light of the sun.
This is what Plato calls the intelligible world, the world of true being.
We might call it the other world, as in other-worldliness, because
Plato is telling us about a level of being that many of us never
realized existed, while we remained convinced that the cave was the
real world.

This is the world of eternal being, of truth that never changes.
Everything down in the cave changes — triangles in the cave change
because you can erase or redraw them — but the true triangle you
come to understand in a mathematics class does not change. So by
climbing up out of the cave you are moving out of the world of
change and death and into the world of eternity and true being.

Of course, the first thing that happens when you emerge is that you
are blinded — it is too bright up there. Learning about true being is
hard. The mind gets dazzled, just as your eyes would be dazzled
coming into bright sunlight for the very first time in your life. You
cannot take it in immediately. But you practice; you learn; you get
educated. You may start off by looking at reflections and shadows in
the light, but eventually you become able to look directly at real
things — not cardboard cutouts, not shadows, not reflections, but

the real thing — and see what the essence, the form, the true being
of a tree is, or of a triangle, or, most importantly of all, of virtue and
justice and the good.

The good, in fact, is the last thing you see. The good is the sun itself,
the shining light that illuminates everything else. It is the first
principle, the supreme being — Plato calls it the good. In religious
terms one might call it the supreme good. Christian theologians
would later arrive at this passage and say: ah, the supreme good —
that is God. The source of being, just as the sun makes possible the
life of plants and animals on earth, giving being to things and also
the intelligibility that makes it possible for us to perceive them.

That is what happens in education, in Plato's view. As we learn to
turn away from mere appearances — the chalk triangle being only an
imitation of the real thing — we are, in a process of growing
education, gradually able to see the real things themselves. The chalk
triangle is just a symbol, an imitation of the real triangle; but with
enough mathematical education, you come to see the real thing
directly.

The Return to the Cave

Then something else happens. You cannot stay up there — not until
you die; we will come to that in the next lecture. You cannot remain
in the other world where all is light and unchanging truth. Eventually
you have to go back down into the cave.

And in fact, following Plato's advice, what you will do is go back to
educate a group of young men, teaching them about true virtue.
Though it must be stressed: they cannot simply be told what true
virtue is. They must climb up themselves, with the help of a teacher
— perhaps someone like Socrates, asking questions, pressing them:
what is true virtue? Each student has to find the answer for himself,

see it for himself. This is exactly like mathematics: you cannot merely
memorize a theorem and be said to understand it. You have to see it
with your own mind's eye. The teacher helps you get started, but you
do not simply memorize what the teacher says — you come to see it
yourself. Then, if you are following Plato's program of education, you
get dragged back down into the cave and made to be king.

That is the idea. Plato wants the rulers of the republic to be people
who genuinely know what the good is, who know what true justice
and true virtue are. You have to know some metaphysics in order to
be a good ruler, Plato thinks, because you have to know what virtue
and justice and goodness truly are. This is why Plato places this
entire program of education at the center of his great treatise on
politics, the Republic. And the heart of that treatment of education is
the vision of the true being of the forms.

So you come back down into the cave. The first thing that happens,
once again, is that your eyes have to adjust. You have grown
accustomed to seeing things in the bright light of day; now you are
back in the cave and you can barely see anything. You stumble
around. You act like a philosopher — like those people who fall down
wells because they are contemplating the sky. That was Thales, the
first philosopher, of whom there is the famous joke: he was gazing at
the heavens and fell into a well. Philosophers are like that. They have
seen the light, and now they must readjust to the darkness of what
we call the real world. They are not good at making money —
Socrates certainly was not. They are not good at playing political
power games — Socrates was not good at those either. They do not
teach the sort of things the sophists teach, and they are not skilled at
what the sophists are skilled at.

But the kind of person you truly want ruling the city is not the
sophist, not the person who believes in political power games, not
the political consultant and those who follow political consultants —

you do not want them in power. You want the people who have seen
what the true good really is. You want people like Socrates.

Socrates avoided political office, but Plato is thinking: if you have
someone like Socrates in your midst, what you do is make him king.
You say, you must rule over us, because you understand more about
this than we do. Even if you do not think you fully know what true
virtue is, at least you are asking the right question. And Plato thinks
that in fact you can ask this question, you can guide young people
toward the answer, you can enable them actually to see what true
virtue is — and once they have seen it, you drag them back down
into the cave and make them rule over you. You make philosophers
into kings. That is the key idea of Plato's political theory.

So Plato is not only inventing this new form of philosophy called
metaphysics — he is saying that metaphysics is the real basis of a
well-ordered human life. It is the basis of ethics. It is the basis of
politics. The person you want to have power Is the one who actually
understands what the true good really is. Knowledge of true being is
the most practically important thing there is, if what we want is a
good life — a life in the city, in the republic, that is genuinely worth
living. And that is why Plato wants to make philosophers kings. But
we will hear more about that in a later lecture.

Lecture 5: Plato, Politics
Professor Dennis Dalton

Introduction: Plato and the Republic

In this lecture on Plato's theory of politics in the Republic, we will be
looking at one of the foremost philosophers of all time. He was born
in 428 BC, which means that he lived some 2,400 years ago. He was
inspired by another great philosopher, his teacher Socrates. He
founded the Academy at age forty in order to set forth his ideas, and
during that time he wrote the Republic, his most famous work and
the major announcement of his entire political philosophy.

It was Alfred North Whitehead, a professor of philosophy at Harvard,
who said — perhaps with some exaggeration — that Plato's
philosophy is so encyclopedic that the whole of Western philosophy
can be seen as merely a series of footnotes to Plato. Plato's purpose
in writing the Republic was to bring philosophy to politics, to uplift
politics, to inject into politics vision and character. As he set forth
these ideas, he manages to expound some of the truly great
concepts of the Western tradition — ideas of justice, peace,
goodness, beauty, and truth, but perhaps above all of power. As we
treat Plato and then Aristotle and then after that Hobbes and Locke,
we will be focusing increasingly on the idea of power, because this is,
of course, at the center of political philosophy.

The Question of Justice: Three Responses

The Republic begins with the question, "What is justice?" and slides
into more and more consideration of power. It is done in the form of
a dialogue — a dialogue between Socrates and a number of his

friends and adversaries. There are three main responses in the first
part of the Republic to this key question: What is justice?

Polemarchus

The first response is given by one of Socrates' young friends,
Polemarchus. He addresses the question and says: justice is no more
than helping one's friends and harming one's enemies. That is, it is
no more than an eye for an eye, a tooth for a tooth, as said in
another biblical context. Socrates argues against this and, very
significantly — for the first time in political thought — announces
that justice, or as he defines it, right conduct, cannot ever mean to
inflict harm on another person.

Socrates disagrees with Polemarchus' definition, sweeping it aside
and saying that justice or right conduct must always mean to elicit
the very best from an individual, to treat that individual in a way that
he or she is uplifted or humanized as a result of that interaction. He
introduces an important concept here — noted in our glossary — of
arete. Arete means the virtue, excellence, or gift that any individual
has, and each of us possesses this particular quality. Just behavior,
right conduct, must therefore always mean trying to bring out one's
arete — one's particular gift, one's excellence.

Socrates says that can never be done through punishing or harming
an individual. Polemarchus' definition of justice — while it might be
necessary at times to harm people in self-defense — cannot be seen
as right conduct or just conduct in all circumstances. Right conduct
or just conduct, Socrates says at the end of this crucial first exchange
with Polemarchus, does not mean an eye for an eye. It means dealing
with others in a humane manner.

Thrasymachus

The next discussant is Thrasymachus. He is entirely different from
Polemarchus. Polemarchus was a young, eager student, but

Thrasymachus is a cynic — a person whom we refer to in Greek
philosophy as a sophist, who believes that all values are necessarily
relative. The exchange between Socrates and Thrasymachus is, |
think, one between a philosopher like Socrates who is defending
ideal values, and Thrasymachus the Relativist, who is saying that
justice can mean no more than might, and might is always right.

From Thrasymachus' point of view — and this is an important point
in political philosophy — as we look back on the tradition of political
thought, we wonder how Plato could have introduced in this text so
many critical questions all around this discussion of justice. Justice,
Thrasymachus says, is no more than what is in the interest of the
strongest party. That person or party or political group which
happens to have charge of a situation will define justice, and
whatever that party says is justice will be just. This is a cynical
definition of justice that Thucydides discusses in his history of the
Peloponnesian War and deplores. It was a definition that had
characterized Athenian thinking during that war, during the
debasement of values and the cynicism into which the Athenians
lapsed.

Socrates says, "Thrasymachus, this cannot be justice. It cannot be
right conduct to insist that might is always right, that the stronger
must always prevail." And at this point Socrates makes an analogy
that is critical for the Republic — the first of a series of analogies —
between the politician and the physician, the political leader and the
doctor. The politician, Socrates says, must be held to certain
standards just as the physician is held to certain standards. What we
want in politics is true professionalism of the kind that we have in
medicine. We want a kind of Hippocratic oath for politics. Remember
that Hippocrates was living during the time of Socrates and must
have influenced Plato's writing in this respect.

When Plato tells Thrasymachus that the analogy of the physician to
the politician should prevail, he means that politicians should act
more like the professionals that we have in medicine. And how do
good physicians act? Presumably, a good physician never sees his
patient as a weaker person upon whom he may impose his will. The
physician sees his patient as one to be regarded with humanity and
considered with genuine interest. If doctors — good doctors — were
the models and set the standards for politicians, we would have in
politics a much more humane practice. We would have a professional
practice. We would be able to hold people to higher standards. What
is justice, then, according to Socrates? Justice is a kind of human
relationship characterized by what we ideally find in the relationship
between the good doctor and the patient — a relationship notable
for its humanity and its concern. And Socrates will say that justice
must first of all be concerned with the Hippocratic maxim: first, do no
harm. \f only in politics we could have that maxim — first, do no
harm.

Glaucon and the Ring of Gyges

After the exchange between Socrates and Thrasymachus, the third
part of the exchange is between Socrates and a young student of his
by the name of Glaucon — who happens to be an older brother of
Plato. Glaucon tries to revive Thrasymachus' argument. He wants to
expand it in a way that applies not just the idea that might is right,
but to say that in human nature there is a quality of selfishness that
makes all our interests necessarily allied to our selfish behavior. He
asks Socrates: how did justice originate?

Glaucon answers his own question by saying that clearly it originated
because in the state of nature — in our original human condition —
people were all concerned with protecting one another and
protecting themselves. There was a state of mutual insecurity in
which people were afraid and needed protection: protection of their

families and of themselves. In that state of vulnerability, law was
created. People grouped together and proposed a contract, a
covenant, that set forth the idea that law or justice meant mutual
protection — that the weak would be protected against the strong.
Justice is law, but law is contrived. There is nothing transcendental in
it, nothing particularly moral — only protection against those who
may be stronger.

To reinforce this argument, Glaucon says, "Let us consider a myth,
Socrates — the myth of Gyges." He draws it from the Greek historian
Herodotus and embellishes it a great deal. It tells the story of a poor
shepherd who was wandering one day in the fields and came upon a
chasm. He descended into it and discovered a skeleton — a huge
skeleton — and the skeleton had a ring. The ring, glowing there,
attracted Gyges so much that he went and took it from the skeleton's
finger. He put it on, not quite knowing what it meant. But as he
began to circulate among other shepherds, he noticed that when he
turned the ring a certain way, his friends would say, "Where is Gyges?
What has happened to Gyges?" He soon understood that turning the
ring in a certain way made him invisible.

Now Glaucon asks Socrates: imagine if we had this power to become
invisible — what do you think we would do? Socrates says he does
not know. Glaucon says: "I'll tell you what Gyges did." Did he found a
philanthropic organization and give away his newfound fortune to
charity? No, quite the opposite. With his invisibility, he managed to
maximize his own interest to the extent of entering the castle,
murdering the king, seducing the queen, and seizing the throne.

From Glaucon’'s point of view, the lesson of the myth of Gyges is that
we all, if we could have the power of invisibility, would do precisely
this. We would all become like Gyges: selfish and greedy, and above
all, lusting for power. That seizure of power, that will to dominate,
that desire to prevail — that is in all of us. What is justice, then?

Justice is no more than a contract that keeps people under control,
that offers protection from Gyges and that kind of behavior. Justice is
law — but law is contrived.

Socrates' Response: Three Waves of Reform

Socrates’ response to Glaucon takes us into the rest of the Republic's
argument. Socrates argues that we cannot all be seen as Gyges. Is
there a Gyges gene — a dominant gene in all of us that is selfish,
greedy, and lustful? He says to Glaucon that this is the worst way of
viewing humanity. We must try to elicit the best from people and not
look to their worst. We must try to define a common interest, not just
a selfish interest. And that common interest is that justice should
prevail — that the good state will certainly emerge.

How will this good state emerge? Socrates says there must be three
reforms, three waves of change — what he calls tidal waves of
transformation that must sweep over the corrupt polis and transform
it into something new, better, right, and true.

The First Wave: The Equality of Women and Men

The first wave of change is to make women and men equal in terms
of their qualifications for rule. This is critical, for at this time women
were regarded — as we will see when we discuss Aristotle — as
those who must perform no public duties, have no property, whose
responsibilities were confined entirely to the home. They therefore
held no public office of any kind, and to suggest that women and
men might be equal — even equal leaders of the polis — was
revolutionary.

Why does Plato want to do this? He is looking to the female
population as people he can recruit, individuals he can use. Women
should not be excluded from rule as they had been. But only, and
this is Plato's emphasis, those women who are truly qualified — just

as men must be truly qualified. That is, women who are intelligent,
who can pass the rigorous examinations — the kind of professional
boards that we might imagine for politics — who can become part of
the intellectual elite that Plato is proposing.

The Second Wave: The Guardians

The second wave is to create an extended family of rulers that Plato
calls the guardians — an elite of intellectuals chosen by the same
rigorous standard by which we choose our physicians. This elite will
rule the polis, forming a minority within the community.

Within this extended family, two major reforms will be undertaken.
First, these guardians will not own property. The abolition of
property is key, because from Plato's point of view it is necessary in
order to abolish greed, lust, possessiveness, and all the rest among
his leaders. Second, the nuclear family itself will be abolished. This
will be a truly extended family, with property in common, spouses in
common, and children in common. These rulers will therefore not be
tempted by nepotism or possessiveness. They will be truly ideal in
the sense that they will be free of the problems that characterized
rulers during that period. This critical reform transforms the rulers
into those who are thinking not of their own private families or
property, but of the state and the needs of the state.

The Third Wave: Absolute Political Power

The last of the three waves is to put these guardians in positions of
absolute political power, with no checks or balances. These are the
people who will administer the state. From the guardians, the vision
will emerge. From these few, rule will be just and rational. The
confidence is that individuals who are free from concerns about
family and property will be able to shape policy in which reason truly
rules.

Two Parables

Plato argues his position further through two parables or allegories
designed to reinforce his point about the just state — a state ruled
by a group of intellectuals, an elite gifted by virtue of their vision,
their education, and their liberation from the narrow, partial, selfish
concerns of family and property, with both men and women included
in what is truly a revolutionary proposal.

The Parable of the Ship of State

The first parable is the Ship of State. Imagine, Plato says, that you
are going on a voyage. You get on the ship and find that the master
— the captain — is not only short-sighted and deaf, but stupid and
completely ignorant of seamanship. Because the master is so
ignorant, the crew finds it very easy to manipulate him, to appeal to
him, to flatter him, and thereby to run the ship themselves. But the
crew happens to be entirely unqualified as well — their only concern
is the maximization of their own pleasure on the voyage, and so they
turn the voyage into what Plato calls a drunken carousal. The third
party on the ship — the navigator, the one person you might look to
for guidance — is viewed with contempt. The passengers are
therefore destined for a terrible voyage, and only shipwreck can be
the conclusion.

Plato says this parable is directly analogous to the democratic state
— the democratic state he knew in Athens. In that state, the master
represents the sovereign people: ignorant, short-sighted, deaf. The
crew represents the demagogic politicians of Athens, the people who
seduced the master and managed to persuade him that they were
right and that they should prevail. During the Peloponnesian War,
these politicians, from Plato's point of view, were catastrophically
bad leaders. Not only did Athens lose the war against Sparta in 404
BC — it was not merely defeated but overrun and humiliated,
marking the end of its great civilization. How could this have

happened? Because it was run by a democratic government, and
Plato despises democracy.

What Plato wants is a ship truly run not by the ignorant captain, not
by the crew of self-interested politicians, but by the navigator —
seen not with contempt, but honored as the person who possesses
the most knowledge. That navigator is the one individual who has a
command of seamanship, who has knowledge of astronomy, who is
a scientist, just as the physician is a scientist. And that navigator
should be analogous to the philosopher-ruler — the person who is in
charge because he or she has mastered the scientific principles of
how to run a state: political science.

The Allegory of the Cave

The second and more famous parable is the Allegory of the Cave.
Plato now asks us — with the extraordinarily vivid imagination he
possesses — to imagine entering an underground cavern filled with
darkness and illusion, in which people can see only shadows and not
reality. They look at the walls of the cave and mistake the shadows
they see there for reality. This, Plato points out, is profound
ignorance — and yet the people who inhabit the cave are entirely
unaware of it.

What would happen, he asks, if a person were able to turn around,
see the light at the entrance of the cave, follow that long tunnel out
into the open, and for the first time, by the light of the sun, come to
understand that the reality outside the cave is true reality, and that
what had been mistaken for reality inside the cave was only illusion?
What if that person came back and told the cave's inhabitants: "You
have been looking only at shadows on the walls, not at reality"?
What would they do?

Plato says — alluding to the fate of Socrates, who was sentenced to
death and forced to take hemlock in 399 BC — they would kill this

person, just as they killed Socrates. The most righteous man in
Athens, when he came back into the cave bearing truth, was
murdered. What kind of state would be needed to put people like
Socrates In power?

Plato says that the state we must have would need to educate the
population in such a way that when that enlightened person
returned from outside the cave, he or she would be welcomed as a
ruler rather than condemned as a lunatic. People must be educated
— not necessarily so that the whole majority can get out of the cave,
but at least so that they can receive that person upon their return
with an understanding that they have been truly enlightened. We
want those individuals to rule.

At the very end of the allegory, Plato writes in the Republic — in one
of its finest passages:

We don't want an unjust polity any longer in which men live fighting
one another about shadows and quarreling for power as if that were
some great prize. The kind of just polis that we need will have rulers
unlike any ruler of the present day, because our rulers will think of
holding power only as an unavoidable necessity and have no lust for it.
For the truth ts that you can have a well-governed society only if you
can discover for your future rulers a better way of life than being in
office. Then only will power be in the hands of men who are rich, not
in gold, but in the wealth that brings happiness, a good and wise life.
All goes wrong when, starved for lack of anything good in their own
lives, men turn to public affairs, hoping to snatch from thence the
happiness they hunger for. They set about fighting for power, and this
internecine conflict ruins them and their country. The life of true
philosophy ts the only one that looks down upon offices of state, and
access to power must be confined to men who are not in love with it.
So whom else can you compel to undertake the guardianship of the
Commonwealth, if not those who, besides understanding best the

principles of government, enjoy a nobler life than the politicians today
and look for rewards of a different kind?

Conclusion: Plato as Philosopher of Power

Notice the central points Plato makes in this crucial passage at the
end of the allegory of the cave. First and foremost, it is one's view of
power that is all important. One must not lust for power. One must
receive power as an obligation — as a responsibility to the whole
state — and use it in service of the common interest, not a selfish
one.

Plato believes in a concentration of power among the few, among
this elite at the top. That is true. But this elite will regard power in an
entirely different way. They will look at it with disinterest. They will
see power as a responsibility, not as an object to be acquired.

When Plato says that the problem today is that people are starved —
starved for lack of anything good in their own lives — and then enter
politics, he makes a critical point: the political leaders we want must
be individuals who have integrity and character, who have
discovered their arete as that of political leadership, and who enter
politics precisely because they understand it to be a noble profession
comparable to the profession of medicine.

When we look to Plato, then, we look to a philosopher of power, and
he is to be acclaimed as the first political philosopher who sets forth
power as a noble enterprise.

Lecture 6: Plato, Psychology
Professor Philip Carey

Introduction: Why More Plato?

We have three lectures on Plato because Plato deserves it, being as
great a mind as any of them. In the last lecture we talked about true
being, or metaphysics. Now we will talk about a connected, related
notion: the true self — the soul. Plato gives us a way of thinking
about the deep layers of the self that is connected to his way of
thinking about the deep, or high, or true nature of being. So there is
a connection between metaphysics — the study of true being — and
psychology, which is the study of the soul, the study of the psyche,
which is Greek for soul. Psychology in ancient thought therefore
meant a philosophical study of the soul.

Plato is in many ways the most influential psychologist of the
Western tradition, because he gave us what is still one of the
dominant ways of thinking about the nature of the soul and indeed
the nature of the human self.

Plato and the Invention of Dualism

Plato was the first — | think he probably was — to have invented this
form of thought: dualism, that is, the notion that the human self
divides cleanly into two parts, soul and body. It seems obvious,
doesn't it? But it was not obvious to anyone before Plato.

In Homer, for instance — the great Greek poet before Plato — the
human self would divide into quite a number of parts, not just two.
There would not be a sort of mental half called soul and a physical
half called body. The terms for psychological functions might include

heart, spirit, breath, liver, or kidneys. The same word, pneuma, could
be translated as either breath or spirit. The same thing, by the way, Is
true of the Old Testament: "kidneys" is an Old Testament word for
something like "guts," and "bowels of compassion" is a New
Testament phrase that comes from Hebrew ways of speaking. But
both the Hebrew ways of talking in the Old Testament and the
ancient Greek ways of talking before Plato had this whole range of
terms which overlapped. Your heart and your spirit covered part of
the same aspects of yourself. Your spirit included your breath. In
Hebrew, your nefesh — your soul — literally meant your neck. Your
guts included both your physical guts and how you feel in your gut.
Your heart included both your physical heart and how you feel in
your heart.

So before Plato, there was no notion of a clean bifurcation or division
of the self into two parts — soul and body, mental and physical. That
seems like an obvious division to us, but that is because Plato lived
and wrote and thought. And if he hadn't, we might not find it so
obvious. This is what happens when you have a great mind: you end
up thinking the way you do in the twentieth century, in part because
of the way he thought two and a half millennia ago.

The Phaedo: The Soul, the Body, and Immortality

How do we arrive at this picture of the human self divided into two?
It begins with a concern for the immortality of the soul, which is the
key theme in Plato's middle dialogue, the Phaedo. The Phaedo is set
on the last day of Socrates’ life. In this middle dialogue, as in the
early Socratic dialogues, Socrates is the main character. But instead
of the question-and-answer method which typically leads to
perplexity, in the Phaedo Socrates has a point to make. He is actually
trying to prove something — to show that the soul is immortal. He is

also trying to help his students see this for themselves, and indeed to
comfort them, because they are in great grief. Their teacher is about
to die. He is about to drink the hemlock, which means he is about to
be executed. Socrates is very calm about it. He is not afraid of death,
but all of his students are. So in order to comfort them, he tries to
show them that the soul is immortal.

This is not as straightforward as it might seem. In Homer, for
instance, the soul is exhaled in the last breath of a dying warrior.
There is a scene many times in Homer's poems where a spear goes
through a man's heart, he breathes his last, and the soul goes out of
the mouth and gets scattered on the wind — scattered and gone.
Perhaps it goes to a dark place called Hades, where it lives a kind of
shadow existence. Perhaps it is scattered on the wind. Homer does
not have a consistent theology about that. But Socrates’ pupils in the
Phaedo are worried that this is exactly what will happen to Socrates’
soul. Because after all, after the last breath, there is no soul in the
body, no life in the body. So the last breath must carry the soul out
— and then what? Scattered on the wind.

If the soul is immortal, we are going to need a different picture of its
nature. The soul is going to have to be made of something other
than breath or blood. And it is going to have to have a destiny, a
direction, a home that is different from anything material.

The Soul as Kin to the Forms

The key notion in the Phaedo is that the soul is somehow connected
with the forms — the eternal, unchanging entities we discussed in
the previous lecture. The soul is somehow kin to the forms, kin to
what is deathless, unchanging, eternal, and true. The soul has a place
to go after death that is not merely Hades — that mythological
underworld of shades and shadows, a place of ghosts. Plato does not
want that kind of immortality. He is not talking about ghosts. He is

talking about a soul that belongs among true being, eternal being, in
the light — not in the darkness, not in a cave underground.

Because the soul is kin to the forms, its identity and destiny are not
situated in this bodily world. The soul is not fundamentally at home
in the body. Hence Socrates in this dialogue will say that true
philosophy, true philosophizing, means practicing to die — because
what happens when you die is that your soul gets separated from
your body. The soul is not at home in the body; the soul wants to go
back where it belongs, which is with the forms. The soul does not
want to be among things that die, and the body certainly dies. The
soul wants to be with things that are deathless, like the forms. It is
more akin to these deathless and eternal things than it is to the
mortal, changing, decaying body.

The Body as Prison

So what we have in the Phaedo is the origin of a tradition of what
one might call otherworldliness. The soul is not at home in the
bodily world. The soul belongs in a more eternal, deathless, and
changeless world. That is what you have to say, | think, in order to
maintain a notion of the soul as inherently immortal, because
nothing in the changing world of bodies is immortal.

Plato wants us to think of ourselves as not entirely at home in our
bodies. He wants us to practice for the separation of soul and body
— what he calls a form of purification. The soul needs to be purified
of its attachment to the body. Consider the implications. If detaching
yourself from the body means purification, then the body is in some
sense impure — something like a form of dirt. Think of what a body
is without a soul: a corpse. And a corpse is as unclean as it gets.
People do not like handling corpses. In Judaism and throughout
world religions, corpses are considered unclean.

The soul, Plato thinks, wants to be separated from that. If the soul
gets too involved in bodily pleasures or in money and other material
things — if the soul gets involved in these material things — it gets
dirtied by them. It gets nailed to the body, Plato will say in the
Phaedo. The soul becomes something nailed to the body by its
pleasures and pains and material interests. The body, Plato will call it,
is a prison for the soul. The soul does not want to be there. It is not
home there. That is why the true philosopher, the true seeker after
wisdom, Is practicing to die — practicing for the separation of body
from soul. The true philosopher is, in a word, an ascetic: someone
who is disciplining the soul to separate itself from bodily pleasures.

| am stressing this a little one-sidedly, because this is not everything
Plato has to say about the soul. But in the Phaedo it is the dominant
theme, and it really does represent a form of otherworldliness that
becomes very important in Neoplatonism and other later religious
forms of thought.

Recollection and the Pre-Existence of the Soul

Now, another step in Plato's argument for the immortality of the soul
must be added to the mix. Not only is the soul kin to the forms, but
the soul comes into the body already possessing some knowledge of
the forms. When we come to see the forms in this life — while still in
the body — that seeing is like a form of recollection.

Think of the experience. Here you are in a mathematics class, trying
to understand a proof. You do not get it for a while, but then all of a
sudden you get it — you see It. And it seems like something that has
always been there. You say to yourself: "How could | miss it? Of
course — in a sense | always knew this." You recognize it. It feels a
little bit like remembering, Plato thinks. It is like recollecting
something you already knew but had forgotten. Because what

happens is the soul gets into the body at birth — an infant body not
set up for knowledge — and you forget. Birth is a forgetting, and all
learning is a form of recollection. In Greek this is anamnesis, which
means recollection, the opposite of amnesia. All learning is
recollection.

This implies, of course, that the soul did not start out in the body.
After all, it is not at home there. The soul had an existence prior to
coming into the body.

The Myth of Transmigration

There is an early version of this story from Plato's dialogue the Meno,
which is also referred to in the Phaedo. This version involves
transmigration — or what many of us would call reincarnation. Our
souls were embodied in a previous life, where they could learn about
the forms, and then they get re-embodied in our present life, but
forget what they knew during childhood and then relearn through
education.

But this is somewhat mythological, and in fact it does not really work
as an explanation of recollection. Because if every time | catch a
glimpse of the true forms — the true being in the true world where
the light is really shining — | am really remembering something |
learned in a previous life, then how did | learn it in that previous life?
By recollecting from a life before that? And a life before that? And so
on to infinity? No, that does not explain it.

The Myth of the Fall: The Phaedrus

So Plato comes up in another dialogue with a more sophisticated,
though equally mythic, explanation. This is the dialogue called the
Phaedrus. |n this dialogue, the soul starts out not embodied in some
previous life, but rather disembodied. Because after all, the real
home of the soul — its real origin — is not among bodies at all. The
soul is imprisoned in a body, so the prison cannot be where it

belongs. It belongs in a disembodied state, contemplating heavenly
truths.

This is again mythic, like the allegory of the cave. Plato pictures the
soul as up in heaven — literally up in heaven — contemplating
things that are beyond the heavens. Greek astronomy pictured the
heavens as a kind of sphere surrounding the world, and up at the
very top of the sphere you could look out beyond the whole physical
world, beyond the whole visible world, to what is beyond vision, to
what is seen only by the mind's eye. That is where the soul is
mythically or metaphorically located at the beginning of its existence
— outside the cave, up in that world of light. Being dragged down
into the cave and chained up is birth.

The soul, therefore, is not made of material stuff. It is somehow kin
to these intelligible forms, which are not material at all. It belongs
with them. It feeds on contemplation — on the vision of these forms,
intellectual vision, the vision of the mind's eye. Getting into the world
of bodies is therefore not natural. Something has to go wrong for
the soul to descend into the world of bodies. Birth is, as it were, a
fall.

In Plato's myth of the fall, the soul is pictured as a charioteer with
two horses that are rather hard to manage. The soul gets tired, falls
down, tumbles, and ends up in bodies where it does not really
belong, and must work its way back through a series of
transmigrations. The key notion is that the soul starts out outside the
body and that its entrance into the body is a fall, a descent — the
opposite movement from the ascent out of the cave up to the world
of light. The upward movement is toward the disembodied,
immaterial, eternal world. The downward movement is_ the
movement of the fall from the eternal world into this changing world
of bodies, mortality, and death.

Eros: Love as Recollection and Ascent

This also explains, Plato thinks, why we fall in love. Think about the
experience of falling in love — especially the wildness of it, the desire
for something ultimate that seems to be part of the experience.
Somehow falling in love hints at some ultimate fulfillment that is
wildly beyond anything in this world.

Sometimes you think that a particular person in flesh and blood can
satisfy that desire, but Plato thinks they cannot, really. The person
you fall in love with — especially the physical sight of them — Is at
best a reminder of what you saw when you were not in the body.
Physical beauty is a reminder of eternal beauty. And eternal beauty
is, again, the beauty of the forms. Beauty with a capital B is, in fact,
another name for the Good with a capital G. The Good is, in one
aspect of it, the Beautiful.

What love is, then, is our response to beauty. All love, Plato thinks, is
directed at something beautiful. We literally cannot love what is ugly.
So when you fall in love, what is happening is that the sight of
another person — or perhaps the knowledge of their soul, their
character, who they are — is reminding you of eternal beauty. If you
are a materialistic sort, maybe it is the sight of someone's body that
is reminding you of eternal beauty. But if you are a noble sort, like
Socrates, maybe it is the sight of someone's soul.

Socrates would, as it were, seduce young men into talking
philosophy with him. He would take them outside the city wall, they
would sit under trees, and instead of engaging in what the Greeks
often expected in such settings — because noble sexuality in ancient
Athens was typically between men, as sex with women was for
reproduction and companionship between men was for higher
things — Socrates would seduce the young man into talking
philosophy. The result would be a perception of the beauty of a soul.

Socrates would perceive the beauty of the young man's soul, his
virtues. The young man, in turn, would also fall in love with Socrates.
There is a famous portrait of Alcibiades — one of the great black
sheep of Athens — falling in love with Socrates. Falling in love with
who he is. Socrates was not a physically beautiful man; he was, as a
matter of fact, rather ugly. But there was something beautiful about
his soul, something beautiful about who he was, that reminded
people of something eternal.

The virtue of a young man touches your heart, Plato says, because it
reminds you of the sight of true virtue that you had before you were
ever embodied. Falling in love is this reminiscence, this reminder of a
true vision of true beauty in the disembodied state.

The Ascent of Love: The Symposium

| have been talking about the descent — the fall — but there is also
this ascent, the climbing up, which we saw in the allegory of the cave.
It is also in Plato's dialogue the Symposium. The word symposium
originally meant, in Greek, a drinking party — and indeed, the
dialogue takes place at a gathering of men talking about love and
about what happens when an older man falls in love with a younger
man.

Socrates, in that dialogue, tries to convince his companions that what
is really happening is they are being reminded of true virtue and true
beauty. The noble lover, he argues, gets beyond bodily things — the
attraction to a young man's physical form — and ascends to love of
the soul, then to love of the city and its just laws. So one is ascending
from bodily beauty, to love of the virtues of an individual soul, to
love of the city and its just laws and constitution, and finally to love
of eternal justice, beauty, and goodness.

This is the original form of what we call Platonic love. It was Platonic
because it was not fundamentally sexual — and yet it started with

something like seduction. Throughout Plato's dialogues, you have
Socrates accosting a young man in a way that any Athenian would
have found rather suspicious. Off they go outside the city to sit
under a tree — in the Phaedrus, under a plane tree — and have a
private conversation. But they are talking philosophy. Socrates is
seducing the young man into love of eternal beauty. Love thereby
becomes a means of ascent, a means of the soul climbing out of the
cave. After all, why do we inquire about the Good? Not because we
are told we must, but because we desire it, we long for it. The whole
motive that drives Platonic inquiry — that desire for the deep and
true being of things — is a form of love, a form of eros. Falling in
love, that fundamental bodily and psychological experience, is a
recollection of that original vision of eternal beauty, spreading
through our whole body and soul.

The Three-Part Soul

Now, one of the things that happens as Plato gets older and writes
more is that he starts with a very simple and radical idea — like the
soul-body dualism of the Phaedo — and then makes it more
complicated, because the initial formulation is too simple. The
extreme otherworldliness of the Phaedo needed to be qualified.

So, for instance, in the Symposium he says that the love of bodies is
not entirely disconnected from the higher world. It is a reminiscence
of eternal beauty. You can start with loving what you see with your
physical eyes and ascend from there. Another thing that happens as
Plato develops his ideas is that he makes the concept of the soul
considerably more complex. In the Phaedo, he insists the soul is
simple — just one thing, having no parts, and therefore incapable of
being scattered on the wind. But in his later works, especially in the

Phaedrus and the Republic, he ends up proposing that the soul has
three parts.

The Chartoteer and Two Horses: The Phaedrus

In the Phaedrus, recall, the soul up in heaven is compared to a
charioteer with two horses. The soul, therefore, has three parts.
There is the charioteer, and there are the two horses. One is a good
horse, who always obeys what the charioteer wants. The other is an
unruly horse, hard to control.

The charioteer represents reason — the part of the soul most akin to
the forms, and the only part Plato was really concerned with in the
Phaedo. One horse could be called the spirited part of the soul —
spirited in the sense of a spirited horse, not in the sense of breath or
spirit, but in the sense of courage and noble energy. Think of a war
horse: obedient, strong, not as smart as the charioteer, but willing to
obey him. This is the courage-driven part of the soul that becomes
the key element of a soldier. The other horse is more lustful —
driven by desires and impulses, going this way and that depending
on what it happens to see. This appetitive horse makes it difficult for
the charioteer to keep the chariot under control, and eventually the
charioteer gets tired, and the fall happens.

Reason, Spirit, and Appetite: The Republic

There is another version of this three-part soul in the Republic, where
it can be explained a bit more clearly. You have three parts. The
highest is the rational part of the soul — like the charioteer. Then
there is the spirited part, a middle part that Plato locates in the chest
— he actually uses the word thumos. We can think of it as the heart,
the soldier part. Indeed, the soldiers in Plato's Republic are the ones
who are predominantly keyed to this part of the soul. And then there
is the appetitive part: think of every part of your body below the
chest — hunger, desire, appetite, and all those drives which are likely
to make you go astray, all those impulses which drive commerce and

business. Plato thinks that people who are driven by commerce are
driven by desire, by those needs that come from the chest on down.
The majority of people in the world are related to this lowest part of
the soul.

At one place in the Republic, Plato compares the appetitive part of
the soul to a many-headed beast, the spirited part to a lion, and the
rational part to the man within us — the inner human being. These
three parts of the soul correspond to three classes in the city of the
Republic: the philosopher-king, who is the rational element of the
Republic; the guardians or soldiers, who are the spirited element and
who obey the orders of the philosopher-king; and the masses — hoi
pollot, Greek for "the many" — who have their function. Someone
has to grow the food and conduct trade. That is part of the city, just
as appetite is part of the soul. But they ought to be obedient to the
higher powers.

Conclusion: A More Complex Picture of the Soul

When the higher powers are not in control, things go wrong in the
city — and likewise, when desire runs rampant, things go wrong in
our souls and in our lives. But notice what has changed between the
Phaedo and the Republic. In the Republic, the thing that goes wrong
is not simply the body. The source of evil, the source of our
problems, is not the body, but the lower — more bodily-oriented —
part of the soul. That gives us a more complex picture: not just a
pure soul and a dirty body, but a soul that is a complex unity, one
that needs to be ordered and governed in a certain way, and which
sometimes falls into disorder. How that governance is supposed to
work is a major theme of the Republic.

Lecture 7: Aristotle, Metaphysics
Professor Philip Carey

Introduction: The Greatest Mind

Talk about a great mind. Aristotle would be my candidate for the
greatest mind that ever lived. Consider the achievements of this one
man. In addition to being the second most important and influential
philosopher in the Western tradition after Plato, he also invented the
science of logic. Think of that as an achievement. He was not the first
person to think logically, but he was the first person to formulate the
rules of logic and give it a name. And he did not stop there. He
invented physics — that is, he formulated physics as a branch of
human inquiry and gave it its name. As | mentioned earlier, he also
gave the name to the inquiry called metaphysics. And in a substantial
sense, he invented the sciences of zoology and botany as branches
of human investigation. He started a whole range of disciplines. His
mind was large enough to inaugurate logic as a form of inquiry on
the one hand, and botany on the other. It is awesome to read
Aristotle — to encounter an intelligence that great.

Aristotle at Plato's Academy

Aristotle was not only the second most influential philosopher after
Plato; he was also Plato's student. He came to Plato's Academy as a
young man. The Academy is, in fact, the very first thing to be called
an academy — except for an olive grove outside of Athens, which
was the original site. Plato bought that olive grove and used it as the
place of his school, and that is why it is called the Academy.

The greatest student in that first Academy was Aristotle. And it must
have been a remarkable place, because Aristotle came into it and

seems from the beginning to have been a critic of Plato's pet theory
— the key theory of Plato's metaphysics, the theory of the forms,
which we discussed in the previous lecture. There are scholarly
disagreements about the history here, but | take the view, along with
many other scholars, that Aristotle was a critic of the theory of forms
from the start. He was the son of a physician, and he had been raised
with an appreciation for inquiry into the physical and natural world.
He wanted philosophy to be concerned with those things. He was
not so impressed with this otherworldly speculation about eternal
entities that you cannot investigate with your hands, eyes, and ears.

Aristotle was looking, in essence, for a theory that would help us
understand things that grow and change — things like human
bodies, things like the subjects of botany and zoology. His criticism
of Plato was that the theory of forms is perhaps wonderful as
something to contemplate, but it does not help explain the changing
world.

This contrast is captured beautifully in a famous painting —
Raphael's School of Athens — in which Plato and Aristotle stand
together. Plato points upward toward the world of the forms.
Aristotle argues with him, his hand pointing forward: Look out there,
Plato — thts ts what we need to understand. Looking up there doesn't
do it. There may be forms up there, but what we need to understand ts
here. We need a science of changing things, an understanding of how
things change and grow.

Physics: The Science of Moving Things

The word for this science is physics. Physics is a word that Aristotle
more or less invents as a name for a certain kind of inquiry. It comes
from the Greek word physis, meaning nature. And a physis is a certain
kind of thing. When you ask, in Greek, what kind of thing something

is, you ask: what physis is it? The root of physis is the verb phuo,
meaning to grow. So you are asking: what kind of growing thing is
it? How does it grow? How does it come into being? How does it
change? What are the rules and laws of its changing and growing?
That is what physics is about — how things move, grow, and change
according to their own nature.

It is natural for a stone to fall. It is natural for an animal to grow from
infancy to its adult form. It is natural for fire to go upward. It is
natural for human beings to reason. What Aristotle wants to
understand is how these changes, growths, and movements happen.

Here | need to point to an enlarged sense of the word movement.
Aristotle's term for movement is a general one that includes all kinds
of change and growth. A child growing from childhood to adulthood
is, for Aristotle, undergoing a movement in this broad sense. What
we would normally call movement — movement through space —
Aristotle calls locomotion, or local motion, meaning change in place.
That is only one kind of motion. Movement, in Aristotle's sense,
includes growth and even growth in understanding. So with this
broad concept of movement, we can say that physics for Aristotle is
the science of movement — a knowledge of processes of change in
the natural world. And this is precisely the kind of thing that Plato's
forms cannot explain, because the forms are unchanging. The forms
might tell us something about eternal structures, but they do not tell
us about the changing world.

Aristotle's Critique of Plato: Form Brought Down to
Earth

Aristotle's key point is that the forms do not exist separately from the
changing world. Plato was wrong about that. Aristotle corrects the
theory of forms and insists that forms are not separate from this

changing world — they are embodied in material things. That is
Aristotle's theory of form, and it is where we must begin in
understanding his metaphysics.

Aristotle starts with Plato's theory of forms, criticizes it, and
essentially tries to bring the forms down to earth. If we are to
understand the changing world, form has to be part of that world. It
has to be embodied in matter.

Matter — a word that can also be translated as material — is the
stuff out of which something is made. As a house is made of wood,
the wood is the matter, or material, of the house. So for Aristotle, a
natural thing — and all natural things are changing things — is a
composite or compound of form and matter. It is something which
has a form and is made out of something else.

Consider a wooden house. The wood is its matter, the material out of
which it is made. But the house is the form, because the form is what
the thing ts. A pile of wood is not a house. If you say that a house is
wood, you have said what its matter is, but you have not said what
its form is — you have not told us its structure or its purpose. To say
what the house is, you must say not just "wood," but "house." House
designates a form; wood designates matter. By the same token, the
form designates the essence or nature of a thing. The answer to the
question "what is it?" is the form, which Aristotle also calls the
essence. The what it is is house. The what it is made of is wood.

Form, Essence, and the Difference Between Art and
Nature

Now, the house is not exactly a natural thing — it is an artificial
thing. This is a distinction we get from Aristotle. There are natural
things, and there are artificial things. The difference is that natural

things grow — like a baby animal becoming an adult — whereas
artificial things are made by an artist. The fundamental contrast is
between art and nature.

Aristotle uses the word art in its original Greek sense: techné. Art
does not mean only fine art or beautiful things. It also means the
making of useful things, like houses. The house is a product of art,
and the carpenter who makes it is, in the literal Greek sense, an artist
— or, as we would say, an artisan. The house comes to be through a
process governed by an artist, unlike a natural thing for which there
is no artist. That is the difference between things made by art and
things that grow by nature.

The reason | start with the example of an artificial thing is that it is
actually simpler to understand. Nature is more complicated. What
Aristotle is doing — and many scholars agree with this — is taking
how things work with art and using it as a model for how things
happen in nature. In both art and nature, what something ts is its
form, and what it is made of is its matter. You and | are made of flesh
and bone, but we are not simply flesh and bone. We are human. The
human is the form. The house is made of wood, but it is not just
wood, because a pile of wood is not a house.

It is Important to note that the form of the house can exist separately
from the house itself — namely, in the mind of the carpenter, like a
kind of blueprint in his head. That is the same form, Aristotle thinks,
as the form of the completed house. This illuminates a great deal
about what form is for Aristotle: it is the thing in the mind of the
artist which Is also realized in the material. That is what form is — the
very same structure that exists in the wood also exists in the artist's
conception before building begins.

Form in Living Things: The Soul as Form

Now we can make the great leap from form in artificial things to
form in natural things — and especially form in living things, in
things that grow. The house comes into being through a process
supervised by an artist. A natural thing grows by its own power,
through its own internal nature, by an intrinsic principle of growth.
That means the form in a natural thing is not in the mind of any
external artist. The form is intrinsic and internal to the organism.

Think of a living thing — say, a puppy. It has this form called dog. It is
a dog. But a dog is not the same thing as what a dog is made of. A
dog is not the same thing as flesh and blood. Consider what would
happen if the dog died: you would have flesh and blood, but not a
living dog. Here we arrive at a crucial notion. When you have a
corpse — flesh and blood but no life — what you have is the matter
of a living thing, but not its soul, not its essence or form. Aristotle
therefore identifies the form of a living thing with its soul, its life.

The Greek word psyche, which means soul, can also be translated as
life — and often is, including in the New Testament. The difference
between a corpse and a living thing is that the corpse no longer has
its psyche, its soul, and therefore no longer has its essence. The
corpse of a dog is not a dog — it is just flesh and blood, just as a pile
of wood is not a house. So the form of a living thing is its soul.
Another way of putting it: the dogginess of a dog, the thing that
makes a dog a dog, is its soul, which is also its nature. Likewise, the
humanness of a human being — the thing that makes you human
and not merely a pile of flesh and blood — is your soul. That is your
essence and your nature. Human nature is soul. Human nature is
essence — the human essence.

Types of Souls

Once again, as so often in Aristotle, we are using a word differently
from how we use it in the modern world. For Aristotle, every living
thing has a soul, because the soul is simply the life of a thing. So
plants have souls. No one in ancient Greece would have been
surprised to hear this, because soul simply meant the life of the
thing. Plants have souls, but what they do not have is a rational soul.

Most of the time today, when we talk about souls, we are thinking of
rational souls — immortal souls, those Platonic souls. But for
Aristotle, every living thing has a soul, because without soul the body
is a corpse. Animals have souls. Plants have souls. Human beings
have souls. But they have different kinds of souls.

Only human beings have rational souls — souls capable of reason
and thought. That is why human beings are defined, by Aristotle, as
rational animals. Other animals are not rational. My dog is an
animal and she has a soul — not an immortal soul, and not a rational
soul. She cannot think, reason, or do mathematics. But she can move
around. She has what we might call a locomotive soul, as all animals
do. Plants do not have locomotive souls — they cannot move from
place to place. Animals do. My dog is, more precisely, a brute or a
beast — the term for a non-rational animal, one that moves around,
perceives, and eats, but cannot perform the higher functions of
thinking or understanding in the distinctively rational sense.

So to summarize: plants have nutritive souls — they take in
nourishment and they grow, but they cannot move from place to
place. Animals (brutes) have locomotive souls in addition to nutritive
functions — they can move around and eat, but they cannot reason.
And human beings are the rational animals — we possess all of these
capacities plus the distinctively rational one. Souls are therefore
defined by the kinds of activity they are capable of: the capacities for

movement, growth, and change that are inherent in a given kind of
living thing.

The Four Causes

At this point we have grasped roughly three-quarters of Aristotle's
notion of form. We can now turn to the key formula of his physics —
what he calls the four causes.

Aristotle's physics, his science of moving things, is organized around
these four causes. Two of them we have already discussed: form and
matter. The four causes are Aristotle's four modes of explanation. If
you want to ask why something is the way it is, you can refer to any
of these four explanatory factors. The names — efficient cause, final
cause, formal cause, and material cause — were assigned by the later
Aristotelian tradition, not by Aristotle himself.

Material Cause

The material cause is what something is made of. For a house, that
is the wood. For a human being, it is flesh and bone. This is the
simplest of the four causes to understand.

Formal Cause

The formal cause is what something is — its essence, its structure,
its defining nature. The form of the house is house; the form of a dog
is its dogginess; the form of a human being is its humanity, its
rational nature.

Efficient Cause

The efficient cause is what the later tradition would call the cause in
the narrow modern sense — the thing that makes another thing
happen, that brings something into being or sets it in motion. If |
throw a ball, | am the efficient cause of the ball's movement. If | am a
carpenter building a house, | am the efficient cause of the house. If |

am a parent, | am the efficient cause of my child — | have set that
child's growth in motion by begetting the child. The parent is the
source of the motion called growth, and through that efficient
causation the child inherits its form, its humanity, from the parent.
Likewise, the puppy gets the form of dogginess from its parent.

In certain versions of modern materialistic physics, efficient cause is
essentially the whole story — everything is explained in terms of
cause-and-effect chains. But for Aristotle, efficient cause is only one
of four. It is not the whole explanation.

Final Cause

The fourth and perhaps most distinctively Aristotelian cause is the
final cause — what Aristotle calls the end, or technically that for the
sake of which something happens. The end for Aristotle does not
mean merely the termination of a process. It means the completion
of a process or a movement — its goal, its purpose. The completed
house is the end of the process of building. The movement of
building is aimed toward the complete house. The complete house Is
the end of that movement, not merely in the sense of where it stops,
but most fundamentally in the sense of what the process is for.

Likewise, the adult form of an organism — the adult dog — is the
end of the process of growth that begins when a puppy Is conceived.
The adult human being is the end, the goal, of the process of
physical growth. The final cause is called final because it designates
the end of a process conceived as the goal of that process.

Notice that the final cause is closely connected with the notion of
form. The form of the house is the goal toward which the carpenter
is working as he builds. The adult form of the dog is the goal of the
process of growth. In order to understand a form, you have to
understand it as the end of a process — as a goal.

Putting the Four Causes Together

Together, the four causes give a complete explanation of how things
change and grow and move. Aristotle believed that previous thinkers
had each grasped a part of this picture. Thales said all the world is
made of water — he was talking about material cause. Plato talked
about form — and that was important, but he neglected efficient and
final cause. You have to put it all together, Aristotle thinks, and what
you get is physics as he conceives it.

To see how the four causes work together, consider the house: the
formal cause is the form house, the structure and purpose that
defines what a house is; the material cause is the wood; the efficient
cause is the carpenter; and the final cause is the purpose — the
complete house considered as the goal of the whole process of
building. The formal and final causes can overlap very dramatically in
Aristotle's scheme.

Likewise, for the process of natural growth: the parent is the efficient
cause; the adult form is also the final cause; and the flesh and blood
are the material cause. The formal cause is the nature of the creature
— its dogginess, or its humanity.

Potentiality and Actuality

One more crucial element of Aristotle's scheme: potentiality and
actuality. Consider again the relation between child and adult, which
is SO important for Aristotle's understanding of nature. The child is
potentially an adult. When you deal with a child, you have to deal
with how the child actually is at present, but you also have to deal
with the child's potential. It would be terrible to treat a child as if the
child would never grow up. Education is treating a child in light of its
potential for becoming an adult.

Movement is therefore this process in which the actual is already
potentially present. The child is not just a babbling, dawdling
creature who understands little — the child is going to become an
adult, and you must deal with that child in a way that respects and
engages that fact. Movement, in Aristotle's sense, is the process from
potential to actual. In some sense, when something is in the process
of movement, it has both the potentiality and the actuality within it,
and the form is what governs that transition.

The house, when it is being built, is moving toward the complete
house. The infant animal is growing toward the adult form.
Therefore, the form is already present — but present as potential,
which is precisely what motivates growth and movement. The puppy
is already a dog, but its dogginess Is initially there in the mode of
potential, orienting its growth.

This same logic extends even to non-living things. A stone falls
because the natural place for a stone is down on the earth. Fire goes
upward because the natural place for fire is up. There are natural
movements all over the world — not just the natural movement of
growth from childhood to adulthood, but the natural movements of
inanimate objects seeking their proper places.

Teleological Physics

For Aristotle, therefore, the whole world moves according to final
causes — according to purposes. The whole world is oriented
toward achieving forms, toward fulfilling purposes and reaching
ends. Movement is always purposeful.

This stands in sharp contrast to modern physics, where things
happen because of efficient causes, laws, or formulas — but not, in
most cases, for any purpose. For Aristotle, things always move
toward an end, if they can. Sometimes they are forced to move

against their nature — a stone thrown upward is moving against its
natural direction — but nature eventually takes over and it falls back
down. Things head toward their proper end. Things go where they
belong.

Aristotle's physics is therefore a teleological physics — a physics in
which things head toward their telos, which is Greek for end or
purpose. Things have a purpose in Aristotle's world. It is still a
metaphysical world, a world where form and true being determine
what things are and what they are to become. But it is not Plato's
world. It is not quite so otherworldly. Yet in another sense it is more
purposeful — more meaningful — than the world of modern physics.
Every movement has a purpose, an end, a meaning. That is the
difference between Aristotle's world and the world of modern
physics.

Lecture 8: Aristotle, Politics
Professor Dennis Dalton

Introduction: Plato's Student and Best Critic

In this lecture on Aristotle's politics, we meet a person who was
Plato's student and Plato's best critic. We will be focusing on
Aristotle's critique of Plato, because it is the most profound critique
that was offered in the tradition of Western political philosophy.

Who was Aristotle? He was born in 384 B.C., forty-four years after
Plato. He was born not in Athens but in Macedonia to the north, and
he came to Athens at age seventeen to study with Plato at the
Academy, where he remained for twenty years. It seems to me
remarkable that Plato's student Aristotle should have become not
only his most distinguished pupil but also his best critic. That is itself
a comment on Plato's ability as a teacher — that he could produce a
person of this magnitude. It is sometimes said that Plato is
excessively dogmatic or intransigent in his views. But if he were that
rigid, he could not have produced someone of Aristotle's worth: a
student capable of reflecting on Plato and criticizing him after having
studied with him for a full twenty years.

After Plato's death, Aristotle founded his own school right next to
Plato's in Athens, called the Lyceum. There he wrote several treatises,
but the one we focus on now Is called The Politics. He wrote The
Politics not only to refute Plato but also to set forth his own political
theory. And it became such an important political theory that it has
been said all of us since, in the Western tradition, are either with
Plato or with Aristotle — either Platonists or Aristotelians. It is for us
to decide which of these two great philosophies we prefer. Our

purpose today is to look at one philosophy against the other — to
compare, but above all to contrast.

Observation vs. Speculation: The Empirical Method

The Politics of Aristotle begins with the words, Observation shows us
— and then Aristotle goes on to argue that through empirical
analysis, that is, through the observation of concrete phenomena, we
can infer certain facts and conclusions. That is important because
observation is a term that does not occur very often in Plato's
Republic. Plato is concerned with deducing great axioms, as one
would as a student of geometry or physics. From Aristotle's point of
view, by contrast, one infers ideas as a biologist or chemist would —
looking at the human species as a laboratory and understanding it
through empirical analysis. It is not for nothing that Aristotle's father
was a doctor and that Aristotle studied biology deeply. It was the
empirical method used by the biologist that fascinated Aristotle.

Unlike Plato, Aristotle seeks the truth by examining the actual rather
than speculating on the ideal. This contrast is magnificently
illustrated in Raphael's painting, at the center of which stand Plato
and Aristotle. Notice the gesture of each figure. Plato points upward,
his finger toward the heavens, as if to say to Aristotle: The truth lies
with the ideals, with the archetypal patterns, with those noble eternal
truths of justice, beauty, and goodness that are the abstract reality we
must study. Aristotle's gesture is equally significant. His hand is
outstretched, and he looks ahead rather than upward. He surveys the
world of reality around him, gesturing at once toward that world of
facts and concrete phenomena. | also think that gesture conveys
moderation — as if Aristotle is saying to Plato: moderation in all
things, the golden mean, is what we must strive to achieve. He seems
to be saying: Cool it, Plato. You have gone to extremes in the Republic.

Let us think in terms of reality so that we do not take fanatical,
extremist steps toward the kind of revolution you propose.

Aristotle believes, then, that the best we can attain is not an
otherworldly republic, a utopia, but rather a mean of moderation
here on earth. And this mean — this moderate but good life — can
be achieved only if we cease building imaginary utopias and
concentrate instead on the very practical goal of the right kind of
state: one that is attainable, workable, and practical.

The problem with Plato, according to Aristotle, is that he is a
revolutionary. He Is trying to change the world so that we may attain
an impossible dream, a model that can never exist. For all his
emphasis on reason — and Plato is certainly identified as a rationalist
— Aristotle sees Plato as wholly irrational and unreasonable in the
demands for change he sets forth in the Republic.

The Three Waves: Aristotle's Response

We want now to examine Aristotle's response in The Politics to the
three waves of change we reviewed in the lecture on Plato. These
three tidal waves of revolutionary transformation were meant to
sweep over the old corrupt polis and transform it into the republic.
Let us examine the arguments Aristotle actually makes, looking
directly at his text. One can only wonder whether, during the twenty
years Aristotle spent with Plato, this debate actually occurred face to
face, or whether Aristotle came to these conclusions only after
Plato's death. In any case, political theory is exciting precisely
because it contains these debates — this conversation among the
greats — and when we look at Aristotle and Plato together, we find
them Joining issue on vital, vital problems.

The First Wave: Women in Political Life

The first of Plato's revolutionary proposals was that women could
rule. This was so extreme that nowhere in the ancient world had
anything remotely like it been suggested or approached. In Asia as
well as in Greece, women were seen as necessarily belonging to the
family and the home — involved only in private life, with no rights of
property. Sometimes, of course, women were despised and treated
as Slaves. Aristotle will not argue that this should be so. He wants
women to be respected as faithful wives and good mothers. But
when he argues against Plato on this issue, he is arguing against a
truly revolutionary step. Remember, Plato had argued in the Republic
that the nature of women should not disqualify them: just because
they are sexually different from men does not mean they cannot be
qualified leaders. If women were only given the opportunity to
become educated in politics, Plato argued, they could demonstrate
their abilities and compete for positions of political power.

Aristotle denied all of this. He asserted, in accord with the
conventional wisdom of ancient Greece, that the best place for
women is in the home, serving in their natural capacities. Women
deserved respect and honor in this function, because it was clearly
indispensable for any society to survive. But Aristotle went further
and argued that women are by nature suited peculiarly for this task
— and he finds justification for this argument in his theory of the
soul and of human nature.

Since they are by nature suited for this position, they cannot change.
Here is what he says specifically in The Politics, on the nature of men
and women and the nature of the soul:

The soul has naturally two elements, a ruling and a ruled, and each
has its different goodness — one belonging to the rational and ruling
element and the other to the irrational and ruled. What is true of the
soul is evidently also true of the household and the state, and we may

thus conclude that it is a general law that there should be naturally
ruling elements and elements naturally ruled. The rule of the free man
over the slave is one kind of rule; that of the male over the female is
another. The slave is entirely without the faculty of reason. The female
possesses the faculty of reason, but in a form which must remain
ineffective.

This is her nature, Aristotle says. Her soul has a quality of reason in it,
but not a quality that can compare with the male. Men have superior
reason — they are superior by nature. Their souls have this stronger
element of rationality. Women, by the nature of their souls, are
meant to be ruled by men, the superior ruling force in society. This is
part of Aristotle's broader discussion of household management —
what we would call home economics — in which women have a
critical place, but are naturally ruled by the male.

He is fond of quoting a passage from the Greek poet Sophocles: A
modest silence ts a woman's crown. This saying, Aristotle contends,
implies a special form of goodness for women — a definite truth, but
a truth that does not apply to men. The particular goodness of
women is to serve, to be silent, and to perform their capacities in the
home — not to mix in politics, and under no circumstances to serve
in positions of rule. Plato's suggestion, then, is revolutionary to the
extreme, and it characterizes Plato's whole attitude: extremism.

The Second Wave: The Abolition of the Family and Private Property
The second of Plato's waves was to create an extended family of
rulers and to abolish, among the ruling class, both the nuclear family
and private property. These institutions were not to exist among the
ruling elite — the larger communal family would be liberated from
narrow family concerns and immune from nepotism and
possessiveness.

Aristotle replies, first, that this suggestion is entirely impractical and
undesirable. It is undesirable because, from Aristotle's point of view,
the family is natural. It is necessary for socializing individuals. It
produces values — trust, loyalty, affection — bonds that are vital for
the state. The family is one way to socialize individuals on the path
toward understanding the larger values of loyalty within the polis. We
meet the values of loyalty and trust first in the family. We learn
responsibility and obligation in the family. For Plato to sweep this
away would be to create a state with no values of this kind.

Aristotle continues his refutation of Plato by arguing that if we were
to institute the idea of an extended communal family, we would have
a group of individuals who are careless in their attitudes toward one
another. As he puts It:

What ts common to the greatest number always gets the least amount
of care. Men pay most attention to what is their own; they care less for
what ts common. Men are more prone to neglect their duty when they
think that another ts attending to tt.

In this vast extended family, each citizen may have a thousand sons,
but those sons will not belong to each citizen individually — any and
every son will be equally the son of any and every father, since in
Plato's state, fathers and mothers will not know their own children.
The result will be that every son will be equally neglected by every
father.

Aristotle also speaks eloquently of the spirit of fraternity. This spirit,
he says, is likely to exist in the very least degree in Plato's system,
precisely when family bonds are loosened:

In Plato's Republic, it would be merely a watery sort of fraternity. A
father would be very little disposed to say "mine" of a son, and a son
would be as little disposed to say "mine" of a father — just as a little
sweet wine, mixed with a great deal of water, necessarily produces a

tasteless mixture. So family feeling is diluted and tasteless when family
names have so little meaning as they have in a constitution of the
Platonic Republic, and when there is so little reason for a father
treating his sons as sons, or a son treating his father as a father, or
brothers one another as brothers.

Men care for their family or for their property, he says, when they can
truly identify with them as their own.

Aristotle then applies this same logic to Plato's proposal to abolish
private property. He attacks this with equal vigor:

We must conclude — whether we are considering family or property
— that to think of a thing as your own makes an inexpressible
difference. The satisfaction of a natural feeling brings pleasure, and it
may well be that regard for oneself, and by extension for what ts one's
own, is a feeling implanted by nature, not a random impulse. The
simple feeling of love for any of these things — family, property,
money — Is universal.

He adds that a very great pleasure is to be found in doing kindness
and giving help to friends, guests, or comrades, and such generosity
becomes possible only when property is privately owned. In a state
like Plato's, with all property held in common, no man can show
himself liberal or perform a liberal act, because the function of
liberality consists in the proper use one makes of property.

This is a valuable point: in order to be generous and charitable, you
must have property to give away. And Aristotle trusts that people
who do own property and accumulate wealth will in fact be generous
with it. He does insist that there must be a limit to the accumulation
of wealth — it should not become so exorbitant as to be useless. But
when you accumulate wealth within reasonable limits, you
accumulate the power to be generous and good to society. Allow the
institution of private property to continue and flourish, because it is

the material of good feeling, affection, and fraternity — the bonds
that family and property together sustain.

The important point, Aristotle is saying, is that Plato is unnatural in
his requirements. We like to own things, to possess them, to know
them, to give them away. To give all of this up is, from Aristotle's
point of view, to become something superhuman or less than
human. We must not do that. We must treasure our humanity, and
the value of family and property is part of that treasure.

The Third Wave: Concentrated Power and the Rule of Guardians
Plato's third wave was to place all power in the hands of the
guardians — the intellectual elite — so that the just state could be
constructed. Aristotle attacks this wave with vigor equal to his attack
on the previous two.

When we examine Plato's insistence that all power be concentrated
in the hands of a few — the rational guardians, those who can rule a
state justly — Aristotle sees an element of danger:

He makes one body of persons the permanent rulers of his state, thus
rejecting the principle — which | favor — of ruling and being ruled in
turn.

This system, Aristotle argues, must breed discontent and dissension
in the state. It is important to note how Aristotle, from a practical
point of view, says of Plato's supposed revolutionary change that it
will breed civil war. Those who do not hold power are bound to
revolt against the few who do. And remember, from Plato's point of
view, the vast majority of people in the state will have no political
power whatsoever. This can only produce immense dissatisfaction.

But Aristotle goes further than that. He argues that there cannot be
any happiness at all in Plato's utopia. The majority will be unhappy,
obviously, because they have no political power and no say in

government. But the philosophers will also be unhappy, because
they are deprived of family and private property — the things that,
from Aristotle's point of view, are the real stuff of life. Plato's chosen
few, his rational rulers and guardians, cannot have the family bonds
or the property that give life its meaning and satisfaction. So
Aristotle concludes: if you wanted to construct a state that is
universally unhappy, it would be Plato's state. No one — not even
those at the top, who are deprived of family and property — will be
fulfilled.

Aristotle's Alternative: The Middle Class and the
Politics of Moderation

Aristotle therefore proposes his own alternative — a much better
State, from his point of view, because it could actually work. It is a
state ruled by the middle class. The larger that class, the better.

Later in The Politics, Aristotle sets forth his mean of moderation: an
ideal state — but unlike Plato's, not an ideal suspended in the
heavens somewhere, but one that can be realized in this life. He says:

We want to consider what ts the best kind of state and the best way of
life for the majority of states and men. We will not employ, as Plato
does, a standard of excellence above the reach of ordinary mortals. We
shall only be concerned with the sort of life which most men are able
to share and the sort of constitution which it is possible for most states
to enjoy.

He refers here to his other great work, the Ethics — and indeed, The
Ethics and The Politics should be read together, because the Ethics
enforces the key principle of moderation in all things. If a truly happy
life is a life of goodness, and goodness consists in a mean of
moderation, it follows that the best way of life for the majority of

men is one which consists in a mean of the kind attainable by every
individual.

In all states, Aristotle says, one may distinguish three parts or classes
of the citizen body: the very rich, the very poor, and the middle class,
which forms the most moderate position between the extremes. As a
general principle, moderation and the mean are always best. The
middle class is most ready to listen to reason, Aristotle argues,
because the middle class has a stake in the system — specifically, a
stake in owning property. Those who belong to either extreme —
whether the over-wealthy or the over-poor — find it hard to follow
the lead of reason.

It ts clear from our argument that the best form of political society ts
one where power is vested in the middle class, and that good
government ts attainable in those states where there ts a large middle
class. It is therefore the greatest of blessings for a state that its
members should possess a moderate and adequate property. Where
some have great possessions and others have nothing at all, the result
is either extreme democracy or an unmixed oligarchy.

What Aristotle wants is neither an oligarchy nor a pure democracy,
but something in between, which he calls a polity — a state ruled by
the middle class. How does this differ from the democracy he knew
in Athens? In Athens, every male citizen could vote regardless of
literacy or property. Aristotle wants to restrict political participation
to those who have property and some education. Where the middle
class is large, he argues, there is least likelihood of faction and
dissension.

Power Dispersed, Not Concentrated

This emphasis on the middle class is vital to Aristotle's theory of
power. From Plato's point of view, power must be concentrated

among a few — the most qualified, the rational, the highly educated
— and if it is so concentrated, the state can be ruled justly. A just
state can be attained only in this way.

From Aristotle's point of view, that concentration will necessarily
result in tyranny and despotism. If power is concentrated absolutely,
it means absolute corruption. No group of individuals can be trusted
with that much power. His very considered and realistic critique of
Plato's theory of power rests on this practical assessment: the middle
class represents those people who can be trusted with power
precisely because power is dispersed among them and not
concentrated in the hands of a few.

The contention that absolute power corrupts absolutely is vital to
Aristotle, and he is among the earliest to suggest it in countering
Plato's argument. He is sometimes seen as the great-granddaddy of
capitalism, since he champions the bourgeoisie and the middle class.
But | prefer to see him as a philosopher of moderation — his hand
outstretched toward the mean, saying to Plato: Do not go to those
extremes. Extremism means fanaticism and dogmatism. Think instead
of reason — reason among those of the middle class who can
administer the kind of government we both want: a government that
will bring about a practical state of justice.

Lecture 9: Aristotle, Ethics
Professor Philip Carey

Introduction: Aristotle and the Invention of Ethics

Aristotle was, as | have suggested, as great a mind as they come. He
invented all sorts of disciplines of human inquiry, including logic and
much of what we call biology. He also gave names to disciplines of
inquiry which had already existed but had never been systematized
until Aristotle got hold of them. He gave us the name metaphysics
for what Plato was doing with the Forms. He also gave us the notion
of ethics as inquiry into the good.

If metaphysics is inquiry into true being, then ethics is inquiry into
the true good. So metaphysics is to being as ethics is to good.
Aristotle's ethics, then, is an inquiry into this question: what is the
good? What is the good for human beings? And Aristotle emphasizes
that point. Just as he wants to take the Platonic Forms and bring
them down to earth — as in the famous Raphael painting, where
Plato points up and Aristotle points out — likewise, Aristotle wants
to bring the good down to earth. He wants to talk about what is the
good for human beings. He is not interested in that vision of the
Good outside the cave, up there in some heavenly, otherworldly
vision. He wants to know what is the good that human action can
obtain.

The Good as an End

When Aristotle talks about the good, he is, as so often in Greek
thought, using a familiar word in a rather different way than we are
accustomed to. For Aristotle, every good has the character of an end
in his sense of final cause — a purpose. Whenever you are trying to

do something, you are trying to achieve some good. If you are
building a house, the good toward which you are aiming is a house.
If you are looking for food, the good for which you are looking is
food. And you are always looking for something good: you want
good food, not bad food; you want to build a good house, not a bad
house. So the end of every process has the character of a good —
what we want out of that process is a good version of its end.

The good for Aristotle, then, has the character of an end: that for the
sake of which we do something. So the question what is the good for
human beings? boils down to the question what is the end — the
goal or purpose — of human life? What is our good? What are we
aiming for simply by virtue of being human?

Happiness as the Final End

In one sense, this question is fairly easy to answer. In one of his early
works, Aristotle says: don't we all want to be happy? Isn't it obvious?
Isn't it necessary? Everyone wants to be happy. Happiness is an end
that we aim for — it is the goal of many of the processes we engage
in. And not only is it an end; it is the final end, the last end. Once you
are happy, you are not looking for anything else. You look for food,
or a house, or any of these other things in order to be happy. So
happiness is a word that designates the end you get to and want for
its own sake. It is not a means; it is an end. Once you are there, you
have got what you want, and you do not want it for the sake of
something else — you want it for its own sake. That is happiness.

However, the word happiness does not mean for Aristotle quite the
same thing it means for us. Our word happiness translates the Greek
word eudaimonia, which does not necessarily designate a feeling. For
most of us in the twenty-first century, happiness designates a feeling
— when we say "I am happy," we mean "I feel happy." For Aristotle

and for Greek language in general, the word designated something
more like success. Think of success when Aristotle talks about
happiness. What makes a life a real success? That is another way of
saying: this is what we want when we want something for its own
sake, not as a means to some other end. Another possible translation
is human flourishing. What is a human life that is truly successful?
What is a human life that is really flourishing? That is what happiness
is.

Now, happiness may be a feeling. It may be that a successful human
life is a life that feels good. That was the answer given by
philosophers known as hedonists. Hedonist is a technical term — it
does not mean people who live a wild life. It means philosophers
who think that happiness consists in pleasure, that happiness is a
pleasant feeling. The Greek word for pleasure is hedone, from which
we get the word hedonist. So a hedonist is a philosopher who thinks
that the goal of human life is to feel good.

That is not an obvious answer to the question of what the goal of
human life is — not even an obvious answer to the question of what
happiness is, at least not if happiness means what Aristotle means by
it, or what all the Greeks meant by it. Happiness is success in human
life. Perhaps, if the hedonists are right, success in human life means
feeling good. But perhaps it means something else as well. And that
is the big issue.

So we have, in effect, marched through three questions: what is the
good for human beings? — which translates into: what is the end or
purpose of human life? — which becomes: what is happiness? These
are the questions that all philosophers after Aristotle agreed are the
key questions for ethics. What is the good? What is the end? What is
happiness? We know that happiness is the end of human life —
everyone agrees on that. But what ts happiness? That is where the
disagreements start. The hedonist says happiness is feeling good. But

there are other possible answers, and Aristotle, like Plato and many
others, is interested in giving a non-hedonistic answer to the
question: what is the good for human life?

Human Nature as Teleological

If we have a good or end for human life that is other than merely
feeling good, then that means that simply because of our human
nature — nature, that key Aristotelian word — simply because of our
human form and human essence, we have a built-in goal. There is an
end of human nature, a goal that we have simply because we are
human. It is not the same thing as any goal that we might choose.
We can choose all sorts of goals that might make us miserable —
imagine someone who is addicted, who craves things that make him
wretched. To say that we have an end or goal is not the same thing
as to say that we choose that end or goal.

Aristotle is interested in what is the intrinsic, objective goal of human
nature. Not a goal that we happen to choose — we might happen to
choose what will make us really happy, and that would be wonderful,
but sometimes we choose things that make us unhappy. So what
makes us happy is not something we choose; it is something intrinsic
to human nature. Human nature is a teleological thing — it has its
own built-in goal. Like the process of physical growth, a human child
has the built-in goal of becoming an adult. The child may choose
toward that goal or may choose against it; the child may even try to
do things that stunt its growth. But the goal of becoming an adult is
built into the child's physical nature.

So Aristotle is asking: what kind of goal is built into our nature as
rational animals? The goal of growing up is built into our nature as
animals — it is a goal we share with dogs and cats and horses, and it
is a real goal of human nature. But it is not a distinctive goal of

human nature. The distinctive goal of human nature must be the
goal associated with our being rational animals — our fulfillment as
rational creatures.

What, then, is that goal? That is the track Aristotle is following in his
inquiry into ethics. Before we get to the answer, let us think a little
more about the notion of a built-in end.

What do we have as examples of built-in goals? | have mentioned
the natural process of growth toward adulthood. Now let us turn to
an art, an artful or artificial process. Think about flute playing. That
has a built-in goal. A flute player, as flute player, has a goal: to play
the flute well, to live the life of a flute player who is good at this art.
Aristotle thinks there is something analogous in human nature as
such. Simply because we are human, there are certain things we
need to be good at in order to be happy. We have to be good at
being human. And there is such a thing as being good at being
human, just as there is such a thing as being good at playing the
flute.

Virtue as Human Excellence

The name for being good at being human is virtue — the Greek
word arete, which can also be translated as excellence. Virtue is the
same thing as human excellence. Virtue means being excellent at
being human, just as skill in playing the flute means being excellent
at flute playing.

You can see what Aristotle has done: he has connected the notion of
the good to the notion of an end in his physics, then to the notion of
happiness, and now to the notion of virtue — a common Greek term.
The Sophists, Socrates, and Plato were all arguing about this
question: what is virtue? What is it to be an excellent human being?

What does human excellence consist in? And so Aristotle now moves
to that very question.

At this point, Aristotle can give us at least a definition of happiness,
which will be the key definition in ethics:

Happiness is the activity of the rational soul in accordance with
virtue.

Happiness means doing what an excellent human being does. So
now we need to find out what human excellence consists in — that
is, we need to inquire into the nature of virtue.

Virtue as Habit Acquired by Practice

First, we will look at virtue in general, and then at specific virtues. For
Aristotle, a virtue is a good state of the soul — analogous to health,
which is a good state of the body. You have to have some name for
what is happening when a body is doing its work well, and that is
health. Likewise, virtue is our name for what is happening when a
soul is doing its work well, and in particular when a rational soul is
doing its work well. Aristotle classifies it as a habit — a habitual
state, meaning a learned capacity to do certain things well. Flute
playing and all other skills are habits in this broad Aristotelian sense:
not something we are always doing, but a stable capacity we possess
even when we are not exercising it. The flute player has her skill
twenty-four hours a day, even when she is sleeping. But when she is
playing the flute, she is exercising that habit, involved in the activity
— not merely the habit — of flute playing.

And remember, Aristotle defined happiness as the activity of a
rational soul in accordance with virtue. So the activity, the exercise of
virtue, is what happiness consists in.

Now here comes another key point: like other habits and skills, virtue
is acquired by practice. It is learned by habituation, just like flute
playing. Think about how this works. You start out wanting to be a
flute player but not yet being one. This is what Aristotle calls a
motion — a movement from potential to actual. You begin not
knowing how to do it, but you do it anyway. You do it badly at first,
but as you practice more and more, you get better. Eventually you
have the skill; now when you play the flute you play it well, and not
only well but easily. You enjoy it far more than you did the first time
you tried.

Acquiring a virtue is like acquiring a skill in exactly this way. How do
you acquire the virtue of courage? You do courageous things even if
you do not feel like it, even if you are not good at it yet. Like a
soldier facing battle for the first time — he practices the act of
courage. As he practices more, he gets better at it, and courageous
acts come more easily, just as flute playing comes more easily once
you have the skill. They come easier, and you take pleasure in them.
A courageous person takes pleasure in acts of courage. A just person
takes pleasure in acts of justice. Because virtue, Aristotle says, is
about how we relate to pleasures and pains. We take pleasure in the
right things if we are virtuous, and in the wrong things if we are
vicious — just as a skillful flute player takes pleasure in playing the
flute. But that is not where we start; we have to acquire the habit, the
virtue, first. And you start simply by practicing, even by doing it
badly. As you get better, the activity becomes easier and becomes, as
we might say, second nature.

It is also worth distinguishing the habit from natural capacities. The
ability to reason is natural — simply by growing up, we will learn
something about reasoning. Walking is a natural ability. But flute
playing is not natural in that sense; it is not inborn, and you have to
acquire it. In that sense it is artificial, an art. But in another sense,

these skills are natural to us because they make use of our natural
abilities. We are not born as flute players, but we can use our natural
dexterity to learn to become flute players. Likewise, virtue is in a
certain sense natural to us, because it is the perfection and
excellence most appropriate to a human being. It becomes second
nature. And in another sense it is natural in that it is the good state
that befits our soul, as health befits a healthy body.

The Four Cardinal Virtues

Now let us talk about virtues in the plural. Aristotle wants to say that
virtue in general is a mean between two extremes, and we can
illustrate this with the first virtue he discusses: courage.

Courage is a mean between the extreme of rashness on one hand
and cowardice on the other. In all the virtues, what happens is that
you exercise intelligent judgment to find the mean between two
extremes — to do the right thing rather than going to wild extremes
in either direction. A courageous soldier does not run away from
battle, but he also does not run toward the enemy simply to get
himself killed for no reason. He is neither rash nor cowardly. He is in
the middle. And in every particular situation, it takes intelligence and
judgment to find the right place in the middle. You need to know
when you should charge a machine gun emplacement and when you
should not. The courageous person is not the one who always
charges into machine gun fire — he is the one who knows when to
do it and how to do it in ways that will be successful.

Courage is often paired with the virtue of temperance, because
courage has to do with how we deal with pain and fearful things,
while temperance has to do with how we deal with pleasure and
desirable things — especially bodily pleasures. The temperate person
eats, drinks, and deals with sex in the moderate and appropriate way:

neither self-indulgent nor insensitive, neither excessive nor
completely cut off from the enjoyment of food, drink, and even sex.
A mature person deals with all bodily pleasures in the right,
moderate, temperate sort of way.

So courage and temperance are, in a sense, mirror opposites of one
another: courage is about how to deal with pain and fearful things,
while temperance is about how to deal with pleasure and attractive
things.

The third virtue is justice, the key political and social virtue. For
Aristotle, justice centers on what he called distributive justice —
giving people what they deserve, what you owe them. Jo each his
own or to each his due is the usual Greek definition, and Aristotle
basically approves of it. Justice means that everybody gets what they
deserve. A just person has the habit of knowing what to give to
people: respect to those who deserve respect, money to those you
owe money to, and so on through all the relationships of give and
take. Not giving too much, not giving things to the wrong people at
the wrong time, not taking what is not yours. Theft is a form of
injustice. Justice means that all the social relationships of give and
take are working out rightly, and that you yourself know in any
particular situation what the right thing to do Is.

The fourth virtue of the four cardinal virtues is practical wisdom —
the kind of intelligence you must have in order to know when to
charge the machine gun emplacement and when to retreat. A
courageous person will sometimes retreat, because If the enemy has
superior force and attacking will simply mean your death, then
attacking is not courage — it is rashness and _ foolishness
simultaneously. So in all of the virtues, practical wisdom plays a part.
You cannot have any virtue without it. You cannot be courageous
unless you know the difference between rashness and cowardice in
any particular situation.

The example | love to give of this is Captain Miller in the film Saving
Private Ryan — the captain whose men love him because he has not
only courage but intelligence. He knows when to attack and when to
retreat. He will not send his men into danger for no reason. They will
follow him into danger precisely because they admire his virtues of
both courage and practical wisdom. He Is a smart man, and that Is
not an accident — it is essential to the kind of courage he possesses.
Any captain you follow into battle must have not only courage but
practical intelligence. In fact, he cannot truly have courage without it.
If all he has is guts, his guts will lead his forces to their deaths, and
you do not want that.

Practical wisdom is the intelligence to make the right decision in the
right situation. So all of the virtues are about making practical
decisions in particular situations, and all of them require that kind of
intelligence. For Aristotle, virtue is not a matter of following rules.
There are rules of thumb, certainly, but virtue is not about applying
the right law in the right situation — because in any particular
situation, there may be more than one rule that applies. Be
courageous. Fight the enemy bravely. But also: Don't be stupid. Retreat
when you must. Both principles apply across all sorts of situations.
You have to judge which to act on when. All situations are particular,
and practical wisdom is the skill, the judgment, to make the right
decision at the right time and in the right place.

A useful analogy is a basketball player. Michael Jordan has to decide
whether to shoot or to pass. He knows which to do because he has
practical basketball intelligence — but that intelligence is totally
inseparable from his other skills. You cannot have the smarts without
the skills, and you cannot have the skills without the smarts.
Intelligence, then, is a crucial part of the moral life. You cannot be a
virtuous person if you are foolish. Foolishness here does not mean a
low IQ — it is a morally loaded term, as it is throughout both ancient

Greek ethics and the Bible. A foolish person is not a virtuous person.
A captain must have good judgment, must know what to do. A
basketball player who does not know when to shoot and when to
pass is foolish about basketball. And a human being who does not
know what decision to make in a crucial situation is simply not good
at being human. The practical intelligence of knowing what to do ina
particular situation is part of virtue because it is part of being
excellent at being human.

Friendship, Politics, and the Good Life

Aristotle's ethics is concerned with social life in two crucial ways.

The largest section of his book on ethics — the longest discussion —
is devoted to the topic of friendship. It is remarkable that a book on
ethics spends more time on friendship than on any other single
topic. The reason is that Aristotle believes a virtuous person needs
friends. Who would choose to live without them? As Aristotle asks at
one point: who would want to have the whole world and yet be
entirely alone? The virtuous person, the happy person, the person
who Is good at being human, will seek out friends for quite a number
of reasons.

Friends do good for one another. True friendship should be based on
virtue — you want a good person as your friend, not a bad one. True
friendship is a friendship among good people who are not merely
using each other for private advantage, but genuinely want to do
good to each other. You seek out a friend so that you can do good
for that friend and the friend can do good for you. The friend also
helps you understand who you are. The friend assists you in the task
of practical wisdom and virtue. As Aristotle famously says, a friend is
a second self — an alter ego, another I. In the friend | have a self who
mirrors me, in whom | can come to understand myself, someone who

wants to be good to me so that | can be good to this person in
return.

Evil people do not make good friends, because if you are friends with
an evil person, you may be required to do bad things in order to
cover up their wrongdoing. Good people never put you in that
position. That is why true friendship is based on virtue — you will
never have to do something bad in order to cover up the acts of a
good person who is your friend.

Finally, at the end of the Ethics, Aristotle turns to politics. Politics, it
turns out, is the overarching science or discipline that deals with the
human good. Ethics is primarily concerned with what happens in
individual souls. Politics is concerned with how you bring a whole
community of human beings together and enable them to live well.
Politics has to be the overarching discipline — it is about how you
Organize a community of human beings so that they can live well
together.

The crucial requirement for this is that they must be virtuous. They
must be good to one another, not bad to one another. And virtuous
people are good to one another. So the crucial task of politics, it
turns out, is to make laws that will help people become virtuous. For
Aristotle, politics is all about legislating morality — but not in the
sense of dictating what people must do in every situation, since that
is not how morality works for Aristotle. Ethics does not consist in
following rules. Rather, in politics you legislate in such a way as to
form people as virtuous people. The point of laws is not to tell
people what to do in every situation, but to help them become the
right sort of person — virtuous people, people who are good at
being human. That is what politics is for. And that is what human life
is for: to be good at being human, in a community where friendship
among good people is possible.

Lecture 10: Stoicism and Epicureanism
Professor Jeremy Adams

Introduction: The Historical Context of Athens

Epicureanism and Stoicism both arose in Athens in the fourth century
BCE. They arose as reasonable responses to the geopolitical
condition of that city. Athens had been, of course, the city-state that
served as the capital of a tremendously rich and arrogant empire —
apparently invincible, at least for the two generations that it held
sway over the commerce and territory of the eastern Mediterranean
world. Athens had also become, in the course of its imperial
dominance, the center of philosophical speculation. This process
began in 494 BCE, when the Persians destroyed the Athenian colony
of Miletus on the lonian coast, and most of the Milesians who could
escape made their way to the mother city.

By 338 BCE, however, Athens had been twice defeated in major and
consequential conflicts. By 400 BCE, after an exhausting two-
generation-long war generally known as the Peloponnesian War,
Athens had been defeated by the rival city-state of Sparta — a state
that was formally allied with the Persian Empire. Then, in 338 BCE,
Athens and its allies had to admit defeat by the large and expanding
kingdom of Macedonia, led by its hereditary king, Philip II. Although
Philip allowed Athens to retain its independence out of respect for its
cultural preeminence, Athens was compelled to accept a subordinate
position relative to this new type of more powerful state.

Philip's son, Alexander the Great, established an incredible empire in
southeastern Europe, western Asia, and northeastern Africa, which
broke up after his death in the city of Babylon in 323 BCE. It broke up
finally into three very large polyglot empires, and it was perfectly

clear by the year 300 BCE that the era of the Athenian-sized city-
state was over. Its day was done.

When the Romans in time — and especially in the second and first
centuries BCE — brought the eastern Mediterranean basin under
their imperial control by defeating the kingdoms of the descendants
of Alexander's generals, they enthusiastically adopted both
Epicureanism and Stoicism.

Epicurus and the Foundations of Epicureanism

Epicureanism was named for its founder Epicurus, a prolific writer
and an Athenian citizen of elevated social status. He was born in 341
BCE, three years before Athens' defeat by the Macedonians, and he
died some seventy years later in 270 BCE. Epicurus' largest book was
a discussion of nature, but happiness and ethical salvation were the
primary concerns of the school he founded.

Following the fifth-century philosopher Democritus, Epicurus taught
that the world is eternal and composed of atoms, which produce
everything through their infinitely variable combinations. Each
human being is so composed, and therefore we dissolve entirely
after the death of the body. There is no afterlife for humans in any
sense.

Epicurus was not an atheist, however. He believed that there were
gods — long-lived beings of such distant refinement that human
lives could mean nothing to them, and to whom it was therefore
obviously hopeless to pray, or to build temples, or to dedicate cities.
Human happiness must be found, therefore, in this life.

To achieve it, Epicurus recommended withdrawal from public life — a
recommendation that would have horrified Plato, and Aristotle even
more so. One should also withdraw from sexual involvement, from

the desire for fame (which was generally thought in ancient society
to be a rather lofty kind of goal), and from the desire for material
goods (less lofty to be sure, though certainly quite widespread). In
such a condition of disciplined withdrawal, which he called ataraxia,
Epicurus taught that one might find maximum pleasure and
minimum pain. One might — but one might not, in which case he
has little advice to offer.

Epicurus' elevated doctrines appealed to idealistic, cultivated men
and women of a world ruled by frantically competitive tyrants.
Epicurean societies were founded in many Hellenistic cities and also
in the Roman cities that followed them. The adherents of these
Epicurean societies were cultivated men and women who treasured
letters of advice from Epicurus himself. These letters of advice were
called epistolat, and the habit of writing epistles about matters of
spiritual and philosophical import was a very old habit by the time
the Apostle Paul took it up. Here, early Christianity plugs right into a
pre-existing mode of communication. The thought that Christianity
owes much to Epicureanism would not commend itself to many
teachers of Christian doctrine, but this represents a kind of fact in
terms of the medium that has carried a very different message.
Media can carry very different messages indeed.

Epicurus himself came to be seen as almost a kind of savior figure —
rather like Orpheus, the highly mythical figure said to have achieved
worders with his music. Epicurus was seen in that same light, except
by a more restrained, disciplined, and understated sort of clientele.

The Decline and Diffusion of Epicureanism

It is no surprise that many Epicureans settled for a much less
elevated, much less ascetic version of the master's doctrine, and
cultivated instead lives of refined intellectual and sensual pleasure,

avoiding politics as far as possible. Not surprisingly, the degree of
refinement tended to decline over time, and eventually there
emerged in the late Roman Empire — possibly in Hellenistic cities
even before Roman takeover — the kind of Epicureanism that is
widespread in American vernacular English today. The luxury food
and wine shop of Neiman Marcus is called the Epicure Shop, and it is
likely that Epicurus himself would have considered it somewhat over
the top, and certainly not conducive to the kind of pleasure he
proposed.

One should appreciate magnificent food, but never become sated or
gluttonous. One should appreciate magnificent wine, but never get
drunk. One should, if possible, have no sexual involvement at all, and
if that were not possible, then certainly restrain oneself and try not to
beget children into this deeply disappointing world.

That version of Epicureanism has, of course, gone much further still
— all the way to the point that Hugh Hefner claimed to be an
Epicurean in his Playboy Philosophy, a truly remarkable collection of
statements which the author, at least, thought deserved the title of
philosophy. One might well wonder what Epicurus would have made
of it.

Lucretius and Roman Epicureanism

The leading Roman Epicurean thinker and writer was Lucretius, who
was born in 99 BCE and died in 55 BCE — traditionally the very year
that Virgil put on the toga virilis, that is, became a man eligible to
vote at age seventeen. Lucretius thus lived a much shorter life than
many of our philosophical figures. It is worth noting how many of
these philosophers enjoyed remarkably long lives — a fact taken in
the ancient world as most unusual, given the demographics and
general state of epidemiology of the time. Long life was generally

seen as a sign that philosophy was a good thing, that if you pursued
philosophy, you might last considerably longer than those who
succumbed to passion and risked their spirits as well as their material
existence in doing so.

Even though he died in his forties, Lucretius wrote a very successful
book. His most famous and central work is a long didactic poem in
six books called De Rerum Natura — On the Nature of Things. It
devised a new philosophical vocabulary for the Latin language, a
work whose completion was taken up by Lucretius’ longer-lived
contemporary Cicero, who lived another twelve years and died in 43
BCE. Lucretius and Cicero could certainly read Greek, and many of
the most cultivated Romans could as well, but they desired to
translate this philosophia into their own language so that it would be
more accessible — accessible at least to the literate members of
Roman society, perhaps the upper five to ten percent of the
population.

Lucretius repeats Epicurus' teachings, and to some_ extent
reinterprets them — particularly, | think, his scientific teachings. The
theological teachings of Epicurus are certainly extended in Lucretius,
who is very clearly an atheist, although he will entertain the notion
that something called gods might exist, while arguing that this is
extremely uncertain and that they do us no good in any case. He was
very much concerned with ethical doctrine, which | think mattered
more to him in many ways than his scientific reasoning. Lucretius
appeals to the innate goodness of mortal human beings who have
been corrupted by ignorance and by the savage atrocities of religion.

His sense of these matters did not convince all of his numerous
readers, who admired the style of On the Nature of Things if not
always its content. Saint Augustine, for example, in The City of God,
gives us no sign whatever of any philosophical influence of Lucretius,
though many scholars suspect they can detect Lucretius’ style

beneath the magnificent prose that Augustine summoned up for that
work, written for what was surviving of the pagan elite in the fifth
century CE.

A moderate Epicureanism very probably became, in the minds of
various historians, the favorite philosophy of the Roman Empire's
upper classes, even if only in a somewhat clandestine fashion.
Epicureanism has never really been approved of by establishment
states or establishment religions, but it has survived very well
nonetheless. It had many medieval Christian adherents — Christian
perhaps deserving quotation marks. One thinks, for example, of the
Franklin in Chaucer's Canterbury Tales: a very pleasant man who lives
very well, who is gracious to everybody, who is a master of
hospitality. Everyone comes to his table, and he takes care to offend
no one. He has no particular ambitions in the political world. He is an
Epicurean to the core, even though he is going on a pilgrimage to
the shrine of a saint at Canterbury, as all decent people do from time
to time. And one can see this kind of lifestyle, wedded to a largely
unstated disbelief in most of the structures of official religion, as a
continuing theme in Western thought.

Lucretius remained favorite reading for gentlemen of the eighteenth-
century Enlightenment. His Latin poetry is quite remarkable, and it
became a sort of advanced schoolbook, continued to be read by
many gentlemen returning to their country estates or provincial cities
as dealing with the problems of that age. Many of his tenets were
revived by the utilitarian philosophers of the later eighteenth and
nineteenth centuries, and in a sense Epicureanism lives on today —
not simply in its more vulgar and sensually oriented forms.

Zeno and the Origins of Stoicism

Stoicism arose as a critical response to Epicureanism and also to
skepticism, another contemporary philosophical movement. The
founder of Stoicism was a very different person from Epicurus. He
was not an Athenian citizen, not particularly upper class, and not
even a Greek. He came from a Phoenician colony on the island of
Cyprus — the colony of Kition. He was born probably in the year 320
BCE, just a few years after the death of Alexander the Great, and
some twenty years after Epicurus, making him a _ younger
contemporary who consciously took on Epicurus in his teaching.

His school — it is hard to call it a school exactly, since Zeno refused
to have one in any formal sense — gathered in the agora, the public
meeting place of Athens. He taught particularly in a great public
portico decorated with famous mural paintings, known as the Stoa
Poikilé, the Painted Stoa. From this favorite place of meeting, the
Stoic school took its name. Zeno preferred unstructured dialogues in
the style of Socrates, whom he greatly admired, and he was
constantly attacking the dominant schools of thought — Platonism,
Aristotelianism, Epicureanism, and skepticism — by confronting them
with moral and logical paradoxes.

Although in his personal style Zeno advocated a return to Socrates,
he shared the Epicurean assumption that reality was essentially
material. This is, | think, one of the hardest points to grasp about
Stoicism. Although it seems to be a highly spiritual doctrine — one
that emphasizes moral conditions, moral realities, and moral duties
— Zeno was convinced that nature was material. In that sense, he
carried on some of the atomistic theory of Democritus. He did not
adopt all of Democritus’ theory of the constitution of matter, nor
certainly the moral consequences that emerged from Democritean
and atomistic thought, which was probably atheistic. But he did

believe that reality was material, which makes Stoicism a surprising
philosophy in many ways.

We should remember, however, that Zeno is a master of paradox.
Stoicism is the philosophy of paradox. Some say that among world
religions, Christianity has accepted paradox with more delight — has
reveled in it more thoroughly — than even Buddhism or
Confucianism. If that is so, it may help explain why, of all the later
Greek philosophies, Stoicism was the one most readily accepted by
Christianity, even before it achieved legal recognition in the Roman
Empire.

Stoic Ethics: Apatheia and Natural Law

Even though Zeno and his followers were convinced of the material
constitution of reality, they were committed primarily to finding
useful answers to the everyday moral problems of ordinary people.
Instead of the elite Epicurean ataraxia — that elite ideal of
disciplined withdrawal — the Stoics proposed the ideal of apatheia,
the etymological ancestor of the English word apathy, but about as
different from modern English apathy as one can imagine. Apatheia
means freedom from pathos, freedom from suffering — and also
freedom from the kind of suffering that is passion, freedom from the
passionate temperament and from passionate behavior. The social
consequences of apatheia were anything but the modern English
sense of apathy: the Stoics insisted strongly on one's duty to one's
community and one's fellow human beings.

Zeno wrote a Republic, which does not survive in full but is known to
us through fairly good descriptions and fragments. It was a republic
considerably more radical than Plato's. In that visionary work, Zeno
declared that all who recognize the spark of divine reason within
themselves must acknowledge the sway of natural virtue — a kind of

virtue which everywhere obeys the dictates of universal justice. If
enough people were ever to do so, the human race might be able to
advance toward the government of a worldwide city-state, a
cosmopolis, a polis of the entire cosmos, ruled by natural law. In such
a polity — such a cosmopolitan realm of justice — all human beings
would be brothers, enjoying a form of civic happiness free of
convention and free of the divisions falsely introduced by such things
as family bonds, schools and loyalty thereto, temples dedicated to
one or another god, law courts, military ambition, and money.

Here we have a remarkable image of a cosmopolitan society that is
disinterested in most of the things we tend to consider signs of
cosmopolitan culture — a fascinating ideal that appealed very
strongly to the Romans.

Cicero and Roman Stoicism

Cicero is, | think, the most striking proponent of Stoicism in the world
of Roman thought. He was born in 106 BCE and died in 43 BCE,
assassinated by the agents of Mark Antony as a consequence of one
of the Roman civil wars. Cicero was himself an eclectic in philosophy
— he liked to draw philosophical beliefs from various schools. He
was very strongly attracted by skepticism until he realized what the
moral consequences of skepticism had to be, and so he moved
toward Stoicism. We know that when he was in his late twenties, he
heard the lectures of a man named Antiochus of Ascalon at Athens,
who was attempting to restore the tradition of Stoicism in the first
century BCE, and Cicero gave him serious hearing.

Many Romans found Stoicism the most acceptable current Greek
philosophy, at least partly because of its respect for disciplined
military valor and also for its sense of a worldwide law. The Romans
were setting up a world state, and they rather liked the idea that

what they might be doing was preparing for the cosmopolis. The
Romans were always concerned about the fundamental morality of
their actions — one is aware, | assume, that the Roman Republic and
the Roman Empire never officially fought an aggressive war. Every
war those masters of warfare fought was declared a defensive action
by the Roman Senate before it got underway. We can look at the
Romans as among the most ruthless exploiters of military talent, but
the Romans themselves were troubled by this image and wanted
everything they did to be moral and just. If the long-range goal of all
these wars — in which much blood was shed, many slaves were
captured, and much wealth was taken back to Rome — was some
philosophical ideal state, a cosmopolis free of attachment to family
bonds, schools, military ambition, law courts, and money, then this
could stand as a fine justification for what the Romans were doing.
Obviously, this kind of idea lies behind the Roman Christian ideal of
the City of God, and behind more modern ideas of one-world
government, which has certainly not come anywhere near realization
at this point. So Stoicism remains a fresh philosophy, at least as
regards its greatest political prescription: the cosmopolis of Zeno's
Republic.

Cicero came to prominence from somewhere between Epicurus'
aristocratic background and Zeno's non-Greek and apparently
middle-class origins. He was what was called a novus homo, a new
man — meaning he was not a scion of the old Roman nobility. He
was from a very fine family in the Volscian region southeast of Rome,
in the area of Campania, and his family claimed descent from the
Volscian kings, which is entirely plausible, though they were certainly
not of old Latin blood. Cicero's father was wealthy, however, and
paid for an excellent education, sending Cicero to Athens, Rhodes,
and other places in the eastern world. He came back a lawyer, rose to
become consul — the chief executive of the Roman state — and was
especially interested in the Stoic conception of natural law, which

several of his works transmitted to medieval and modern Western
thought.

Seneca

Seneca was a native of the patrician Roman colony of Cordoba in
Spain, born in 4 BCE and dying in the year 65 CE, committing suicide
in his bathtub in response to commands from the Emperor Nero to
do so. Seneca's uncle was a very famous orator and lawyer whose
books on legally effective rhetoric continued to be read throughout
the Middle Ages and even into the modern period. Seneca himself
came to Rome with money, means, and ambition, and made for
himself quite a reputation and quite a considerable fortune.
Unfortunately, from the point of view of those who take Stoicism
very seriously, Seneca made most of his money from tax speculation
— lending money at staggering rates of interest on the expectation
that the borrower would profit enough from tax futures to be able to
repay him. He may, in fact, have had a hand in this indirect way in the
tragic rebellion of the British queen Boudica against the Roman
occupation of Britain under the Emperor Claudius.

Seneca had been appointed tutor to the young man who became
the Emperor Nero, and he attempted to teach him philosophy and
Greek — probably including the use of the Greek language for
elevated kinds of statements. He did not succeed very well in
teaching Nero how to be a Stoic, to put it mildly. He wrote a great
deal: prose treatises of a philosophical character, disguised as formal
letters to a friend, which would grow into full treatises on subjects
such as clemency, tranquility, or how to endure misfortune in a cruel
world. He also composed dramas, not so much for stage
presentation as for recitation — it is not at all clear that any of his
dramas were ever actually performed on stage until the late Middle

Ages and the Renaissance. Hamlet, famously, is very much in favor of
various of Seneca's plays, and they clearly became part of the
theatrical side of Renaissance education. Both types of work — the
dramas and the letters — circulated throughout the empire even
during Seneca's own lifetime and remained favorite items of school
reading for eighteen centuries.

As his pupil Nero turned against his advice, Seneca stoically endured
the danger of not complying with his pupil's preferences, and then
committed suicide with dignity — an entirely honorable act in the
ancient world, and particularly within the orbit of Stoic thought.

Marcus Aurelius

Probably the most impressive of the Roman Stoics, at least in terms
of rank, is the Emperor Marcus Aurelius. He was born around 120 CE
and died in the year 180, apparently one of the first victims of the
bubonic plague, which had just come out of India and was beginning
to ravage Europe. The bubonic plague — known in its later medieval
reappearance in the fourteenth century as the Black Death — was a
terrifying disease which at least dispatched its victims within just a
few days. We are told that Marcus Aurelius met his end stoically. In
fact, he did everything stoically, as far as we can tell.

He was trained as a young man to be a philosopher-king in
something like Plato's ideal, ruling over something very like a
cosmopolis. One can see that he harbored the thought that it would
be good for rulers like himself to turn the Roman Empire into a
genuinely Stoic cosmopolis. Unfortunately, reality meant that he had
to spend almost all of his fourteen-year reign — he was emperor
from 166 to 180 — on the river Danube, campaigning against the
incursions of Germanic peoples with evocative names such as the
Quadi and the Marcomanni. He did so successfully enough that

these peoples disappeared from history after his reign. He died, in
fact, very near the modern city of Vienna, at the Roman military camp
of Vindobona on the Danube, which lies today in the northern
suburbs of that later imperial city.

We know most about Marcus Aurelius through his book of personal
reflections, The Meditations, which some see as _ the first
autobiography, though most consider it something other than that.
At the end of a tough day, Marcus Aurelius would come back to his
tent and write down his thoughts about this or that moral problem,
this or that general issue — very rarely relating it to his own
experience that day or indeed at any point in his lifetime. The
Meditations are a series of reflections on general issues written by a
particular man who preferred to find validation in general principles
rather than in individual privileges. Since this man was the most
powerful ruler in the world known to West Europeans, West Asians,
and North Africans, that was a rather impressive kind of
commitment.

The most striking message one receives from The Meditations is its
sense of unflinching adherence to private and public virtue as the
only form of real happiness. Virtue is its own reward for Marcus
Aurelius, as indeed for the entire Stoic tradition. Rarely has that
conviction been more powerfully stated by a man of anything like
comparable power.

Although composed in Greek, The Meditations of Marcus Aurelius
have been read in Latin and in translations into modern languages
ever since they were written, and they enjoyed special favor among
eighteenth- and nineteenth-century European gentlemen struggling
with the demands of imperial rule. Many an English product of a
public school, or perhaps of Oxford or Cambridge, who was out in
India or in the British West Indies trying to rule an empire both justly
and at all, took great comfort in reading The Meditations. It is very

short, and one can read it in short passages — just as they were
composed, a short burst at a time — while ensuring that one's Greek,
or if one's Greek was fading, one's English translation with facing
pages, was still up to the task.

The Breadth and Egalitarianism of Stoicism: Epictetus

Some think that Epicureanism appealed to most upper-class Romans
of any philosophical inclination, though they often did not want to
admit it. But we must recognize that Stoicism was by far the most
publicly favored philosophical school. On the basis of the surviving
written evidence, Stoicism was the most widespread Greek
philosophy among the rulers and beneficiaries of the Roman Empire.

It was also remarkably egalitarian, which is a surprise given that the
Roman Empire was a very good deal for the top five percent of the
population. The clearest evidence of this is the career of the Phrygian
slave Epictetus, born around 55 CE and dying apparently in 135 CE,
who became a respected teacher of Stoic ethics. Freed by his master,
he moved to Rome, where his teachings were so popular that a
student compiled an anthology of his lectures called the Manual.
That work tremendously influenced the Meditations of Marcus
Aurelius — a remarkable arc, from slave to emperor, by means of
manumission and the delivery of lectures written down and compiled
by faithful students.

Epictetus believed that the only things truly in our power are our will
and our body. Our will is always free, and we must keep both it and
our body untainted. In that way, we will avoid pain, which is merely
external to us and cannot touch the inner citadel of a truly Stoic soul.

Lecture 11: Roman Eclecticism, Cicero and Polybius
Professor Jeremy Adams

The Nature of Roman Philosophy

It is time to talk about Roman philosophy. What is Roman
philosophy? It is very easy to dismiss it as entirely derivative —
entirely drawn from Greek thinking and from Greek thinkers. And of
course that is true. But there are two important things we should
remember about what the Romans did with the Greek philosophical
tradition. First, they tended to combine its various strands in new and
interesting ways, producing combinations that were not simply a
matter of Greek components assembled together, but those
components brought together with a new element, a new cast, a new
spin. Second, as has already been remarked, the Romans managed to
spread Greek philosophy, finding for it an audience far wider than
any of the Greek philosophers had originally expected.

The distinctive Roman style in philosophy — and it Is a style the
Romans can legitimately claim — could be described by three terms:
eclectic, synthetic, and syncretic. These three terms may sound very
nearly the same, but they are in fact quite different. Taking elements
from the various Greek schools and traditions — the eclectic process,
choosing from here and there — Roman thinkers would then
synthesize those elements into a new compound, a new mixture. The
entire process of eclectic choosing and synthetic blending goes by
the term syncretic, which is not simply a synonym for either of the
other two words but rather a description of the overall process that
involves them both as sequential steps.

If anything, Romans preferred broadly eclectic synthesis to the revival
of any single philosophical tradition. From about the second century

BCE onward, Greeks began to become Neo-Platonists, Neo-
Aristotelians, Neo-Stoics, and so forth, attempting to recover the
purity of one original tradition — and in doing so, if anything,
narrowing the appeal of that tradition to a larger clientele. The
Romans, by contrast, tried to combine these traditions, and not
merely to combine them but to give a new character to the very
combination.

Cicero: Life and Political Background

The most influential of the Roman syncretic philosophers was Marcus
Tullius Cicero, born in 106 BCE and dying on the seventh of
December, 43 BCE — a day which seemed to many conservative
Roman intellectuals a day of infamy, even back in that year, without
having to wait until 1941.

Cicero was what the Romans called a novus homo, a new man. He
was not a member of the old Roman nobility. His family was quite
distinguished as well as quite wealthy, claiming descent from the
kings of the Volscians to the southeast of Rome — and given the size
of those populations and the stability of social structures in that part
of the world at that time, it seems an entirely reasonable claim. The
particular stretch of country that he came from, the small city of
Arpinum on the slopes of the Italian mountains sliding down into the
Campania, the great flat plain south of Rome, was also the home of
Marius — Marius who would become the uncle-by-marriage of Julius
Caesar, a man of modest origins who rose by talent to be the
Supreme commander of the Roman army and then consul half a
dozen times in succession. In later centuries, that same stretch of
countryside would attract Benedict of Nursia, who founded the
greatest of his monasteries at Monte Cassino, and some centuries
after that it would be the home of the family of Thomas Aquinas.

From Marius to Cicero to Benedict to Thomas Aquinas, that particular
stretch of Italian countryside has been extraordinarily rich in thinkers
and figures of ethical idealism.

Cicero's parents sent him to study rhetoric and politics in Rome,
hoping he would make a political career for himself, and then sent
him east — to Athens and to Rhodes — where he loved to attend
the lectures of the various clashing schools, not only schools of
rhetoric but schools of philosophy of other kinds as well. When he
returned to Rome in his late twenties, ready to begin a practical
political career, he attached himself to the party of the optimates, the
aristocratic party, and especially to a faction within it derived from
the so-called Scipionic circle of the second century BCE. This circle
had gathered around the great Publius Cornelius Scipio Aemilianus
Africanus Numantinus, whose very name indicates his high standing
in Roman society — a group which favored Stoicism, and especially
not Stoicism alone, but Stoicism insofar as it could be blended with
old native Roman traditions of patriotism and virtue. Here already, in
the second century BCE, we see the Roman tendency to bring in new
philosophy not simply by copying it, but by fusing it with native
elements into something genuinely new.

While in Greece, Cicero had developed a particular interest in Skeptic
and in Epicurean philosophy as well. In political life back home, he
showed the same combining tendency. He displayed more sympathy
for the popular concerns of the rival party of the populares than the
right wing of his own party really liked, and he picked and chose
various positions, trying to bring together the various elements of his
own party and to find common ground at the center. Picking and
choosing what seemed to him good ideas and good attitudes was
simply his temperament, his personality — one that expressed itself
naturally in the kind of eclectically synthesized philosophy he was to
contribute to the future.

No doubt Cicero's sense of the wisdom of eclecticism was
heightened by the fact that during his lifetime the Roman Republic
was passing through its terminal phases of internal dissension and
dissolution. Civil war followed dictator followed civil war followed
dictator, finally culminating in the victory of Octavian — the nephew
and adopted heir of Julius Caesar — over his enemies, including
Brutus, and ultimately over his first ally and then rival, Mark Antony,
in 31 BCE. Twelve years after Cicero's death, the process had reached
its conclusion. Cicero was born into a world in which he already knew
that process was underway, and he was deeply distressed by it. Even
if he had been the sort of person who would have preferred to
adhere to one or another traditional doctrine or traditional party, he
saw that that was no longer possible. And if it was not possible, how
could it be good? At base, Cicero was a man of deep practicality —
moral as well as political.

Cicero's Career and the Philippics

Cicero's synthesizing eclecticism is most evident in his ethical
doctrines. It has been remarked that he splits the difference between
Epicurean ethics and Stoic ethics, and it is entirely fitting and natural
that he should do so.

From Stoicism, Cicero drew the principle that virtue is sufficient for
happiness — that virtue ts happiness, whatever atrocities the external
world inflicts upon us. And the external world was doing a very good
job of inflicting atrocities, not simply on conquered subject
populations but on the aristocracy of the Roman Republic itself, the
aristocracy of the party which at the moment was losing.

Appropriately enough, Cicero felt rather ill-treated by the Roman
political class after he had reached the pinnacle of legitimate political
power during his term as consul in 63 BCE. The consuls, as you will

recall, were the chief executives of the Roman state — a dual
executive, each serving for only one year, but wielding extraordinary
power during that year. Cicero was probably aware that he had
reached the consulship largely because of a serious split within his
own party. He was a kind of compromise candidate who would not
normally have been put forward in the regular course of things. He
then felt that he was rather badly abandoned by his party when
many of his fellow party members failed to back him up in the
aftermath of his actions against the conspiracy of Catiline.

Cicero therefore retired from public life for ten years. During that
period, he was invited by Julius Caesar to join the new triumvirate of
Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus, which was then positioned to take over
the direction of the Roman state, but he refused. He remained in
philosophical retirement, collecting and editing more than eight
hundred letters he had written to friends and family across the years,
and composing a whole series of treatises on practical philosophical
topics such as old age, friendship, the nature of the gods, divination,
and many other subjects as well.

Then, after Caesar's assassination on the Ides of March, 44 BCE,
Cicero briefly and brilliantly re-entered the political arena, directing
his attacks against Mark Antony. Octavian, imitating his adoptive
father's policy of trying to win Cicero over, found in Cicero a
sympathetic figure. When Octavian and Antony began to show signs
of strain between them, Cicero focused his attacks upon Antony
directly, and with tremendous courage delivered a series of speeches
in the Roman Senate attacking him — consciously imitating the
speeches of the great Athenian orator Demosthenes, who had had
the courage to speak out against Philip of Macedon several centuries
before. Just as Demosthenes’ speeches against Philip were called the
Philippics, so Cicero's speeches against Antony have been known by
the same name ever since.

Antony, understandably, hated Cicero and ignored whatever appeals
Octavian may have made for mercy. He had Cicero killed, and caused
his tongue and his hands to be nailed to the rostra — the speaker's
platform in the Roman Forum, upon which had long since been
mounted the prows of captured Carthaginian warships. It was an act
of terrible symbolic completeness directed against a man whose
power had lain entirely in words.

Cicero's Ethical Synthesis

From Epicureanism, Cicero drew the complementary principle that
the cultivation of refined and disciplined pleasure is not inconsistent
with virtue. The Stoics had given him the notion that virtue was its
own reward — and since the reward he actually got in life was
anything but comfortable, it was fortunate that he held that doctrine.
But from Epicureanism he drew the further principle that virtue need
not stand alone, that refined and disciplined pleasure could be
entirely consistent with virtue and constituted a valid source of
happiness.

To Cicero, rejecting such pleasure — which was certainly available to
him as a highly educated and very wealthy member of the ruling
circle of the world — seemed excessive and unnecessary. In the
Epicurean tradition, discipline certainly did not require asceticism.
They saw asceticism as irrational and unnatural.

Cicero appears to have lived a life consistent with this balanced
Stoic-Epicurean principle of pleasurable virtue and virtuous pleasure.
We know a good deal about his deep admiration for and closeness
to his wife, about his relationship with his family, and we have no
evidence of his indulging in inappropriate relationships of any kind.
We know that he treated his slaves with considerably more kindness
than was normal in that world, and the proof of it came at the very

end: when Antony's assassins closed in upon him as he was
attempting to escape in his litter — knowing, probably, that there
was no real point — his own slaves stood by him. That was most
unusual behavior in the ancient world, and a sign of the tremendous
decency with which this Epicurean Stoic had treated those
dependent upon him.

Cicero's Political Philosophy and Deontology

A similar combination of virtue and pleasure — and in fact a far more
thoroughgoing synthesis of the two — occurs in the realm of
political philosophy.

From Stoicism, Cicero drew a deep commitment to duty, drawing on
a form of moral reasoning that some called deontology, from the
Greek word deon, meaning duty or obligation. For Cicero, civic virtue
was essentially civic deontology — obedience to duty, whatever the
consequences. And he had to face quite severe consequences at
several points in his life, most brutally at the very end.

From Aristotle, however, Cicero drew certain ideas that made the
Stoic deontology to which he was attracted a good deal more
pragmatic, and, | think one must say, attractive. The kind of
pragmatism he found in Aristotle was not unprincipled. It was a more
or less consistent blending of principles already dignified by the
name of empiricism — essentially the study of what experience
actually shows us works in a consistent manner, and the application
of the results of that study to one's own behavior. In civic and
political life, which mattered most to Cicero in this regard, empiricism
meant examining what had actually worked over time and using
those findings as a guide to action.

Polybius: The Mixed Constitution and the Rise of
Rome

A major influence on Cicero's practical empiricism was the great
Greek diplomat, historian, and philosopher Polybius, born around
200 BCE and dying around 118 BCE. Polybius was a deeply patriotic
Greek who came to Rome in the year 167 BCE as a hostage. His
particular political faction in Greece had been defeated by the
Roman general Aemilius Paulus at the Battle of Pydna in 167 BCE —
a battle which gave Rome dominance over the European part of the
old Macedonian Empire, the most thoroughly Greek part of the
Hellenistic world that had dominated the eastern Mediterranean
since the death of Alexander the Great.

He was, however, extremely well treated by the Romans, who
respected him and who had a general policy of cultivating hostages
for later political connections. Polybius became a valued member of
the Scipionic circle, entering the formal staff of Publius Cornelius
Scipio Aemilianus Africanus Numantinus — who earned the title
Africanus just as his adoptive grandfather had, by finally defeating
Carthage, and who earned the title Numantinus by ending the last
significant rebellion against Roman rule in Spain with the terrible
siege of Numantia. This extraordinary man, born around 185 BCE and
dying in 129 BCE after a relatively short life of some fifty-six years,
was the first prominent convert among the Roman upper class to
Stoicism. He also cultivated the arts, sciences, literature, and poetry
of the Greek East, attempting to transplant them to Roman use —
sometimes having them translated into Latin, but in any case
endeavoring to make good use of them for the Roman future.

Polybius wrote a history that was, in a certain sense, the first
comprehensive history ever written. By comprehensive, | mean a
history that accounts for events throughout the world as Polybius

knew it. From our perspective this is decidedly Western — he Is
essentially talking about the Mediterranean basin — but neither
Herodotus nor Thucydides before him, the two other historians of
comparable stature, had attempted anything like it.

Polybius was absolutely fascinated by a fifty-three-year period
stretching from 220 BCE to 167 BCE, the date of the victory at Pydna.
In 220 BCE, Rome did not yet even control all of Italy. By 167 BCE, it
controlled what Polybius called the whole world. Direct rule over the
Asian coast and Egypt was not achieved until later, in the days of
Julius Caesar, but Rome did not really need direct control — it had
absolute dominance over those kingdoms to whatever extent it
desired.

What could account for the incredible rise of this republic in the
course of that half-century and more? Polybius goes back to recount
the great stories of the Punic Wars — the wars in which the
Carthaginians, called the Poenit by the Romans because they were
Phoenician in origin, fought the Roman Republic; the great wars
between the Scipio family and the family of Hannibal Barca. Polybius
shows himself a master of military narrative. But what really interests
him even more than the magnificent stories of Hannibal crossing the
Alps with his elephants is the story of character. The character of
commanders such as the Scipios, such as Hannibal and his heroic
brothers and brothers-in-law. The characters also of the peoples
these commanders led. The Romans come in for much praise from
Polybius on that score, for he felt that Roman character was of a kind
that Rome could endure any hardship and emerge victorious.

In Book Six of his Histories, Polybius goes further — becoming more
strictly political, more thoroughly philosophical in a practical sense.
He sets out to explain Rome's success not merely through its
character, but through its constitution, which he saw as a perfect
example of the mixed government that Aristotle had pragmatically

recommended: a combination of monarchy, aristocracy, and
democracy. In the Roman case, the monarchy was embodied in the
consulate, even though consuls served only a single year and
although there were two of them to check one another — for their
power within that year was tremendous, and in certain moments
could be as absolute as anything exercised by any Eastern tyrant. The
monarchy was checked, balanced, and blended with by the Senate,
from which practically all consuls arose. And the element of
democracy was present in the election to any significant Roman
office by the whole body of citizens — a democracy also conscious
of its rights, since the Romans had early on established the office of
the tribune, who existed specifically to protect the rights of the
common people. In this blend of consular monarchy, senatorial
aristocracy, and tribunician democracy, Polybius saw Aristotle's
practical political philosophy expressing itself in powerful and useful
ways.

Cicero's De Re Publica and the Dream of Scipio

Cicero agreed entirely, and he stated in his dialogue De Re Publica
that the Roman state so constituted had been the most perfect civil
society in human history — at least in the days of the magnificent
grandfather of the Scipio he knew, and even into the days of the
Scipio that Polybius had known, though Rome was already beginning
to slip by that point.

Cicero's De Re Publica is a fascinating work, written during his active
political career rather than during the ten years of philosophical
retirement at the end of his life. He sets it up as a dialogue among
people who actually existed, some of them people he personally
knew. He tells us he learned about this conversation from one of its
youngest participants, when that person was an old man governing a

Roman province in Asia with such virtue and dignity that the Asians
could scarcely believe it, and the Romans could scarcely believe it
either.

What Cicero does is reach back in time to the days of the great six-
named Scipio and describe a conversation at Scipio's country estate
about what would be the most perfect form of government. His aim
is to construct a kind of answer to Plato's Republic — with which he
did not agree — and also a response to Zeno's cosmopolitan
Republic, with which he did not entirely agree either. Instead, he
proposes a republic, a res publica, which has actually existed in
history, and which during the long course of its existence was the
most perfect state in human history and the most perfect state that
human beings could probably ever dream of creating. In his own
time, of course — the time of the dissolution of the Republic —
Cicero was acutely aware that this state no longer existed, at least
not in its inner substance and moral character, even if it still existed
On paper.

The dialogue comes to an extraordinary conclusion in the passage
known as the Dream of Scipio. Pressed by his friends to describe an
unusual out-of-body experience he has hinted at, Scipio reluctantly
tells of it. What Cicero is doing here is constructing a myth in the
manner of Plato — a myth meaning, in this sense, not a falsehood
but a statement that goes beyond ordinary rational speech into the
higher reaches of the moral imagination.

In the dream, the younger Scipio Africanus — our six-named hero —
is brought up to visit his dead grandfather, the elder Publius
Cornelius Scipio Africanus. What the elder Africanus then does is
show his grandson the world. He lifts the dreaming younger
Africanus to a still point high above the earth from which they can
survey the entire globe — a view very much like the view of the
globe which Socrates describes in the Phaedo. From this vantage,

Carthage, the old enemy now restored to commerce, and the Roman
Empire itself seem rather puny. The Romans, rather like Socrates’
Greeks, are inhabitants of only a small portion of the earth, though
they imagine that they fill the globe with their virtue and grandeur.

The elder Africanus predicts the future, explains the nature of the
universe, and reveals a doctrine which cannot be demonstrated or
determined by reason alone, but which can be stated in this kind of
mythic revelation: that there is an afterlife. An afterlife of
blessedness, reserved for those who practice virtue — civic virtue
and interior virtue — to a heroic degree. Heroic virtue earns
immortality. Those who do not achieve it simply vanish and
disappear. There does not appear to be much, if anything, in the way
of an accompanying hell — although there has been some
disagreement on that point — but that seems to be the direct
reading of the text.

Here, then, is a reward for virtue that transcends virtue itself and
transcends the kind of disciplined and refined pleasure that the
Epicurean tradition praised and that Cicero had sought to attach to
Stoic devotion to virtue in his earlier thinking. He makes a religious
statement: there is in fact a life of considerable blessedness for those
who can combine pleasure and virtue in the right way, provided they
do so to a heroic degree. It is, | think, rather interesting that this very
definition of the requirement for immortality is essentially the
definition that medieval Christianity would adopt for sainthood. A
Saint is a person who practices heroic virtue and is thereby honored
at the altar. This is an extension of Cicero's attempt to define the
ultimate reward of virtue for his own pagan time. An Africanus elder
and an Africanus younger had achieved a great deal of both victory
and fame in this world. But here is a reward beyond even those. And
here was a kind of religious philosophy — practical, concrete,

focused on a disciplined elite — that a practical Roman could
appreciate, could spread, could preach, and could believe in.

Cicero's Influence on Later Western Thought

Cicero's influence on later periods of Western thought has been
absolutely immense. From the eighth to the twelfth centuries — the
Carolingian period and the first great expansion of medieval West
European culture — Cicero was the most respected, studied, and
imitated classical thinker in Western Europe. When Charlemagne was
attempting to get his Frankish nobles to do a better job of ruling the
great empire that had fallen to them — not only in terms of treating
subject peoples better, but in terms of governing the Frankish people
with greater justice — he had the scholars of his palace school teach
him, his sons, and their companions through Ciceronian dialogues.
Alcuin, the Englishman brought in to improve the culture of the
Carolingian court, staged a dialogue with Charlemagne about
rhetoric — an effort to present to the Christian Franks the whole
bundle of Cicero's teaching, thought, and example.

After yielding some ground to Plato and Aristotle in philosophy from
roughly 1200 to about 1330 — though never yielding ground as a
model of Latin prose style — Cicero arose again as a dominant
influence in the Renaissance, and his influence lasted brilliantly
throughout the seventeenth, eighteenth, and into the nineteenth
century. For a very long time, well into the era of the public schools
of imperial England, he was regarded as a perfect model for the
cultivated gentleman — both in his forensic oratory and in his
elegant but concise prose style, and as the spokesman for an
enlightened and humane personal ethic that made him a good
friend, a good husband, a good consul, a good diplomat, and a good
human being.

Lecture 12: Roman Skepticism and Sextus Empiricus
Professor Jeremy Adams

Origins and Character of Skepticism

Skepticism was a philosophical tradition — sometimes organized
into formal schools, but often far more casual than that — which
arose in the generation of drastic cultural change following the death
of Aristotle. It is more traditional to speak of the transformation of
the Greek world after the death of Alexander in 323 BCE, but the
death of Aristotle the following year, 322 BCE, is of course far more
important for the history of philosophy. Although looked upon with
some suspicion by Roman culture, skepticism continued to thrive
under Roman rule, especially in the Eastern and Greek-speaking half
of the Empire. It was revived in modern times for the Enlightenment
of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, and it remains a
powerful current in modern Western thought.

The skeptics were trying to do what most philosophies have
attempted to do — that is, to find a secure basis for human
happiness. What distinguished them from the Greek philosophical
schools that preceded them, including Sophism, Platonism, and
Aristotelianism, and from the new schools of Epicureanism and
Stoicism that arose at roughly the same time, was that the skeptics
tried to ground the pursuit of happiness in a denial of knowledge
rather than in the affirmation of one or another path toward
knowledge.

Pyrrho of Elis: The Founder

The apparent founder of skepticism was Pyrrho of Elis, born around
365 BCE and dying around 275 BCE. He was a soldier attached to the

staff of Alexander the Great on the long march to India, where he
must have encountered a world very different from the Hellenic
normality of his own small mountainous polis of Elis in the
Peloponnesus. Some scholars speculate that Pyrrho may have
communicated with Hindu wise men and thus encountered a very
different intellectual world as well.

In any case, after Alexander's death in Babylon in 323 BCE, Pyrrho
returned to Elis, his small native city. Taking absolute skepticism to its
logical conclusions, he wrote nothing — which is pleasantly ironic,
since his followers proceeded to write book after book of Pyrrhonic
hypotheses, Pyrrhonic sayings, and Pyrrhonic compilations of every
kind for some five centuries after his death.

According to the disciples who did publish, Pyrrho taught that one
must neither trust nor reject sense impressions, nor any other kind of
apparent knowledge. Reason — apparently more reliable than the
varying deliverances of the senses, and the faculty to which most
philosophers had had recourse before him — cannot in fact, said
Pyrrho, reach the inner substance of things. Whether as objects of
rational inquiry or as items of sense data, things can be known to us
only as they appear to one or another of our faculties. Since things
appear differently to different observers, and indeed to the different
faculties of any single observer, and since we can develop equally

And since we can be certain of nothing, it is wise to withhold
judgment. Epechein is the Greek word from which came the doctrine
of epoché, meaning the withholding or suspension of judgment in
scientific or moral matters.

A life of tranquil withdrawal was Pyrrho's ethical ideal, a state labeled
ataraxia by his disciple Timon of Phlius, who wrote the first of the
series of Pyrrhonic books about his master's sayings. The wise

person, according to Pyrrho, will strive to attain and maintain the
soul in such an ataraxic condition. It seems to have worked well
enough for Pyrrho himself, who appears to have died at the then
extraordinary age of ninety.

What about human laws, customs, and religious practices — the
public liturgies essential to the functioning of the polytheistic civic
culture of the Greeks, and indeed of all the other cultures of the
ancient Mediterranean, West Asian, and South Asian world at that
time? How should a reflective person thoroughly convinced of
Pyrrhonic skepticism respond to those liturgies of public life? Obey
them, Pyrrho is reported to have said. Stay out of trouble. Avoid
scandal. Act decently in your community. Be decent.

Timon — whom the later Roman skeptic Sextus Empiricus called the
apostle, or prophet, of Pyrrho's sayings, somewhat as Paul stood in
relation to Jesus in the elaboration of Christian teaching — spiced up
this first book of the skeptical tradition with satirical treatments of
other philosophers of the time, excepting of course Pyrrho himself,
and, interestingly enough, Xenophon. Pyrrho's followers spread his
oral teachings widely.

The Academy's Turn toward Skepticism

A sign of skepticism's considerable appeal to the contemporary
Greek mentality is the fact that Plato's school of advanced study, the
Academy at Athens, was dominated by fairly extreme forms of
skeptical philosophy from the early third century to the early first
century BCE.

Arcesilaus, 314-241 BCE, who became the director of the Academy
less than a century after its foundation by Plato around 369 BCE, is
generally described as the founder of the Second or Middle
Academy. The Academy itself remained in continuous legal existence

as an institution until 529 CE, when the Christian Roman Emperor
Justinian closed it for reasons both fiscal and doctrinal — an
extraordinarily long duration in the course of which it passed
through many different phases of attachment to various
philosophical modes and affirmations.

Arcesilaus, upon becoming head of the Academy and directing it into
its second phase, is supposed to have declared that he was not even
certain of his own uncertainty — thus going Socrates one better,
since Socrates at least knew that he knew nothing. Arcesilaus was
particularly critical of Stoic thought, and one can understand why,
since Stoicism found itself in the awkward position of being
committed simultaneously to a materialist view of ultimate reality —
including a God who, for the Stoics, was himself a material reality —
and an overarchingly spiritual set of moral imperatives.

Arcesilaus aspired to do to Stoicism what Socrates had done so
devastatingly to Sophism, although he was far less successful than
Socrates had been. It is important to recognize that, despite his many
departures from Plato's own line of thought, Arcesilaus tried to
remain true to the broader tradition, seeking to re-embody the style
of Socratic teaching and, as he understood it, the substance of
Socratic content as well.

Consistent with Pyrrho's denial of knowledge was Arcesilaus'
exploration of human subjectivity — and here again one can see
echoes of the Sophist tradition against which Socrates had originally
reacted: the tradition that abandons the search for objective truth
and turns instead to an exploration of the subjective dimensions of
human experience. No sense impression we receive, and no
statement we make about any external object, carries any guarantee
of validity, according to Arcesilaus. Why? Because we can be firmly
convinced subjectively of something that others can see to be

objectively false, or that we ourselves will in time come to see as
objectively false.

The obvious counter-argument, launched by the Stoics in response,
was that the skeptics had undercut the very notion that any kind of
objective certainty exists — and in doing so had sawed off the
branch on which they were sitting. The skeptics denied this, but,
much as they accused their opponents of circular reasoning, they
found themselves caught in considerable coils of circularity as well.
