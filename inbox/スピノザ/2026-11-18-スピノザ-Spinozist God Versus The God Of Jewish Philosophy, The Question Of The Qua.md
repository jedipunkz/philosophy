THE SPINOZIST GOD VERSUS THE GOD OF JEWISH PHILOSOPHY FROM
THE MIDDLE AGES TO THE CLASSICAL AGE: THE QUESTION OF THE
QUATENUS

Jacques J. Rozenberg!

Centre National de la Recherche Scientifique, France

ABSTRACT: Although presented by German philosophers of Nature as a
"drunk" man with God, Spinoza was soon considered a pure atheist, and
in particular by Orobio de Castro, who was the first Jewish critic of the
Ethics. His analysis focused on the theoretical difficulties of this work,
specifically demonstrating that Spinozism consists in imperceptibly
shifting from a reference to God as traditionally defined by His uniqueness
to His identification with multiple nature. Orobio de Castro showed that
such a shift was made possible by a semantic procedure, retaining the
same reference but radically changing its meaning. He thus mentions
reduplicative formulas, using the term quatenus. This article aims, on the
one hand, to bring to light the semantic role of the term quatenus, which
made it possible to elaborate the Spinozist conception of a God immanent
in nature, and on the other hand, to confront Spinoza's use of this term
with that proposed by Jewish philosophy in the Middle Ages, the
Renaissance, and the Classical Age.

KEYWORDS: God; Spinoza; quatenus; Orobio de Castro; intension;
extension; Maimonides; Gersonides

Orobio de Castro and the First Jewish Refutation of Spinozist Metaphysics

Itshaq Orobio de Castro, who presented the first Jewish refutation of Spinoza's
metaphysics (Kaplan, 2004, p. 265 n6), summarized the main objections that followers
of the Law of Moses could argue against the Ethics.4 He thus inaugurated, on a
metaphysical level, the "Spinoza controversy" (Schwartz, 2019, pp. 6-7).>5 In 1684,
Orobio de Castro published his work Certamen Philosophicum Propugnate Veritatis
Divine ac Naturalis Adversus J. Bredenburgi Principia. This work was a critique of
Spinozism through that of the writings of the Collegiant Johannes Bredenburg (1643-
1691): Enervatio, tractatus theologico-politict, una demonstratione, geometrico ordine
disposita, naturam non esse Deum, published in 1675 (Bredenburg, 1675; van Bunge,
1990, p. 121).° Bredenburg sought, among other things, to disavow Frans Kuyper’s (or
Cuperis) (1629-1681) criticism of Spinoza’s Tractatus theologico-politicus (Cuperis, 1676,
Touber, 2018, p. 105).

Bredenburg-Spinoza's Definition I, which Orobio de Castro aims to refute, states: "By
a being or anything necessary is meant a being whose existence proceeds from its
essence, or a being that exists necessary in an essential way" (Per ens, vel aliquid
necessarium, intelligitur ens, cujus existentia est de eius essentia: vel ens quod necessario
essentialiter est). Orobio de Castro emphasizes that this proposition is true (vera est) if it
applies to God alone (soli Deo), but when it is applied to creatures, or to nature as a
whole, as Spinoza does, one denies "the one God, creator of the universe, and believes
that the whole universe is God: which is precisely Spinoza's atheism." (unicum Deum
univers creatorem negat : et totum universum credit esse Deum; quod Spinozae atheismum
plane constituit) (Orobio de Castro, 1703, p. 10). Orobio de Castro shows that the
subtlety of Spinozism consists in passing imperceptibly from one plane to another,
pretending to refer to God as He is traditionally defined by His uniqueness, when in
fact it applies His definition to the multiple nature.

To bring to light this process of surreptitious passage, Orobio de Castro analyzes
Bredenburg-Spinoza's Definition II, positing that "Attributes are qualities whose
essence constitutes the nature of the thing of which they are the attributes or qualities,

Spinoza posits that "In Nature, there is nothing contingent; but everything is
determined by the necessity of the divine nature to exist and to act according to a
particular modality" (In rerum natura nullum datur contingens sed omnia ex necessitate
divinae naturae determinata sunt ad certo modo existendum et operandum) (Spinoza, 1925,
Ethics I, 29). This is why, according to Spinoza, every action is necessarily determined.
In his Proposition III, Orobio de Castro challenges this thesis, showing that it stems from
Spinoza's negation of the notion of creation ex nihilo.’ This is why action cannot
constitute any novelty, because that would imply that it could come from nothing. This
assumption, which Orobio de Castro qualifies as an axiom (axioma) and a dogma (sed
dogmaticum), aims to posit that existences and actions are eternally determined (Orobio
de Castro 1703, p. 37). As a result, Spinoza comes to deny the faculty of will or non-
will (volendi et nolendi), for everyone is necessarily determined to will this or that action
by a necessary cause (Spinoza 1925, Ethics II, 48, demonstration). Now man can
always, at a given moment, not want to achieve something and then want it later.
Orobio de Castro underlined that to deny this observation forces us to suppose that
from all eternity two contradictory actions have coincided, and this supposition
clashes with logic itself (Orobio de Castro, 1703, p. 37).

As I shall show later, despite the clear distinction that Spinoza made between
substance and its affections, the impossibility of deducing the finite modes from the
infinite substance led him to identify these two incommensurable domains. Orobio de
Castro's analysis has revealed the difficulties of Spinoza's concept of attributes, which
makes the monism of substance problematic. Nearly a century before Hegel, Orobio
de Castro was the first commentator to propose a subjective interpretation of
attributes. This interpretation conceives attributes as multiple forms specific to
intellect, thus allowing us to grasp substance while preserving its simplicity and
uniqueness.’

Following Maimonides (Maimonides 1963, I, p. 52), Orobio de Castro seeks to prove
that attributes, while allowing us to relate to God, concern only His actions and not
His essence. Whereas for Spinoza, the attribute "is what the intellect perceives of a
substance as constituting its essence (quod intellectus de substantia percipit tanquam
ejusdem essentiam constituens)" (Spinoza 1925, Ethics, I, definition IV, Ethics, I, 19),
according to Orobio de Castro, attributes are mere qualities, which only express the
"weakness of our intellect (nostri intellectu imbecillitatem)" (Orobio de Castro, 1703, pp.
11-12). He thus attempts to refute the objectivism of the attributes that Bredenburg
aims to justify. He emphasizes that such a conception necessarily implies a multiplicity
of heterogeneous attributes, whereas they are supposed to constitute the single
substance. Analyzing Propositions 4 and 5 of the De Deo, Orobio de Castro shows that
the author of the Ethics has undermined divine simplicity, which remains incompatible
with the notion of composition. He adds that Spinoza confused, with regard to
attributes, three forms of discernment: diversity, difference and distinction (diversitatem,
differentiam and distinctionem inepte confundunt) (Orobio de Castro, 1703, p. 59). In Ethics
I, 4, and in Ethics I, 5, Demonstration, Spinoza mentions only diversity and distinction,
whereas the term differentia will appear only in Ethics II, 17, Scholium. According to
Orobio de Castro, diversity is the strongest form of discernment: two things are diverse
if they have no species or genus in common, and therefore they are incomparable.
Spinoza states precisely, in Ethics I, 4: "Two or more distinct things are distinguished
from each other, either because the attributes of substances are diverse, or because the
affections of these same substances are diverse (Due aut plures res distincte vel inter se
distinguuntur ex diversitate attributorum substantiarum vel ex diversitate earundem
affectionum)" (Spinoza 1925, Ethics I, 4). According to Orobio de Castro's classification,
Spinoza's description of the attributes in terms of diversity then makes them
heterogeneous from each other; thus contradicting the essence of the divine unity. In
this sense, Spinoza could affirm, in Letter 50 to Jarig Jellesz: “I establish that God can
only be very improperly called one and unique (Deum non, nisi valde improprie, unum,
vel unicum dici posse) regarding essence, but this can be said with all rigor regarding
existence” (Spinoza 1925, Letter 50 to Jarig Jellesz, G. IV, p. 239). And in the Ethics,
Spinoza related only to the unique existence of substance (unicam substantiam existere),
and not to its essence (Spinoza 1925, Ethics I, 10, scholium).

are dissimilar, although they remain comparable in the species or genus to which they

seeking to affirm a minimal discernment between substances, Spinoza posits that not
only attributes, but also modes maintain a relationship of diversity of maximum
dissimilarity: ex diversitate attributorum, and ex diversitate affectionum. Moreover, in
Spinoza's hypothesis that there could be several distinct substances (Si darentur plures
distincte) (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, 5, demonstration), according to Orobio de Castro,
these substances should not have diverse attributes (diversitate attributum). If Spinoza
had been consistent, he should have noted, from a terminological point of view, that
attributes are only distinct and not diverse. Orobio de Castro's differentiated
classification then calls into question the theoretical basis of Ethics I, 4-5 and
Demonstration, thus proving that Spinoza’s monism of substance remains unfounded
(Feldman ,1988, pp. 228-229).

Founding immanence?

Orobio de Castro notes that the expression Deus sive Natura (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics IV,
Preface) only veils Spinoza's inability to prove the monism of substance, which he
seeks to transfer to the unity of nature. The Ethics clearly implies that natura naturans
cannot be reduced to natura naturata, insofar as the former designates "that which is in
itself and is conceived by itself (quod in se est et per se concipitur)," while the latter
concerns "all that follows from the necessity of the nature of God (omne quod ex
necessitae Dei naturae)" (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, 29 scholium; Curley, 1988, p. 37). But
the precise relations between the two natures were never made explicit by Spinoza,
nor did succeed to account for the production of natured nature from naturing nature.
As stressed by Jonathan F. Bennet, “the absence of an effective contrast between God
and not-God” allows to qualify Spinoza as both a pantheist and an atheist (Bennett,
1984, p. 32).

For Spinoza, as everything proceeds from God, the finite mode must be determined
by God or by His attributes, in so far as He is modified by such and such a modification
(quatenus modificatum est tali modificatione), that is to say, by a finite mode (Spinoza,

in something else and is conceived by this other thing (id quod in alio est et per id in quo

only given further on, in Definition V.

Then we need to ask how can the mode, as qua-modification of substance, be
produced? Indeed, the mode must already pre-exist the modification it brings about
of the substance which, thus modified, can then produce the mode. For Spinoza, the
answer to this question seems to be found in the identification of causa sui with the
causality that produces modes: "in the sense that God is said to be the cause of Himself,
He must also be said to be the cause of all things (eo sensu quo Deus dicitur causa sui,
etiam omnium rerum causa dicendus est)" (Spinoza 1925, Ethics I, 25, scholium). This
causal equivalence implies, as Gueroult notes, that the things produced, constituting
the inherent properties of God, are to this extent God Himself, since He cannot cause
Himself without necessarily causing them (Gueroult, 1968, pp. 266-267). Let us
emphasize that the relationship of God to the universe, as Spinoza conceived it, ceases
to be the traditional one of the infinite to the finite, to become that of the cause to its
effect, by positing finite modes as properties. However, these notions are
heterogenous: causality is assigned to the extensional order, whereas quality is
assigned to the intensional order. Spinoza has never explained the assimilation he
operates between these two orders, which he simply posits as the equivalence between
cause and reason (causa sive ratio) (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, 11 aliter).!° Describing this
equivalence, as surprising, Alexandre Matheron points out that, for example, a
geometric figure has many properties but it produces no effect (Matheron, 1991, p. 85).

In Ethics I, 28, Demonstration, the notion of modification, by which Spinoza thinks
he can account for the production of finite modes by the infinite substance, is based on
the use of the term quatenus. It should be noted that this usage allows for circular
reasoning. Spinoza presupposes in the premises of the Demonstration of Ethics I, 28,
the existence of finite modes capable of modifying the divine substance. Thus, he
posits that what is finite must therefore follow from God or from one of his attributes,
"in so far as He is considered to be affected by some modes" (quatenus aliquo modo
affectum consideratur). And Spinoza then affirms “subsequently” (deinde), but without
any deduction; as he states: “Then this cause or mode... must also be determined by
another, which is also finite and has a determined existence.” (Deinde hc rursus causa
sive hic modus ... debuit etiam determinari ab alia que etiam finita est et determinatam habet
existentiam) (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, 28, Demonstration). I recalled earlier that Spinoza
thus posits in a circular way modes as affections of substance, in order to explain the
production of modes. He first supposes the existence of modes as affections of
substance, even before he has given an account of their productions. In addition, this
attempt to explain the production of modes is opposed to Spinoza's earlier assertion
that the finite cannot be produced by an infinite attribute, because everything that
follows from infinity necessarily exists as infinite and not as finite (Spinoza, 1925,
Ethics I, 22). In other words, as John Caird points out, it amounts to assuming "without
proof what has already been denied" (Caird, 1888, p. 165). It should also be noted that
the notion of causality can only concern the finite and never the infinite. For the cause,
whether efficient or posited as causa-sui, exhausts its whole being in its effects, and
therefore there can never be infinite effects from infinite causes, for the latter would
then necessarily limit their infinity. As Maimonides had shown, God cannot be
considered a cause, because this would imply that His existence is linked to the effects
He produces (Maimonides, 1963, pp. 118-119). Causality is confined to the finite
domain, where it is determined by the order of succession or coincidence between
causes and effects (Caird, 1888, p. 167). In this sense, Orobio de Castro has shown,
contrary to Spinoza, that no reduplicative formulation (in reduplication) can, as a cause
(quatenus causat), equate the effect to its cause: "No cause, in its act of causing, produces
an effect which is entirely similar to itself as a cause, even if the effect is univocal (that
is to say, follows directly from it)" (causa ut causat, nullus effectus quamtumvis univocus,
est qualis sua causa ut causans) (Orobio de Castro, 1703, pp. 29-30).

The term quatenus, like that of jam, plays a decisive role in the logic of Ethics, by
attempting to bring together incommensurable notions, such as that of mode and

Although Spinoza assigned to the adverb quatenus the burden of avoiding any
contradiction regarding monism, he never defined its conceptual status, and he uses
this term in four different senses: formal, abstract, causal, and conditional (Krop, 2014,
p. 299, Powell, 1906, p. 157 n2).15 From a stylistic point of view, the use of the adverb
quatenus is monotonous (Kajanto, 2005, p. 52), and in the Ethics Spinoza utilizes it 444
times in a logical sense, and he uses the verb sequi 247 times, in a causal sense.

quatenus appears somewhat disproportionate to that of the term sequi (Leerke, 2012,

It may also be noted that, for example, in Ethics I, 21, Scholium, Spinoza uses the
term jam in order to justify what is commonly called psycho-physical parallelism: "the
mind and the body are one and the same individual, who conceives himself sometimes
(jam) under the attribute of thought, sometimes (jam) under the attribute extended
(Spinoza, 1925, Ethics, II, 21, Scholium). The term jam makes it possible to establish
what Chantal Jaquet called a "logic of alternation," according to which we can
understand things, either from the point of view of the attribute of thought, or from
the point of view of the attribute of extension (Jaquet, 2012, pp. 69-79). In fact, the
adverb quatenus is the driving force behind this logic of alternation.

Let’s recall that this adverb was frequently used in the seventeenth century. For
example, Suarez used it, among other things, to justify the notion of the Trinity
(Suarez, 1856, IV, XIII), and in the Third Meditation, Descartes evokes those ideas that
"are of certain ways of thinking" (quatenus ideae istae cogitandi) (Descartes, 1996, AT IX,
27; AT VIL 35). In the Fourth Meditation, he emphasizes the idea of "the human mind,
insofar as it is a thing that thinks" (quatenus est res cogitans) (Descartes, 1996, AT IX, 42;
AT VIL, 53; Principia I, 9; AT VIIL, p. 30-31). In all these cases, the Cartesian use of the
adverb quatenus was limited to the sole domain of thought, which he did not seek to
link to another fundamentally heterogeneous domain.

(exavadinAa@ois, iteration) of the same term (Aristotle, Prior Anal. I, 38, 49a11-49b1).
Spinoza assigned to it the function of uniting the level of the absolute and the level of
the natured, in order to prove the immanence of the infinite substance to its finite
modes (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics II, 9, Naess 2005, p. 56). However, he never deduced the
way in which finite modes are produced by infinite substance, although he did attempt

Orobio de Castro pointed out that, if regarding the essence of a thing its attributes

subject to a combination of essences and random circumstances. He specifies that the
necessity of the essence of a thing should not be confused with the contingency of its

that "for example if there are twenty men in nature... it will not be sufficient (to account
for the existence of these twenty men) to give the cause of human nature in general.”
(Si exempli gratia in rerum natura 20 homines existant non satis erit (ut scilicet rationem
reddamus cur 20 homines existant) causam nature humane in genere ostendere). However,
Spinoza does not assert the contingency of the existence of particular individuals but
posits that “there must necessarily be a cause for the existence of each of them”
(uniuscujusque debet necessario dari causa cur existat) (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, scholium).

If Orobio de Castro rejects absolute necessitarism, because it leaves no room for
contingency, he nevertheless provides the conditions that allow to conceive
legitimately the notion of necessity. He thus uses a reduplicative proposition such as
"white as white is necessarily white" (ut album, quatenus album, necessario est album)

natural modes: "All doing, and every action, is necessarily of such a kind that it could
not have been otherwise" (omne agere, et omnis actio, talis necessario est, ut aliter esse non
potuisset). Orobio de Castro's use of the term quatenus remains limited to the same
reduplicated domain, whereas Spinoza's use of it is expanded to some entirely distinct
domains. Then Orobio de Castro notes that, if an action occurs in existence, it is always
because of an essence that is at the origin of this power to act, but this action is not
itself a "necessary existence" (sit necessariae existentiae). However, he specifies the
circumstances in which a proposition remains "eternally true" (propositio aeternae
veritatis). He gives as an example the statement: “man is reasonable” (homo est
rationalis). This truth is affirmed whenever a man exists, and as a result that particular
man is endowed with the faculty of reasoning. It is only in this sense that the essential
attribute of a thing necessarily exists (Orobio de Castro, 1703, p. 45).

It should be noted that, generally speaking, the adverb quatenus determines
semantic contexts of an intensional order, which are therefore referentially opaque
(Jarrett, 1977, p. 87, note 21, Hennig, 2011, pp. 147-149). Indeed, Leibniz emphasizes
that a redundant expression is reflexive because it refers to our thoughts (Belaval, 2004,
p. 126n. 77). But Spinoza uses this type of expression to operate an illegitimate passage
between the intensional and extensional orders. In this regard, the term quatenus,
according to Spinozism, would carry a function akin to the one Jacques Lacan, and
subsequently Jacques-Alain Miller, allocated to the term "suture" which attempts to
link incommunicable orders (Lacan, 1973, p. 127, Miller, 1966, p. 39). In the context of
Spinozism, the suture remains a purely tautological expression, devoid of any
demonstrative content, and it only aims to circumvent the difficulties of Spinoza’s
system (Caird, 1888, p. 166). William James emphasized that the quatenus is the

The adverb quatenus and the question of God's modifications

The reduplicative process of the quatenus also applies to the expression eo sensu,
whereby, "in the sense in which God is said to be the cause of Himself (eo sensu quo
Deus dicitur causa sui)," He is also "the cause of all things (omnium rerum causa)
(Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, scholium). Thus God, in so far as He is modified by mode a,
causes God in so far as He is modified by mode BD. Finite causality would then be
nothing but divine self-causality, as it is modified in a particular way (Leerke, 2013, pp.
72-73, Sangiacomo and Nachtomy, 2018, p. 118).

As I recalled previously, according to Spinoza, infinity can exclusively follow from
infinity (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, 22), whereas a finite thing can only be produced by
"another cause which is itself finite (quae etiam finita)" (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, 28). He
seeks also to show that substance produces finite things, since there is nothing apart
from substance and modes, and that everything has been predetermined

are not produced by eternal attributes, and modes are only the affections or
modifications of the substance which interact with each other (Gueroult, 1968, p. 339).

It should also be noted that the term affectus, even when used in connection with

the modes by which He is affected, and therefore this passivity proceeds solely from
His activity, the fact remains, as Francis Kaplan points out, that the Spinozist God is
also passive. This then contradicts the absolute perfection of God which necessarily
envelops His essence (absolutamque perfectionem involvit) (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics I, 11,

production of the infinity of His modes. Wherefore, although a substance is by nature

not before his decrees (ante sua decreta non fuisse), and without them He cannot be" (nec

since they are qualified as "decrees in created things" (rebus creatis decreta) (Spinoza

concipitur cannot depend on any being in alio that He necessarily produces, but in fact

The qualitative and quantitative aspects of Spinozism: an unresolved tension?

As I have pointed out earlier, Spinozism was formed from an unthought passage
between two differentiated semantic orders, intensional and extensional, whose
distinction Spinoza did not always respect.

While the Cartesian analytic method is inductive and perceptual, and therefore of
an extensional order, Spinoza's synthetic and geometric method is deductive, and
proceeds, from an intensional order, by concepts and definitions (Haserot, 1950, p.
483). As early as the Short Treatise, Spinoza rejects extensional knowledge by genres
and differences (van geslagt en onderscheid), because such a method calls for definitions
that are not capable of producing any new knowledge (Spinoza, 1925, KV I, VIL 9, GI,
46). The privilege he will then give to the more geometrico implies a rejection of
experience, and therefore a depreciation of the extensional procedures related to
induction (Saisset, 1842, XXXVI-XXXVIII). It must be emphasized that Spinoza's

metaphysical conceptuality is of an intensional order (Newlands, 2006, pp. 42-43),19
even if extensional logic also intervenes in the Ethics (LeBuffe, 2010, pp. 534-535).

Charles Ramond has identified, notably in Letter 12 to Lodewijk Meyer, "violent
clashes" between qualitative and quantitative conceptions. These clashes are
constitutive of Spinozism determining, inter alia, the distinction between natura
naturans and natura naturata. | specify that the logic of quality falls under an intensional
order, whereas that of quantity proceeds from an extensional order (Badesa 2004, 10,
note 1). Win Klever notes that in the Tractatus de intellectus emendatione § 40, Spinoza
passes, without any transition, from the quantity of knowledge (quo plura mens novit)
to the quality of the mind's understanding of its own powers concerning the order of
nature (eo melius et suas vires, et ordinem Nature intelligit). According to Spinoza, the
greater the knowledge of nature (quantity), then the better will be our knowledge
(quality) (Spinoza, 1925, TIE § 40; Klever, 1994, p. 118, note 17). Ramond specifies that
the qualitative aspect presents Spinoza as a Neoplatonic sage, while the quantitative
aspect makes him a materialist thinker (Ramond, 1995, p. 332). As I have shown
previously, Spinoza tried to make a transition between these two orders, by means of
semantic operators, and in particular the terms tanquam, jam, and especially quatenus.
Through the widespread use of these operators, Spinoza sought to overcome the
opposition between intension and extension, by means of some non-deduced
transitions from one order to another. This distinction has been described as the
"distinction of distinctions," insomuch as it enables to stratify the differentiated
domains of the intelligibility of reality (Rovere, 2003-2004, p. 33; Auroux, 2008, p. 22).
This is why Spinoza failed in erasing the fundamental dualisms from which his
philosophical system was elaborated. Therefore, many terms of the Ethics remain
ambiguous, and have a double meaning, referring indifferently to logical and physical
determinations (Garrett, 2003, p. 61), the first being of intensional order and the second
being of extensional order.

The problematic use of semantic terms, like guatenus, makes it possible to assimilate
certain propositions of the Ethics to paradoxes of logic, to the point that the entire Ethics
has been described as a complex version of the Liar's Paradox (Nielsen, 2003, p. 28).
Indeed, according to Bertrand Russell, monism is the result of a flawed logic, inspired
by mysticism (Russell, 1927, p. 264), which confuses the "class," which is extensive in
nature, and the "category," which is intensive in nature (Koyré, 1947, p. 33; Ortigue,
1982, p. 182; Rozenberg, 1994, pp. 175-182). Let us recall that Epimenides' proposition,
"All Cretans are liars," which presents itself as "the assertion Y that X is making at this
moment is false," becomes a sophism when it is transformed into a sui-referential
statement: "I lie." Nonsense arises when the statement claims to apply to itself. As
Alexandre Koyré points out, no concept can be applied to itself, at the risk of
presenting real ambiguity. In accordance with Orobio de Castro's remark that I
previously mentioned. Koyré underlined that the "term remains the same; its meaning,
however, changes (Koyré, 1947, p. 13; Lacan, 1973, p. 127). The enunciated always

The quatenus in Jewish philosophy of the Middle Ages and the Renaissance

According to Julie R. Klein, the term quatenus, which, in Spinoza, allows to make a
transition from the naturing point of view to the natured point of view, would take up
the expressions of Gersonides (1288-1344): be'ofen mah (in such a way as), and micydo
(from his point of view). These expressions, which seem to be equivalent to the term
quatenus, would have allowed, in a similar way, these two authors to avoid dualism
and the multiplication of real entities (Klein, 2003, p. 35; Klein, 2014, p. 183). In general,
the commentators stressed the probable influence of Gersonides on Spinoza, who
describes him as an "extremely learned man" (virum alias eruditissimum) (Spinoza, 1925,
TTP IX, annotation 16, G IH, 256). Warren Zev Harvey has noted, on the one hand, a
convergence between the two authors concerning the notion of conatus (Harvey, 2012,
pp. 273-297), and on the other hand between the Spinozist notion of "amor Dei
intellectualis" and the Gersonide notion of desire (esheq) (Harvey, 1998, pp. 103-104).
Menachem Kellner has suggested that Gersonides announces the Spinozist
identification of the Deus sive Natura (Spinoza, 1925, Ethics IV, Preface; Kellner, 1998,
p.22). Concerning such an identification, it should be noted that, if the commentary on
the Song of Songs seems to associate God and nature, this association in fact concerns
Gersonides' reference to Aristotle's writings on his Historia Animalium (Gaziel 2012,
pp-243-271). However, in Milhamot Ha-Shem (The Wars of the Lord), he explicitly states
that "since God has ordained the whole plan of nature (sider zeh ha-tev'a), it seems
impossible for a natural being to have the power to change nature and its order of
functioning (leshanot ha-tev'a we-ha-sidur), unlike God, who has ordained ('‘asher sider)
this nature" (Gersonides, 1923, pp. VI, IL, 10, 445; Feldman, 1984, p. 474). Gersonides
thus conceives of God as being transcendent in relation to the nature He orders,
without ever identifying them but underlying that God can always change nature.

Concerning the term quatenus, Idit Dobbs-Weinstein has tried to draw a parallel
between certain passages of Gersonides' Super-commentary on the De Anima, and
Spinoza's Ethics. She particularly insists on the perspectivist use of the expression "in
this regard" (me-ha-cad) (Dobbs-Weinstein, 1998, pp. 205-206). Following her, Julie R.
Klein emphasized the similarity between Spinoza's use of expressions such as "as far
as possible" (quamprimus) and the Hebrew use of "manner to" or "to a certain extent"
(be'ofen mah). She also noted the proximity between the term quatenus and the term
micad, each of which expressed two different ways of conceiving the same thing. Such
expressions would have allowed these two authors to perceive real things, while
avoiding positing abstractions and separate entities. For these two authors, it would
be a question of promoting an approach qualified both as aspectival and perspectival
(Klein 2003, p. 35; Klein, 2014, p. 183).

By referring directly to several texts by Gersonides, I wish to test the relevance of
such a convergence. In his work Milhamot Ha-Shem, the expression be'ofen mah
generally means in such a way to (Gersonides, 1923, III, V, 3, 233-234). But in his
commentary on the Song of Songs, to which Klein refers, Gersonides uses the
expression be'ofen mah in a perspectival sense: "from the point of view of the material
and potential intellect, and from the point of view of the degrees of the act (be'ofen mah
lesekel ha-hyiul'any u-be'ofen mah bemadregat ha-po'al) (Gersonides, 1860, 9a; Klein, 2002,

73

pp. 295-324; Leerke, 2017, pp. 14-15, note 15). It should be noted, however, that
Gersonides here refers to Aristotle’s De Anima, to describe the complementary points
of view of potential and act, but without referring to any incommensurable orders,
such as those of the natura naturans and the natura naturata, which Spinoza's pantheism
tried to identify (Gueroult, 1968, p. 267). Similarly, Gersonides uses the terms "micad"
and "micydo," which signify according to and on the side of. However, while referring to
the various aspects that must be taken into account, these terms do not in any way link
ontologically separate domains (Gersonides, 1923, I, 11, 82; V, UL, 11, 277; VI, IL, 8, 435;
Gersonides, 1860, 4a). In his Super-commentaries on Averroes, Gersonides uses the
term micad several times, in order to specify the "concrete domain insofar as it is
concrete in act" (ha-muhash micad shehu' muhash be-po'al) (Gersonides, 1984, p. 397). By
the term micad, he also emphasizes that "something limited can only exist in potential"
(shel'o yme'a bilty ba'al taklyt 'el'a micad ha-koah) (Gersonides, 2006, p.10). Likewise,
referring to Averroes, Gersonides specifies that, when speaking of a builder we do not
refer to an individual insofar as he is a man (micad mah shehu' 'adam), since by the act
of building, the builder builds as a builder and not as a man (we-l'o micad mah shehu'
'adam), because not every man is a builder (ky 'eyn kol 'adam boneh) (Gersonides, 1923,
pp. L 4, 28; Feldman, 1984, p. 134).

As J. R. Klein suggests, it seems that terms equivalent to that of quatenus were taken

"are not taken as (non quatenus), but as having a magnitude and a divisibility" (Klein,
2014, p. 183n18; Aristotle n.d., Metaphysics M, 3, 1077 b20; Tricot, 1974, p. 728 n1).
However, in Aristotle, as well as in his Arab commentators (Avicenna, 1972, p. 53;
Averroes, 2003, pp.112-115), the equivalent of the term quatenus is not used in a
perspectival sense, but it means: "under the title of." In the same spirit, R. Hasdai
Crescas (1340-1411), who refers largely to Gersondes, uses the term "level" (behynah) to
distinguish, from an epistemological point of view, between two distinct approaches.
He thus emphasizes that the effect (‘alul), at the level of its reason ('iylah), necessarily
exists (mehuyav ha-mecy'ut), while at the level of the effect itself, its existence is only
possible (mibehynat 'acmo 'efshar ha-mecy'ut) (Hasdai Crescas, 1990, p. 266). These two
levels do not belong to different domains.

It should also be noted that medieval commentators on the Sefer Yegyrah, such as R.
Abraham ben David de Posquiéres (1120-1198), used the expression in connection with
or based on (micad), which Gersonides would take up, to describe the complex system
of the sefyrot (divine emanations). Thus, for example, a person's merit is determined in
relation (micad) to sefyrah Hesed (generosity), while his guilt is in relation (micad) to
sefyrah Dyn (rigor) (Perush ha-R'aved 1965, IL, 1, 74). R. M'eyr Ibn Gabay (1480-1540)
specified that the changes that man remains capable of perceiving in divine actions
should only be conceived in relation to us (micydenu), and not in relation to God
(micydo) (M'eyr Ibn Gabay, 1890, 14a). In this sense, and contrary to Spinoza's use of
the adverb quatenus, the term micad underlines the uncrossable distance between the
divine and the human domains.

74

Conclusion

Starting from the fact that Spinoza was quickly considered an atheist, particularly by
Itshag Orobio de Castro, I began by analyzing this author's critique of the logic of the
Ethics. He showed that Spinozism consists in imperceptibly shifting from a reference
to God as traditionally defined by his uniqueness to His identification with multiple
nature. He also emphasized that such a shift uses a semantic procedure, retaining the
same reference but radically changing its meaning. He thus mentions reduplicative
formulas such as the term quatenus. In this article, I have attempted to show the
semantic role of the adverb quatenus in establishing the immanence of God in nature.
I have therefore supported the thesis that the distinction between intensional and
extensional orders reveals the difficulties of Spinozism, which is based entirely on an
illegitimate shift from one order to another. These difficulties concern, among others,
on the one hand, the notion of finite mode conceived as an affection of infinite
substance, and the deduction of the finite domain from the infinite, and on the other
hand, the permanent tension between the qualitative and quantitative points of view
that Spinozism never managed to resolve. Finally, I sought to compare Spinoza's use
of the adverb quatenus with that proposed by Jewish philosophy in the Middle Ages,
the Renaissance, and the classical period. While Spinoza's use of the term quatenus
seems to have been inspired by the Hebrew expressions be'ofen mah, micydo, or behynah,
which Spinoza may have found in Gersonides’ writings, among others, he completely
transformed their meaning. In fact, he attempted to identify the ontological levels that
these Hebrew expressions were specifically intended to distinguish.
