The Palestinian Nakba: Roots, Events

(In the history of the Palestinian question, 1882—2023)

Introduction

Studying the Palestinian question faces major methodological challenges, as it is intertwined
with conflicting national narratives, moral judgments, and political biases. This work aims to
offer a balanced historical reading, grounded in academic documentation, that examines
diverse accounts and analyzes events within their international and regional contexts, while
also considering the human and ethical dimensions.

Chapter One: The Distant Roots (1882-1918)
1.1 Palestine in the Late Ottoman Period

Until the late 19th century, Palestine was part of the Ottoman Empire, administratively
divided into several provinces. Its population was predominantly Muslim Arab, with Christian
and Jewish minorities who had lived in the holy cities for centuries. There was no Jewish
state or independent political entity; Palestine was simply part of the Ottoman Empire's
administrative structure.

Critical analysis: In his book The Palestinian Refugee Question, Israeli historian Benny

Morris argues that Palestinian society was a traditional, agrarian society lacking unifying

national political institutions before the Mandate. In contrast, Palestinian historian Walid

Khalidi points out that Palestine possessed a distinct Arab identity, and that the Jewish
presence was local and peaceful.

1.2 The Emergence of the Zionist Movement

With the rise of European nationalism and antisemitism, calls for a Jewish homeland
emerged. Theodor Herzl rose to prominence as the leading theorist, publishing his book The
Jewish State (1896), which advocated for the establishment of a Jewish state recognized by

the major powers. The First Zionist Congress convened in Basel (1897) and formulated a
political program aimed at:

- Encouraging organized migration.

- Buying land.
- Establishing economic institutions.
- Seeking international recognition.

Differing viewpoints:

- The Zionist narrative: It sees the movement as a national liberation movement for an
oppressed people, which sought to solve the problem of Jewish refugees by creating a safe
homeland.

- The Palestinian narrative: views the movement as a settler-colonial project aimed at
displacing the indigenous population.

- Critical academic narrative (such as Ilan Pappe in The Zionist Genocide): describes the
project as settler colonial, using modern tools of power to achieve its goals.

1.3 Beginnings of Jewish Immigration and Settlement

The period 1882-1914 witnessed waves of organized Jewish immigration (the First and
Second Aliyahs), accompanied by:

- Purchasing land from absentee landlords or from the Ottoman government.
- Establishing agricultural settlements (kibbutzim and moshavim).

- Establishment of financial funds (such as the Jewish National Fund, 1901).
- Establishing independent educational and economic institutions.

Critical analysis: In his book Palestinian Identity, historian Rashid Khalidi argues that these

migrations aroused Arab fears of demographic change, leading to early local protests (such

as the petitions of 1891). In contrast, historian Anita Shapira, in The Zionist Transformation,
argues that the migrations were vital for the revitalization of Jewish life in Palestine.

1.4 World War | and Parallel Agreements

World War | (1914-1918) represented a major turning point. The Ottoman Empire entered
the war on the side of Germany, while Britain:

- Gained Arab support through the Hussein—McMahon Correspondence (1915-1916), which
promised the Arabs independence in exchange for revolting against the Ottomans.

- Reached an understanding with the Zionist movement through the Balfour Declaration
(1917), which declared Britain's support for the establishment of a "national home for the
Jewish people in Palestine."

- Concluded the Sykes—Picot Agreement (1916) with France to divide spheres of influence in
the Levant.

Critical analysis: Historian Albert Hourani argues that these agreements represented the
pinnacle of colonial hypocrisy, as Britain's promises to the Arabs, Jews, and French
contradicted each other. Israeli historian Yigal Yadin, however, views the Balfour Declaration
as a diplomatic achievement for the Zionist movement.

Chapter Two: The British Mandate and the Struggle for Identity (1919-1939)
2.1 Establishing the British Mandate

Following the San Remo Conference (1920), Britain was granted the mandate over
Palestine, and the League of Nations adopted the Mandate Instrument (1922), which
included:

- Implementing the Balfour Declaration.
- Facilitating Jewish immigration.
- Recognizing the Jewish Agency as the representative of the Jews.
- Protecting the civil and religious rights of non-Jewish communities (in the absence of Arab
representative institutions).

Critical analysis: In The Shadow Army, historian Tom Segev argues that the British
administration was not neutral but rather facilitated the establishment of a Jewish state while
neglecting Arab demands. Conversely, in A History of Israel, historian Martin Gilbert
contends that Britain implemented the Mandate in good faith but faced difficulties in
reconciling its conflicting obligations.

2.2 The Rise of Jewish Immigration and the Growth of Zionist Institutions

The 1920s and 1930s saw a steady increase in the number of Jewish immigrants (especially
after Hitler came to power in 1933). Zionist institutions developed:

- Independent educational and health institutions.
- Labour unions (Histadrut).
- Defence forces (Haganah, Irgun, and Lehi).
- Financial and economic networks.

Critical analysis: Historian Shlomo Amar argues that these institutions formed the nucleus of
the state, while Palestinian historian Nur Masalha argues that these policies led to the
economic and political marginalization of Arabs.

2.3 The Palestinian National Movement and Revolutions
Palestine witnessed escalating confrontations:

- Disturbances of 1920-1921 (Jerusalem, Jaffa).

- The Buraq Uprising (1929) over the Western Wall, which resulted in deaths and injuries,
and led to the formation of an international commission that recognized the rights of Muslims
to ownership of the wall.

- The Great Palestinian Revolt (1936-1939), which began with a general strike and turned
into armed resistance, demanded:

- Stopping Jewish immigration.

- Preventing the transfer of land.

- Establishing a national government.

- Termination of the mandate.

Critical analysis: Historian Taysir Aruri argues that the revolt was the largest national
liberation movement in Palestine, but it was thwarted by British military superiority and
internal divisions. Israeli historian Yaakov Roy, however, believes that the revolt
demonstrated the Palestinians' capacity for organization, but ultimately failed to achieve its
goals.

2.4 The Peel Commission and the White Paper (1937-1939)

The Peel Commission (1937) recommended the partition of Palestine into two states, one
Arab and one Jewish, with Jerusalem remaining under British administration. The
Palestinians rejected the partition, while the Zionist leadership initially accepted it. In 1939,
Britain issued the White Paper, which restricted Jewish immigration and imposed limitations
on land sales, and indicated the possibility of a Palestinian state within ten years. The Zionist
movement rejected the White Paper, viewing it as a retreat from the Balfour Declaration.

Critical analysis: Historian Avi Shlaim argues that the White Paper was a belated British
attempt to appease the Arabs, but it came too late. Palestinian historian Walid Khalidi,
however, believes it failed to address the consequences of previous Mandate policies.

Chapter Three: From World War II to Partition (1939-1947)
3.1 World War II and Its Effects

Palestine became an important British military base. Thousands of Jews joined the British
Army and gained military and organizational experience. Local industries and supply
networks developed. In contrast, Palestinian society suffered the effects of the suppression
of the Great Revolt.

Critical analysis: Historian Martin van Creveld believes that World War Il was a qualitative
leap in Zionist military capabilities, while Palestinian historian Abdullah Hourani believes that
it weakened the Palestinian national movement.

3.2 Escalation of Zionist Operations Against Britain (1944-1947)

Zionist armed organizations (Irgun, Lehi) carried out operations against British targets (such
as the bombing of the King David Hotel in 1946), with the aim of forcing Britain to end the

mandate and open up immigration.

Critical analysis: British historian John Bull describes these operations as terrorist, while
Israeli historian Meir Bay sees them as national liberation operations.

3.3 The United Nations and the Partition Resolution (1947)

Britain referred the matter to the United Nations, and the UNSCOP committee was formed.
The majority recommended the partition of Palestine into two states, one Arab and one
Jewish, and the internationalization of Jerusalem. Resolution 181 was adopted on November
29, 1947. It was accepted by the Zionist leadership and rejected by the Arab and Palestinian
leaderships.

Critical analysis: Historian Michael Warshaw views the resolution as an international attempt
to resolve the conflict, but one that was unfair to the Arabs in terms of geographical
distribution. Israeli historian Yitzhak Rabin (in his memoirs), however, sees it as providing
international legitimacy to the Jewish state.

Chapter Four: The 1948 War and the Nakba (1947-1949)
4.1 The Civil War (1947-1948)

Immediately after the decision was issued, clashes broke out between Jewish forces and
Palestinian factions. Both sides focused on controlling roads and mixed cities. The period
witnessed:

- Plan Dalet (March 1948): An Israeli military plan to secure the areas designated for the
Jewish state, control supply routes, and clear strategic areas of Arab presence.
- The Battle of Al-Qastal (April 1948) and the killing of Abd al-Qadir al-Husseini.
- The Deir Yassin attack (April 9, 1948) carried out by the Irgun and Lehi, which resulted in
the killing of a large number of civilians, sparking panic that led to a mass exodus.
- The fall of Haifa and Jaffa into the hands of Jewish forces.

Critical analysis: Historians disagree about the role of Plan Dalet in the displacement:

- Benny Morris (the new Israeli historian): He believes that the plan was primarily military, but
its implementation led to displacement in the context of the war.
- llan Pappe (the new Israeli historian): He sees it as a systematic plan of extermination and
ethnic cleansing, as in his book The Ethnic Cleansing of Palestine.
- Walid Khalidi believes that it was part of a settlement project aimed at changing the
demographic composition.

Palestinian displacement (the Nakba):
By May 14, 1948, approximately 300,000 Palestinians had been displaced or forced to flee
their towns and villages. The reasons for displacement varied: direct fighting, fear,

evacuation orders, and massacres such as Deir Yassin.

4.2 Arab Intervention and the Declaration of the Establishment of Israel (May 1948)

On May 14, 1948, David Ben-Gurion declared the establishment of the State of Israel. On
May 15, the regular Arab armies (Egypt, Jordan, lraq, Syria, and Lebanon) entered
Palestine.

The declared objectives of the Arab intervention were:

- Preventing the implementation of partition.
- Protecting Palestinians.
- Establishing an Arab state in Palestine.

However, political and military differences between Arab states weakened coordination.

Critical analysis: Historian Ahmed Khalifa argues that Arab intervention was hesitant and
inconsistent, allowing Israel to expand its control. Israeli historian Gideon Pardo, however,
views the Arab armies as an existential threat to the nascent state.

4.3 Stages of War and Truces

- First truce (June 1948): The Israelis used it to strengthen and train their forces, and to
receive new arms shipments.
- Israeli military operations (July—October 1948): expanded control over the Galilee and the
Negev.
- Second truce (July 1948): was followed by repeated violations.
- The assassination of mediator Bernadotte (September 1948) by Lehi, which provoked
international condemnation.

4.4 Armistice Agreements (1949)

Israel signed separate armistice agreements with Egypt, Lebanon, Jordan, and Syria. The
armistice lines (the Green Line) became de facto borders, with Jerusalem remaining divided.

4.5 Results of the 1948 War

- Israel controlled 78% of the area of Mandatory Palestine.
- 700,000—750,000 Palestinians were displaced or forced to flee (according to United
Nations estimates).
- The West Bank came under Jordanian administration, and the Gaza Strip came under
Egyptian administration.
- Hundreds of Palestinian villages were destroyed or evacuated.
- Hundreds of new settlements were established on Palestinian land.

Critical analysis:

- The New Israeli Historians (Morris, Pappe, Shlaim) acknowledge the forced displacement,
but they disagree about its intent.
- Palestinian historians view it as a national catastrophe (the Nakba) perpetrated by Israel
with the help of international silence.

Chapter Five: After the Nakba (1949-1967)
5.1 The Refugee Issue and UNRWA

The United Nations established UNRWA (1949) to provide relief and employment for
Palestinian refugees. The refugee issue has become a central focus of the conflict, with
Palestinians insisting on their right of return, while Israel rejects it.

Critical analysis: Historian Onur Avni argues that the refugee issue replicates global patterns
of displacement due to wars, but has become the longest refugee crisis in modern history.

5.2 Absentee Property Law (1950)

Israel passed the Absentee Property Law, placing the property of Palestinians who left
during the war under official administration, and used a large part of it to house Jewish
immigrants, sparking ongoing legal controversy.

5.3 The Situation Inside Israel

Around 150,000 Palestinians remained within Israel's borders and later obtained citizenship,
but they lived under military rule until 1966, with restrictions on movement, property
ownership, and political activity.

5.4 Regional Developments

- The July 23, 1952 revolution in Egypt and the rise of Gamal Abdel Nasser.
- The Suez Crisis of 1956: Israel's occupation of Sinai and Gaza, followed by withdrawal
under US-Soviet pressure.
- The founding of the Fatah movement (1959) and the Palestine Liberation Organization
(1964).

Chapter Six: The 1967 War and Aftermath
6.1 The Six-Day War

On June 5, 1967, Israel launched a preemptive attack on Egypt, then Jordan and Syria. The
war ended with Israel controlling:

- The West Bank.

- East Jerusalem.

- The Gaza Strip.
- Sinai (until 1982).

- The Golan Heights.

Critical analysis: Historian Momen Mohammed believes that the war was the result of an
ill-considered Arab escalation, while Israeli historian Eli Harel sees it as a preemptive
defensive operation.

6.2 Resolution 242 (1967)

It called for Israel's withdrawal from territories occupied in the conflict, recognition of the right
of all countries in the region to live in peace, and a just settlement to the refugee problem.

6.3 A New Wave of Displacement (1967)

Some 300,000 Palestinians were displaced from the West Bank, Gaza, and the Golan
Heights, exacerbating the refugee crisis.

6.4 The Rise of Palestinian Resistance

The period 1967-1982 witnessed the emergence of Palestinian factions as a major military
and political force:

- The Battle of Karameh (1968) boosted the morale of the resistance.
- The events of Black September (1970) led to the organisation's departure from Jordan to
Lebanon.
- The October War of 1973 opened the door to negotiations with a change in the balance of
power.
- Arab recognition of the PLO (Rabat Summit 1974) as the sole legitimate representative of
the Palestinians.
- The Israeli invasion of Lebanon (1982) and the organisation's departure to Tunisia.

Chapter Seven: The First Intifada and Negotiations (1987-1993)
7.1 The First Intifada (1987-1993)

It erupted in the Gaza Strip and spread to the West Bank, characterised by its popular nature
(strikes, demonstrations, boycotts, civil disobedience). The Hamas movement emerged
during this period (late 1987).

Critical analysis: Historian Sari Nusseibeh argues that the uprising redefined the Palestinian
issue as a civil rights issue, while Israeli historian Zeev Schiff argues that it surprised Israel

and exposed the limits of military rule.

7.2 Declaration of Independence (1988)

The Palestinian National Council in Algeria declared the establishment of the State of
Palestine, and accepted Resolutions 242 and 338, thus opening the door to a political
solution.

7.3 Madrid Conference (1991)
The first direct negotiations between Israel, Palestine, Jordan, Syria, and Lebanon.
7.4 Oslo Accords (1993)

Mutual recognition between Israel and the PLO, and the establishment of a transitional
Palestinian Authority, with the postponement of final status issues (Jerusalem, refugees,
borders, settlements).

Critical analysis: Historian Rashid Khalidi believes that Oslo was a temporary solution that

did not address the core issues, while Israeli historian Ron Ben-Yishai sees it as a historic
opportunity lost due to extremism on both sides.

Chapter Eight: From Authority to the Second Intifada (1994—2005)
8.1 Establishment of the Palestinian Authority
The Palestinian Authority was established in 1994 as a transitional authority administering
parts of the West Bank and Gaza. The agreements came into effect amid significant internal

opposition.

Critical analysis: Historian Nathan Brown argues that the Palestinian Authority has become a
security apparatus subservient to Israel under the continued occupation.

8.2 The Assassination of Rabin (1995) and the Slowdown of Peace

Israeli Prime Minister Yitzhak Rabin was assassinated by a Jewish extremist, which
disrupted the peace process.

8.3 The Second Intifada (2000—2005)

It erupted in September 2000 following Ariel Sharon's visit to Al-Aqsa Mosque. It became
more violent, with Palestinian armed operations, large-scale Israeli military operations
(Operation Defensive Shield 2002), and the construction of the separation barrier.

Critical analysis: Historian Hussein Agha believes that the uprising was a result of the failure
of Oslo, while Israeli historian Tom Segev believes that it demonstrated the impossibility of a

military solution.

8.4 Withdrawal from Gaza (2005)

Israel withdrew from the Gaza Strip and evacuated the settlements, while retaining control
over the crossings, airspace, and waters.

Chapter Nine: The Division and the Gaza Wars (2006-2023)
9.1 The Palestinian Division (2006-2007)

Hamas won the legislative elections (2006), then seized control of Gaza (2007) after clashes
with Fatah. A tight blockade was imposed on the Strip.

9.2 Wars on Gaza

- Operation Cast Lead (2008-2009).
- Operation Pillar of Cloud (2012).
- Operation Protective Edge (2014).
- Operation Sword of Jerusalem (2021).
- Operation Al-Aqsa Flood (2023), and the subsequent Israeli war.

Critical analysis: Human rights organisations argue that these wars cause significant civilian
casualties and raise questions about compliance with international humanitarian law. Israel
says its objectives are to dismantle Hamas's military capabilities and rescue captives.

Chapter Ten: Core Issues Without Resolution

- Final borders: 1967 lines versus Israeli annexation.
- Jerusalem: its religious, political, and legal status.
- Refugees: The right of return in exchange for compensation.
- Settlements: their legitimacy, number, and impact.
- Security and water resources.
- The form and future of the Palestinian state.

British correspondence:
1. The Hussein-McMahon Correspondence (1915-1916).
2. The Sykes-Picot Agreement (1916).
3. The Balfour Declaration (1917).

Mandate documents:

Baruch Spinoza

Text 2: Spinoza was critical of the use of religion in carrying out political actions. He might
have questioned the religious justifications of both Palestinian extremist groups and the
more extreme sectors in Israel.

Text 3: Spinoza valued freedom of thought and expression. He might condemn restrictions
on freedom of expression and censorship, and support the right of Palestinians to tell their
story and fight for their rights.

deception. The pressure to pretend is central to a narrative about how repression severely
weakens the conditions for meaningful communication.

Text 5: Spinoza's exploration of hatred and the mechanisms of power provides a framework
for understanding how hatred can be used to justify mass violence, and how power can be
exercised without hatred through the pursuit of justice.

Spinoza's philosophy of necessity and ethics of joy can reveal its deeper meaning in the face

of destruction.

Hannah Arendt, Frantz Fanon, Michel Foucault

Arendt: Her concepts of the banality of evil and dehumanization can be applied to the war in
Gaza. The greatest evils are committed by ordinary people who obey orders without critical
thought and consider their victims less than human.

Fanon: His writings in The Wretched of the Earth provide a framework for understanding
Israeli colonial violence and Palestinian resistance. He argues that colonial violence is
systematic violence aimed at destroying the human dignity of the colonized, and that armed
resistance is a natural and legitimate response to this violence.

Foucault: His concept of biopolitics (power over life) explains how Israel manages the lives
and deaths of Palestinians through blockade, restrictions on movement, and control of
resources.

Chapter Thirteen: Summary and Recommendations
Established Facts

1. The conflict is rooted in a contradiction between two national projects on the same land.
2. The results of 1948 radically changed the demographic composition.

3. The refugee issue remains unresolved and represents the longest-running refugee crisis
in the world.

4. The continued occupation of the West Bank, Gaza, and Jerusalem is at the heart of the

conflict.

5. Settlements are illegal under international law and constitute a major obstacle to peace.

6. A just solution requires addressing the issues of: borders, Jerusalem, refugees, security,
and water.

- The military solution has not succeeded and will not succeed.
- A political solution requires international will and serious negotiations.
- Ignoring Palestinian rights does not lead to peace.
- Extremism on both sides fuels the conflict.
- International law should be the foundation, not be exploited as a political tool.

The Need for a Fair Settlement

- The two-state solution remains the most internationally accepted framework.
- Jerusalem needs a special arrangement that recognizes the religious importance for
everyone.
- Refugees need a solution that reconciles the right of return with compensation.
- Security must be mutual, not unilateral.
- Settlements need to be frozen or removed, and dismantled illegally.

The International Role

- The United Nations must play a more effective role.
- International courts can contribute to the enforcement of international law.

- Major powers must adopt balanced, not biased, policies.
- Civil society and human rights organizations are essential for monitoring violations.

The Palestinian issue is not merely a conflict over land; it is a struggle for identity, memory,
and justice. It is a human rights issue, an international law issue, and a moral issue. Any
lasting solution must recognize the rights of all parties and be based on equality, justice, and
peace.

Chapter Fourteen: The Beating Heart of the Conflict
14.1 Jerusalem in History

Jerusalem is a city of immense religious significance for the three Abrahamic faiths: Islam,
Christianity, and Judaism. The Al-Aqsa Mosque (comprising the Dome of the Rock and the
Qibli Mosque) is the third holiest site in Islam. The Church of the Holy Sepulchre is the
holiest site for Christians. The Western Wall (also known as the Wailing Wall) is the holiest
site for Jews.

Critical analysis: In Jerusalem: One City, Three Religions, historian Karen Armstrong argues
that the city has always been a place of coexistence and tension, and that the current
conflict is not purely religious, but rather a national and political conflict that uses religion as

a cover.

14.2 Jerusalem during the Ottoman and Mandate Periods

Under Ottoman rule, Jerusalem was an Arab city with a Muslim majority and Christian and
Jewish minorities. During the British Mandate, Jerusalem became a center of national
conflict. In 1947, the partition plan stipulated the internationalization of Jerusalem, but the
war resulted in its de facto division between Israel and Jordan.

Critical analysis: Historian Tom Segev, in One Jerusalem, Two Jerusalems, argues that the
division of Jerusalem was a compromise, but it did not last long.

14.3 Jerusalem in the 1967 War
In June 1967, Israel captured East Jerusalem and annexed the Old City. In July 1980, the
Knesset passed the Jerusalem Law, which declared a unified Jerusalem as the capital of
Israel, a move rejected by the international community in Resolution 478.
Critical analysis: Historian Rashid Khalidi argues that the annexation of East Jerusalem was
a violation of international law, while Israeli historian Moshe Amirav argues that it was

necessary for Israeli national security.

14.4 Jerusalem Today

Today, East Jerusalem is home to approximately 350,000 Palestinians, while West
Jerusalem is home to approximately 200,000 Israelis. The city also includes Israeli
settlements, such as Gilo, Ramat Shlomo, and Har Homa, which are considered illegal
under international law. UNRWA and human rights organizations document the displacement
of Palestinians from Jerusalem, home demolitions, and building restrictions.

Critical analysis: Human rights activist Jeff Halper argues that Israel's policies in Jerusalem
are aimed at changing the demographic composition, while Israel says it maintains freedom
of worship for all religions.

Chapter Fifteen: The Refugee Issue — The World's Longest-Standing Refugee Crisis
15.1 Figures and Statistics

By 2023, the number of Palestinian refugees registered with UNRWA was approximately 5.9
million, distributed as follows:

- Jordan: 2.4 million.
- Gaza: 1.4 million.
- West Bank: 870,000.
- Syria: 560,000.
- Lebanon: 480,000.
- Other countries: 200,000.

Critical analysis: Researcher Leif Feinberg argues that UNRWA's figures are inflated due to
the adoption of an expanded definition of a refugee that includes successive generations,
while Palestinian researcher Salam Tamari argues that the figures underestimate the scale
of the disaster.

15.2 Right of Return and Compensation

- Resolution 194 (1948) stipulated the right of refugees to return to their homes and receive
compensation.
- Resolution 242 (1967) spoke of a "just settlement" of the refugee problem.
- The Arab Peace Initiative (2002) stipulated an agreed solution to the refugee issue.

Different positions:

- The Palestinian position: The right of return is non-negotiable; it is an individual and
collective right.
- The Israeli position: The right of return is not applicable due to demographic changes, and
compensation is the solution.
- International position: Supporting a compromise solution that combines limited return and
compensation.

15.3 UNRWA — Role and Presence

UNRWA provides education, healthcare, and social services to more than 5 million refugees.
Israel considers UNRWA an obstacle to peace because it creates a growing demographic
reality, while Palestinians consider it a lifeline and a symbol of the right of return.

Chapter Sixteen: Settlements — An Obstacle to Peace
16.1 History of Settlement

Israeli settlement activity in the West Bank and Gaza began after 1967, with the
establishment of settlements in strategic areas. Settlement expansion increased significantly
during the 1990s and the first decade of the 21st century, particularly in major settlement
blocs such as Ma'ale Adumim, Ariel, and Gush Etzion.

Critical analysis: Historian Jeff Halper argues that settlement was part of a colonial project to
change the geographical reality, while Israeli historian Daniel Gutman sees it as a legitimate
security and settlement necessity.

16.2 Legality of Settlements
- Resolution 2334 (2016) affirmed the illegality of settlements under international law.
- The International Court of Justice (2004) said that the separation wall in the West Bank is
illegal and affects settlement activity.

- Israel rejects this view, arguing that the settlements are not an occupation but rather
disputed territories, and that international law does not apply to them in the same way.
16.3 The Impact of Settlements on Peace
Settlements hinder peace because:

- The Palestinian territories are divided.
- They create a demographic reality that is difficult to change.

- They deprive Palestinians of natural resources (water, agricultural land).
- They create daily tension with the Palestinian population.

Chapter Seventeen: Lessons from Similar International Experiences
17.1 South Africa and Apartheid

Many activists and scholars have compared Israel to the apartheid regime in South Africa
because of:

- A separate legal system for Palestinians and Israelis in the West Bank.
- Restrictions on movement and transportation.

- Land confiscation.
- Military government.

Critical analysis: Activist Desmond Tutu believes that comparing Israel to South Africa is
legitimate, while Anthony Lewis rejects it because Israel is a democratic state for its citizens,
whereas the apartheid regime practiced segregation within its sovereign territory.

17.2 Northern Ireland and Sectarian Conflict
Northern Ireland witnessed a conflict between Catholic nationalists and Protestants loyal to
Britain, which ended with the Good Friday Agreement (1998), which established a political
solution based on participation and equality.

Lessons learned:

- The solution requires political will and international mediation.

- Full equality and mutual recognition are prerequisites for peace.

- Disarmament must be reciprocal and internationally supervised.

17.3 Bosnia and Ethnic Division
The war in Bosnia ended with the Dayton Agreement (1995), which divided the country
along ethnic and sectarian lines with a weak central government. The agreement did not
address the root causes of the conflict, but it did end the war.

Lessons learned:

- A solution that satisfies all parties may fall short of perfection.

- International intervention may be necessary, but it is not sufficient.
- Reconciliation takes a long time.

Chapter Nineteen: The Oslo Accords — Details and Background
19.1 The Secret Oslo Negotiations

In early 1993, secret talks began in Oslo between Israeli and Palestinian officials, under
Norwegian auspices. Behind the scenes, the two sides worked on drafting a declaration of
principles.

Critical analysis: Political analyst Anmed Qurei (Abu Ala) believes that the agreement was
the best that could be achieved at that time, while critic Edward Said believes that it was a
surrender to Israeli conditions.

19.2 Agreement Terms

- Israel's withdrawal from Gaza and Jericho.
- Establishing a Palestinian transitional authority.
- Negotiations on the final status after 5 years.
- The issues of Jerusalem, refugees, and settlements are left to final negotiations.

19.3 Reasons for Failure

: Failure to fully implement the agreements.
- Continued settlement activity in the West Bank.
- Violence escalated on both sides.
- The 2000 Camp David summit was a failure.
- Lack of trust between the two parties.
- Changes in leadership on both sides.

Critical analysis: Historian Onur Avni argues that Oslo failed because Israel wanted a
unilateral peace, while the Palestinian Authority wanted to end the occupation.

Chapter Twenty: Regional and International Roles
20.1 United States

The United States is Israel's biggest political, economic, and military backer. It uses its veto
power in the Security Council to shield Israel from critical resolutions. It has played a key role
in peace negotiations, but is often accused of bias.

20.2 European Union

It supports a two-state solution and provides substantial aid to the Palestinians, but lacks
direct political influence. It criticizes settlements, but does not take strong punitive measures.

20.3 Russia

It played a role in the peace negotiations (Madrid Conference) and supports a two-state
solution, but it uses the issue as leverage in its relations with the West.

20.4 China

It adopts a pro-Palestinian stance, but focuses on economic interests with Israel and Arab
states, and avoids direct involvement.

20.5 Arab Countries

- Egypt: The first Arab country to sign a peace treaty with Israel (1979), and played a role in
mediating between the Palestinian factions and Israel.
- Jordan: Signed a peace treaty with Israel (1994), and hosts large numbers of refugees.
- Syria and Lebanon: They support the resistance but are militarily weak.
- Iran: Supports Hamas and Hezbollah, and considers Israel a major enemy.
- Gulf States: Adopted a gradual normalization with Israel (Abraham Accords 2020).

Chapter Twenty-Three: Summary and Recommendations
23.1 What We Have Learned

- The conflict is complex, multi-dimensional, and has deep roots.
- The military solution has not succeeded and will not succeed.
- A political solution requires will and sacrifices from both sides.
- International law should be the foundation, not a tool for exerting pressure.
- The international community is responsible for supporting peace, not for freezing crises.
- Reconciliation requires time, effort, and participation of all parties.

23.2 Recommendations for a Solution

- Mutual recognition of the rights and legitimacy of both parties.
- A halt to settlement activity and a freeze on it as a condition for negotiation.
- A just solution to the refugee issue that reconciles the right of return with compensation.
- An international status for the city of Jerusalem that respects all religions.
- Mutual security arrangements that guarantee the safety of all parties.
- An effective international role (United Nations, European Union, United States, Russia,
China) to ensure the implementation of agreements.
- Supporting the building and strengthening of Palestinian state institutions.

- Reconciliation programmes between peoples at the popular and official levels.
23.3 A Call to History

The Palestinian cause is not merely a conflict over land; it is a struggle for identity, memory,
and justice. It is a human rights issue, an international law issue, and a moral issue. Any
lasting solution must recognize the rights of all parties and be based on equality, justice, and
peace.

The Nakba is not just a memory; it is a living reality experienced by millions of Palestinians
today. The Nakba is not the end of history, but the beginning of a long struggle. Peace is not
a dream, but a necessity and a possibility. What is required is will, courage, and humanity.
The history of Palestine teaches us that injustice does not last, that truth does not die, and
that peace is born from the womb of suffering.

Future generations will ask us: what did you do? What did you leave behind? The answer
will determine the future of the region and the future of humanity.
