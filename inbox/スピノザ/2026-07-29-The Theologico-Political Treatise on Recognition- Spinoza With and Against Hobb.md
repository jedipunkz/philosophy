## Abstract

This article is the second part of a comparison between Hobbes’s and Spinoza’s theories of recognition as they appear in De Cive and the Tractatus theologico-politicus. It analyzes the impact of admiration, both of God and of man, on social and moral relations, as well as on political ties. This analysis reveals a dual discourse that to some extent echoes that found in De Cive. Concerning social and moral relations, this article demonstrates that the Treatise, similarly to De Cive, downplays the capacity of the desire for recognition to drive socialization and moral development. Instead, it underscores the potential of this desire to generate conflict and social breakdown. Spinoza overlooks how Hobbes’s discussion of worship allows for the emergence of an intersubjective and cooperative origin of both subjectivities and power, an origin that is constitutively mediated by recognition.  Moreover, Spinoza introduces a critique of the falsity of recognition and the virtues it fosters that is absent from Hobbes’s work. On the political level, the article argues that the Treatise addresses the subtler dimension of Hobbes’s theory, namely, the idea that all political power rests on the cooperation of the governed, a cooperation made possible through recognition. At the same time, by identifying the libera respublica as the only legitimate form of state, Spinoza finds himself in opposition to Hobbes’s implicit and explicit discussions alike. He must reject the idea that recognition bestowed upon non-democratic authorities provides ideological support for servitude. Moreover, he redefines the politically positive function of recognition, arguing that its true value lies in its potential to incite resistance against non-democratic powers.

Introduction

If it is unlikely that Spinoza had knowledge of Leviathan during the writing of the Tractatus theologico-politicus, considering that it is difficult to find in the Tractatus theologico-politicus
(hereafter Treatise) any trace of the arguments that distinguish Leviathan from De Cive, his acquaintance with Hobbes’s later work at the final stage of the composition of the Ethics is probable, though definitive evidence is elusive. Despite the difficulty, even today, of determining how much
Spinoza knew of Hobbes at this or that moment in his life, it has never been doubted that he had carefully read the De Cive at the height of the Treatise. A comparative approach to these two works and their theses is, however, complicated by the fact that the Treatise and De Cive (as well as the
Ethics and Leviathan) prove to be incomparable for a variety of reasons related to the diversity of their aims, the subjects they deal with, the methods they use, the theories they put forward, the cultural and political contexts in which their two authors operate, and the audiences for which their authors write. The aim of De Cive is to offer a general theory of justice, rooted in a realistic anthropology and capable of defining a rational morality of peace and sovereignty as the sole guarantor of this morality and its rationality. The aim of the Tractatus, made explicit in its title, is to prove that “the Republic can grant freedom of philosophizing without harming [salva] its peace or piety, and cannot deny it without destroying its peace and piety” (TTP, title/C II 65).2 Since the pietas which should be “saved” along with peace is obedience to divine command, which implies belief in certain dogmas, and since in our time “we have no prophets” (TTP 1.6/C II 78) and divine word seems therefore to coincide with Scripture, detractors of libertas philosophandi argue that it endangers pietas and peace by shirking the authority of Scripture. Spinoza wants instead to show that pietas and peace are inseparable from libertas itself and compatible with the authority of Scripture. Thus,
De Cive argues for its thesis based on a precise conception of man’s fundamental needs and his natural condition, explains the natural laws that should enable these needs to be satisfied, then examines the state in its various forms and functions, its rights and duties, and only occasionally, in its first two sections, does it enter into dialogue with sacred texts. The Treatise, however, argues for its thesis through constant dialogue mainly with the Old Testament, and thus with the narratives it contains concerning the political history of a particular people, with the major concepts mobilized by these narratives (such as those of prophecy, election, law, miracle), and with the institutions to which they bear witness. Unlike De Cive, therefore, the Treatise contains no detailed general reflections on the state of nature, on the articulation of natural laws, or on different forms of government. This radical difference in argumentation is accompanied by a multitude of differences in content. From a moral point of view, to take just one example, De Cive focuses on the laws of nature as instruments to promote outer peace that is useful to avoid the summum malum of violent death and disregards the idea of a supreme good capable of guaranteeing perpetual inner peace. By contrast, the Treatise conceives natural laws as means to the supreme good of man, identified with a knowledge and love of God that makes one with true freedom, true virtue, and true tranquility of

These radical differences between the two works, however, do not imply the absence of any relationship between them, and the purpose of this second of my companion pair of articles is precisely to highlight an underappreciated aspect of the influence that the former work has had on the latter, by studying the way in which the analysis of the theme of recognition offered by Spinoza in the Treatise interfaces with that offered by Hobbes in De Cive. In the first article, which was devoted to De Cive and which was published in issue 1/2025 of Consecutio rerum, I showed that the account of recognition that Hobbes develops in the 1641 work is not a simple theory that can be reconstructed in a linear form, because it is developed on two different and, at least apparently, contradictory discursive levels.3 On the first level, which is the one most closely related to the notion of glory and most prominent in the first two sections of the work, and which, not surprisingly, scholars have paid the most attention to, the desire for recognition appears from an anthropological, moral, and political point of view as the main factor of conflict, violation of natural laws, and contestation of sovereign authority. At the second level, which appears in the first two sections of the works only in marginal passages and does not become central until the discussion on worship presented in in the third section, but which is nonetheless, in my opinion, the more fundamental of the two, recognition appears as a passion capable of promoting not only social relations of mutual empowerment, independent of the contractual form and the presence of sovereign power, but also a certain morality among the partners in cooperation and, finally, both the consolidation and the contestation of political power. Obviously, it is not possible to offer a simple and straightforward reconstruction of Spinoza’s reception of such a layered theory, if only because, in order to understand
Spinoza’s view of Hobbes, we must also verify whether Spinoza interacts only with the theses that can be detected from the first level of Hobbes’s discourse, accessible to any moderately attentive reader, only with those of the second level, accessible only to a particularly attentive reader, or both.
From this perspective, it will then be necessary to show that, in the Treatise, Spinoza does not uncritically absorb the unaltered ideas of De Cive, but selectively and actively takes up these ideas through a reworking that sometimes coincides with a real reversal of perspective and the introduction of issues that are essentially absent in Hobbes. Admiration of God and of the Fellow Man

In De Cive, the fundamental role that honor plays behind the scenes does not prevent glory from assuming the leading role when it comes to recognition. Identifying a concept with a function like that of Hobbes’s glory is more difficult in the Treatise. In order to grasp this concept and understand its uses, four things need to be noted: first, the privilege Spinoza gives to the relationship between man and God; second, the way in which the structure of this relationship is reconstructed around the notion of admiration; third, the extension of the same structure to individual and collective relations between men; and fourth, the epistemological focus of Spinoza’s discussion. In De Cive, the concept of glory was mobilized in the anthropological, ethical, and political spheres independently of the concept of worship, which is considered in the section on religion. In the Treatise, however, it is difficult to separate the question of recognition from the theological field in which it arises: since recognition is considered first as man’s recognition of God, and only secondarily as one man’s recognition of another, its primary form is that of worship. As is evident from his recovery of the concept of glory, Spinoza identifies the structure resulting from the relationship between Yahweh and other Gods in the eyes of the Hebrews and the structure informing the relations of recognition between men, organized by passions at times different from admiration. Just as in De Cive Hobbes removes the relation to power that in other works is central to his discourse on glory and allows it to reappear only in sections on worship, so in the Treatise the discourse on glory removes the relation to power that is central to the discourse on honoring God.
As for Hobbes, moreover, for Spinoza glory belongs to no one if it belongs to all, because the enjoyment of one’s self-image is inseparable from the enjoyment of one’s imaginary superiority over another. “The true happiness and blessedness of each person consists only in the enjoyment of the good, and not in a self-esteem [gloria] founded on the fact that he alone enjoys the good, all others being excluded from it,” or in perverse joy of being “more blessed and more fortunate than others”: the one who takes pleasure on this exclusion “rejoices because of an evil occurring to someone else” and is therefore “envious and evil, failing to know either true wisdom or the peace of true life” (TTP 3.1/C II 111).
Having grasped the far from linear ways in which Spinoza’s notion of admiration takes on a similar function to that played by glory at the first level of Hobbes’s theory,13 we still need, in order to fully appreciate the interplay that unites the dominant notions of the theories of recognition elaborated by De Cive and the Treatise, to note how Spinoza’s discourse on admiration contains an epistemological focus that is absent from Hobbes’s discourse on glory. Certainly, Hobbes does not fail to distinguish glory and vainglory from the accuracy or inaccuracy of the self-esteem on which they are based. But Spinoza goes further. To sum up, it is possible to distinguish three levels of discourse on God and man in the Treatise. The first is the level of philosophy; the second is the level of religion, especially the Catholic religion; and the third is the level of superstition. Now, admiration does not play a role at the philosophical level. At this level, which is not developed in the Treatise as it might have been in the Short Treatise and as it will be in a very different way in the Ethics,
God is Nature, his power is that of Nature itself, his eternal and necessary decrees are those that establish the unchangeable order of Nature, his message, if there can be one, is inscribed in the minds of men and must be understandable by the natural light. 3.

Individual or Collective Recognition and Conflict

In the Treatise, anthropological reflection is present only fragmentarily and mainly within analyses of the events, beliefs, prophecies, narratives and institutions of the Jewish people. Spinoza’s conception of desire for recognition does not emerge directly from a preliminary and privileged moment of the discourse, as Hobbes’s conception does from the analysis of the state of nature, but indirectly from the treatment of a variety of ethico-political aspects of the Jewish religion. These insights, however, make it possible to draw some conclusions on a level that it is not precisely the anthropological one concerning the characteristics peculiar to man in every place and time, but that nonetheless touches on the theme that in De Cive was at the heart of the anthropological question: that of sociality.
