Eighteenth  century  America  was  dominated  by  three  great  powers:
France,  whose  empire  stretched  from  the  mouth  of  the  St  Lawrence  to
the  mouth  of  the  Mississippi;  Spain,  which  dominated  Florida;  and  England,
which  controlled  the  rest  of  the  colonies  along  the  East  Coast.

One  of  the  principal  causes  for  the  emigration  of  the  German  speaking
peoples  to  America  was  to  escape  persecution  by  the  Catholics  in  the
mother  country.  Britain  was  the  only  Protestant  power  in  the  colonies.
With  some  exceptions,  therefore,  most  German  emigrants  in  the  first  half
of  the  eighteenth  century  settled  on  British  territory.  In  the  southeast,
this  comprised  North  and  South  Carolina,  and  Georgia.  The  settlers  were
later  able  to  move  further  west  when  French  positions  were  challenged
by  the  British  during  the  great  French  and  Indian  war,  in  the  latter  half

of  the  eighteenth  century.

The  very  earliest  records  of  Germans  in  the  southeast  go  back  to  the  late
seventeenth  century,  and  show  German  explorers  in  the  Carolinas.

The  first  was  Peter  Fabian,  a  Swiss  and  a  member  of  the  expedition  sent
out  in  1663  by  the  Carolina  Company  in  England.  His  report  was
contained  in  the  earliest  history  of  Carolina  by  John  Lawson,  London,

1709.

More  extensive  discoveries  were  made  by  John  Lederer,  a  German  who
was  sent  by  Sir  William  Berkeley,  governor  of  Virginia,  to  explore  the
land  south  and  west  of  the  James  River  in  1669  and  1670.  At  this  time,
North  and  South  Carolina,  containing  only  two  white  settlements,  were
one  province,  governed  by  the  Lords  Proprietors  under  the  trusteeship  of

Charles  II.

From  Lederer’s  map  and  journal,  it  has  been  ascertained  that  he  passed
through  North  Carolina  and  into  South  Carolina  as  far  as  t  e  ^nt^e
River.  The  journal,  written  in  Latin,  was  translated  and  published  by
Sir  William  Talbot,  governor  of  Maryland,  after  Lederer  fled  to  ttat
state  from  Virginia.  Englishmen  who  accompanied  him  on  his  expedition
soon  had  abandoned  him  and  turned  back  to  blacken  his  name  m  his
home  state.  Lederer,  who  continued  his  explorations  alone,  was  therefore

forced  to  flee  on  his  return.

A  third  early  explorer  was  the  direct  cause  of  the  earliest  German
settlement  in  the  Carolinas.  Louis  Michel  was  sent  by  his  home  canton,
Bern,  to  select  a  suitable  tract  for  a  Swiss  settlement  there.  Travelling
also  in  Maryland  and  Pennsylvania,  Michel  finally  chose  a  site  in  ort
Carolina.  This  became  the  town  of  New  Bern.

Palatines  among  them.  Driven  from  their  homes  by  the  accu™lated

While  imprisoned  at  Frederica,  a  somewhat  curious  incident  occurred.  An
arsenal  exploded  next  to  his  cell,  but  instead  of  running  for  safety  or
trying  to  escape,  Priber  simply  squatted  in  his  cell  until  the  danger  had
passed.  Soon  after  this,  he  died  of  fever,  his  elaborate  plans  for  a  new
society  dying  with  him.

Fever  also  took  the  lives  of  many  of  the  members  of  the  third  ‘transport*
who  settled  in  New  Ebenezer.  As  the  summer  heat  increased,  they  died  off
as  quickly  as  the  first  group.  The  new  settlers  were  also  inhibited  by  lack
of  tools;  Oglethorpe  had  sent  all  their  supplies  to  Frederica,  and  they  were
slow  to  arrive  in  New  Ebenezer.  Von  Reck  was  blamed  for  this,  along
with  openly  cheating  the  settlers:  when  Bolzius  wrote  to  Oglethorpe
requesting  tools,  he  also  accused  von  Reck  of  withholding  provisions  he
had  bought  at  his  own  initiative  “for  a  great  prize  and  for  a  little  use.”

Bolzius  disliked  both  von  Reck  and  Commissioner  Vat,  both  of  whom  he
accused  of  cheating  the  settlers.  Von  Reek’s  irreverence  was  criticised,  as
when  he  fired  shots  to  celebrate  Vat’s  departure  for  Europe  while  people
lay  sick.  Bolzius  was  also  displeased  at  the  settlers  von  Reck  had  recruited
for  the  third  transport:  believing  them  to  be  intended  to  reinforce  the
garrison  at  Frederica,  von  Reck  had  taken  many  who  were  not  pious
Salzburgers,  or  even  Christians.  Commissioner  Vat  was  accused  of  with¬
holding  supplies  from  the  Salzburgers.  Both  commissioners  eventually
returned  to  Europe,  Bolzius  assuming  responsibility  for  the  distribution
of  all  supplies.  The  pastor,  however,  always  acted  in  his  congregation’s
best  interests,  as  when  he  gave  up  a  comfortable  parsonage  and  risked  the
displeasure  of  Oglethorpe  and  the  SPCK  to  move  to  Red  Bluff  for  their
good.

While  von  Reck  did  not  perhaps  always  have  the  settlers’  interests  at
heart,  the  enthusiasm  he  displays  in  his  travel  diaries  is  charming.  He  pre¬
sents  a  lively  and  fascinating  picture  of  colonial  America,  and  his  astute
observations  on  the  Indians  and  the  Negro  slaves  reveal  a  sympathetic,
compassionate  nature.  On  his  return  to  Europe,  von  Reck  married  an
heiress  ten  years  older  than  he  was,  and  occupied  responsible  positions
first  in  the  Hanoverian,  then  in  the  Danish  civil  service.  His  displacement
in  Georgia,  where  he  was  also  prevented,  by  Bolzius,  from  taking  land
rightfully  belonging  to  the  ‘commoners’,  shows  an  interesting  reversal  of
roles  from  the  mother  country,  characteristic  of  the  New  World  where
everyone  presumably  had  an  equal  chance.

Another  example  ,of  the  Salzburgers’  charity  was  their  orphanage,  the  first

modelled  on  the  one  founded  by  A.  H.  Francke  in  Halle.  Maintaining  its
children  —  three  boys  and  eight  girls  to  begin  with  —  at  a  cost  of  three

worship  until  a  church  was  built.  The  English  Evangelist  George  White-
field  visited  it,  later  to  use  it  as  the  pattern  for  his  own  orphanage  in

Savannah.

The  Salzburgers  were  naturally  also  a  powerful  religious  influence  in  the
community,  largely  due  to  a  succession  of  fine  pastors.

The  first  of  these,  Bolzius  and  his  assistant  Gronau  actually  became  broth¬
ers-in-law,  by  marrying  two  sisters.  When  Gronau  died  in  1945,  Boziuss
duties  were  overwhelming.  He  not  only  preached  to  his  own  congregation,
but  also  to  those  of  Savannah,  and  Goshen  and  Zion,  two  outlying  Ger¬
man  settlements.  In  addition,  he  had  to  attend  to  the  administration  of
the  by  that  time  considerable  property  of  Ebenezer.  A  replacement  for
Gronau  was  therefore  sent  in  the  person  of  the  Reverend  Hermann  H.
Lembke  from  Halle,  who  lived  to  serve  nearly  thirty  years  and  married
Gronau’s  widow.  The  work  load  still  too  great,  a  third  pastor,  named
Christian  Rabenhorst  was  sent,  arriving  with  a  group  of  Wurttembergers
in  1752.  Bolzius  died  in  1765,  having  delegated  responsibility  for  Church
property  to  Lembke,  who  in  turn  passed  it  to  Rabenhorst  before  his  own
death.  Rabenhorst  lived  25  years  in  Ebenezer.

The  Lutheran  Church  under  these  men  contrasted  greatly  with  the  Angli¬
can  Church  in  Savannah,  which  had  many  unsuccessful  ministers  and  was
often  without  one  at  all.  It  has  been  remarked  that  Ebenezer  had  three
pastors  and  no  tavernkeeper,  while  Savannah  had  three  tavernkeepers
and  no  pastor!  During  Lembke’s  ministry,  Jerusalem  Church  was  begun
and  completed  in  1769.  Unlike  the  other  three  churches  built  at  Zion,
Goshen  and  Bethany,  it  is  still  standing  and  active  today.

The  Saxe-Gothans  had  a  Reformed  minister,  Reverend  Christian  Theus,
who  arrived  in  1739.  Informing  the  provincial  government  that  if  the
German  settlers  were  not  provided  with  churches  and  schools,  they  would
migrate  to  Pennsylvania  where  such  were  to  be  found,  he  was  promptly
awarded  500  pounds  to  build  a  church.  The  church,  situated  a  few  miles
from  Columbia,  was  named  St  John’s.

A  somewhat  gruesome  tale  is  told  of  Theus’s  ministry.  Several  years  after
his  arrival,  he  was  forced  to  flee  some  of  his  own  parish  who  were  mem¬
bers  of  a  sect  called  the  “Weberites”.  Happening  on  one  of  their  meetings,
he  found  Jacob  Weber,  the  leader,  contending  he  was  God,  while  a  Peter
Schmidt  maintained  he  was  Christ,  and  the  unconverted  must  be  healed
by  stripes”.  When  Theus  protested,  his  life  was  threatened,  but  he  escaped
across  the  river.  Weber  was  eventually  hanged  when  he  selected  one  of
his  followers  as  the  reincarnation  of  Satan  and  had  him  brutally  murdered.
Fortunately,  the  Weberites  were  highly  unrepresentative  of  the  majority
of  German  pioneers,  who  were  pious  and  hard-working.

Members  of  this  sect,  which  had  its  origins  in  the  Hussite  Unitas  Fratrum
of  Bohemia  and  Moravia,  had  first  arrived  in  the  colonies  with  the  Salz¬

Wesley  who  is  said  to  have  realised  the  true  meaning  of  faith  when  he  saw

sectarian  threat  to  its  authority.

Once  in  Georgia,  the  47  Moravians  began  the  kind  of  evangelistic  work
which  characterizes  the  sect  still  today,  establishing  a  school  for  the  In¬
dians  at  Irene,  said  to  have  stood  a  mile  above  Savannah  on  an.  island
occupied  by  the  Creeks  in  the  middle  of  the  Savannah  River.  Their  mis¬
sionary  work  soon  ended  here,  however,  for  shortly  after  their  arrival,
Savannah  began  preparing  for  war  with  the  Spaniards.  Refusing  to  bear
arms,  the  Moravians  petitioned  Oglethorpe  to  be  allowed  to  go  to  Penn¬

they  had  all  left  Georgia,  continuing  their  work  in  Pennsylvania  and  New
York  and  establishing  the  settlements  Bethlehem  and  Nazareth.

In  1751,  they  purchased  100,000  acres  in  North  Carolina.  Their  Bishop,
August  Gottlieb  Spangenberg,  who  had  previously  been  expelled  from
the  University  of  Halle  for  his  beliefs,  went  to  survey  the  land,  eventually
locating  the  tract  in  Forsyth  County,  to  the  east  of  the  Yadkin  River.
The  deed  for  the  land  was  signed  on  August  7,  1753,  and  the  land  received
the  name  Wachovia  in  honor  of  a  former  estate  of  Count  von  Zinzendorf,
the  Moravians’  Saxon  protector.  The  Wachovia  Society,  dissolved  in  1763
after  it  had  successfully  served  its  purpose,  was  then  founded  under  Span-
genberg’s  direction  to  help  finance  the  venture.

In  Autumn,  1753,  the  first  settlers  arrived,  twelve  single  Moravian  breth¬
ren  with  a  wagon,  six  horses,  cattle,  and  the  necessary  household  and
agricultural  implements.  On  the  spot  where  they  built  their  hut,  a
monument  bearing  the  inscription  “Wachovia  Settlement,  begun  the
17th  November,  1753”,  was  erected  in  1802.  In  1754,  the  first  group
was  joined  by  seven  more  single  men.  Together  they  built  a  village  with
a  mill,  to  which  people  are  said  to  have  ,  come  from  many  miles  to  have
their  grain  ground.  The  place  became  a  refuge  for  travellers  and  was
named  Bethabara,  “house  of  passage”.  The  Cherokees  and  the  Catawbas,
at  war  with  the  Ohio  Indians  in  1758,  often  passed  through  the  village.
Well  pleased  with  the  treatment  they  received  there,  they  called  the
place  “the  Dutch  fort,  where  there  are  good  people  and  much  bread”.
Bethany,  the  second  town  built  by  the  Moravians  on  the  Wachovia
tract,  was  laid  out  in  1759,  three  miles  north  of  Bethabara  on  Muddy
Creek.  Six  years  later  it  had  78  inhabitants,  and  Bethabara  ten  more.
The  third  town,  Salem,  the  Winston-Salem  of  today,  was  begun  in
1766,  five  miles  southeast  of  Bethabara.  It  became  the  most  important
settlement  in  the  region.  As  a  sign  of  the  growing  prominence  of  the
area,  ten  new  colonists,  hitherto  all  from  Pennsylvania,  arrived  direct
from  Germany  the  year  Salem  was  begun.

Salem  was  realised  by  Frederick  William  Marshall,  chief  officer  for
material  affairs  in  Wachovia,  educated  at  the  University  of  Leipzig.
He  was  assisted  by  Christian  Reuter,  a  Moravian  surveyor.  They  planned
the  town  so  that  important  buildings  were  gouped  around  a  large
square,  alongside  which  ran  the  main  street.  By  April,  1772,  the  town
was  ready  for  occupancy,  and  the  brethren  moved  there  ‘en  masse’  from
Bethabara,  leaving  this  a  small  rural  community.

The  last  large  body  of  German  emigrants  to  the  Carolinas  settled,  in
1763,  at  Hard  Labor  Creek  in  Abbeville  County,  in  western  South
Carolina  at  the  Georgia  border.  The  originator  of  the  settlement  was  a
German  officer  named  Stiimpel  who  tried  to  speculate  in  German
colonists  after  his  discharge  from  Frederick  the  Great’s  army.  His  applica¬
tion  to  the  British  Government  for  land  successful,  Stiimpel  recruited
about  600  Germans  and  took  them  to  London.  Finding  himself  unable
to  keep  his  promises,  he  then  abandoned  them.  Publication  of  their
plight  by  a  German  clergyman  brought  assistance  from  the  British
government,  which  awarded  the  colonists  300  pounds,  erected  tents  for
them,  and  organized  the  collecting  of  donations  for  their  relief.  Eventu¬
ally  finding  their  way  to  South  Carolina,  where  the  provincial  govern¬
ment,  under  Governor  Boone,  voted  them  500  pounds,  they  were  con¬
ducted  to  their  land,  Londonderry  township  in  Abbeville.  Like  others
before  them,  the  settlers  had  brought  their  own  Lutheran  pastor.  His
church,  St  George’s,  became  part  of  the  incorporation  of  the  fifteen
German  churches  in  the  interior  of  South  Carolina,  in  1778.

The  first  Lutheran  church  in  South  Carolina,  St  John’s  at  Charleston,
however,  did  not  take  part  in  the  incorporation,  indicating  the  division
of  interests  between  frontier  and  sea-coast.  St  John’s  was  large  and
influential  by  itself,  and  assisted  in  the  foundation  of  the  German
Friendly  Society  in  1766.  This  was  a  charitable  organization,  praised  by
Muhlenberg  as  the  “flower  and  crown  of  the  German  nation  in  this
place”.  After  eight  years  it  had  over  eighty  members,  and  by  the
Revolution,  with  over  a  hundred,  it  was  able  to  advance  2,000  pounds
to  the  state  for  defense  against  the  Crown.

The  first  president  of  the  Friendly  Society,  a  rich  merchant  from
Charleston  named  Michael  Kalteisen,  was  also  instrumental  in  founding
Charleston’s  volunteer  company,  the  German  Fusileers.  Active  during
the  Revolution,  it  was  formed  after  the  battles  of  Lexington  and
Concord,  in  May,  1775,  with  over  a  hundred  Germans  in  its  ranks.
Kalteisen  was  its  second  lieutenant.

Unlike  South  Carolina,  North  Carolina  had  few  organized  churches
before  the  1770s,  and  consequently  few  ministers.  The  Germans  there
were  obliged  to  apply  for  them  to  the  Consistory  Council  in  Hannover,
receiving,  as  a  consequence,  Adolf  Nussman  and  a  schoolteacher  named
Gottfried  Arndt.  Nussman  was  minister  to  seven  counties  on  his  arrival
in  1773:  Rowan,  Mecklenburg,  Davidson,  Guilford,  Orange,  Stokes  and
Forsyth,  which  provides  a  good  indication  of  how  far  the  German
settlers  had  spread  by  this  time.  Expansion  also  occurred  on  the  Wachovia
tract  in  the  early  1770s,  when  the  towns  of  Friedburg,  Friedland  and
Hope  were  built,  receiving  settlers  from  Germany  and  Maryland.

28

The  years  of  the  Revolution  saw  small  numbers  of  loyalist  German
settlers  migrating  to  British  West  Florida.  Offered  the  inducements  of
free  transportation  and  land  grants  by  Governor  Chester,  the  majority
of  the  migrants  settled  in  the  Mississippi  region  of  West  Florida,  where
they  arc  said,  to  have  flourished.

Finally,  when  the  Revolution  ended,  the  settlement  of  the  West  began,
and  hundreds  of  immigrants  of  German  descent  joined  the  migration  to
Kentucky,  Tennessee,  and  the  new  west.  Among  the  explorers  who  pave
the  way  for  them  was  a  Pennsylvania  German  named  Michael  Stoner
(originally  Steiner),  who  preceded  Daniel  Boone  in  Kentu  y  an
Tennessee,  travelling  as  far  as  the  present  city  of  Nashville.  Encourage
by  Steiner  and  others  like  him,  Germans  at  the  turn  of  the  century
settled  the  West  in  favor  of  the  Southeast.

JOSEPH  JENORE’S  WILL.

In  the  name  of  God  Amen.  I,  Joseph  Jenore,  Surveyor
Generate  of  North  Carolina,  being  Sick  and  weak,  but  of  Sound
mind  and  memory,  do  make  and  ordain  this  my  Last  will  and
Testament,  desiring  my  body  may  be  decently  buryed,  And
recommending  my  Soul  humbly  to  the  mercy  of  God.

Impris.  I  will  That  all  my  Just  Debts  be  payed  as  soon  as
Conveniently  may  be,  and  after  my  funeral  Expences  &  Debts
are  payed,  I  Devise  &  bequeath  all  my  Estate,  real  &  personal
whatsoever  and  wheresoever,  to  my  Dear  wife,  Anne  Jenore,
to  her,  her  heirs  &  assigns.

And  I  do  hereby  appoint  my  sd.  wife  sole  Executrix  of  this
my  last  will  and  Testament.  My  son  being  already  provided
for  &  under  the  Care  of  his  grandfather,  I  give  him  one  Shilling
only,  not  doubting  but  due  Care  will  be  Taken  of  him.  And  I
do  hereby  revoke  all  other  wills  by  me  made.

In  testimony  whereof,  I  have  hereunto  Set  my  hand  &  Seal.
This  30th.  (30  )  day  of  Sept.,  1732.

Jo.  Jenore.
