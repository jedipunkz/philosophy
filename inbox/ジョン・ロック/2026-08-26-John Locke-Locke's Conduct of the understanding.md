PREFACE.

The editor's aim in the present volume of the Stu-
dent's Series of English Classics has been to furnish
a correct text of the Conduct of the Understanding,
with such helps as would assist* the student to a
clearer perception of the meaning of the essay. There
has been no attempt to edit the composition from a
literary standpoint, for the nature of the piece forbids
such treatment. Here the student will have no little
exercise in a sort of mental gymnastics ; his acuteness
and power of analysis must develop, if his work be
thoroughly done. Any boy or girl old enough to read
such a treatise should not need to be told that dic-
tionaries are for use, and it is no part of our purpose
to free any one from a necessary condition of all
intelligent reading.

To use the chronology of Locke's life and writings
in a sketch of his career as a statesman and philoso-
pher, did not seem practical. The bare events in his
life, therefore, have been set forth in a chronological

BIOGRAPHICAL SKETCH.

Locke was a man fitted well to his time. The tur-
moil of England's history in the seventeenth century
affected but little the eminent philosopher's calm
dignity. Charles I. was beheaded ; the Commonwealth
ruled ; royalty was restored in 1660 ; the Stuarts were
banished forever in 1688 ; and a new dynasty held the
throne. Change was the constant order, sometimes
peaceful but more often bloody. It was a day of
tracts and polemics, and of political, literary, and
religious quarrels. Theories of philosophy, education,
and morals, long revered and held sacred as the king's
divine right, were like that right denied, and were
supplanted by newer thought. Nought was stable or
in any point secure, and he that would walk amid
those toppled ruins of systems and opinions had need
of a clear eye, a firm step, and dauntless courage.

These qualities were the characteristics of John
Locke, the philosopher. His mind was lofty, resolute,
and unflinching. He was fitted by education and
character to grapple successfully with the unstable
conditions amid which he lived and wrought. He
saw beyond the confusion of the present, and led his
fellows toward the clearer light. He showed the
necessity of toleration in all matters of Church and

From this we may infer that Locke was not a
literary man in the ordinary and accepted use of that
somewhat vague term. He did not write poetry nor
drama, light essays nor fiction. His studies were
directed toward the deeper and more stolid reflections
of the philosopher. The so-called literary men who
have lived in turbulent times have seemed unable to
hold the middle course. Dryden pandered to the
vicious tendencies of a licentious court and prostituted
his power to ignoble uses. Other writers of the same
period chose a similar course. For twenty years the
magnificent poetic genius of Milton, even, was squan-
dered on a senseless war of tracts. Political bickerings
and squabbles of religious sects are ill adapted to the
poet-soul. But with Locke the case was entirely dif-

bring all discussion into the court of reason for final
judgment. His literary work in every instance,
whether religious, educational, or philosophical, had
the attainment of this condition as its first purpose.

always associated with this masterpiece.

INTRODUCTORY NOTE.

Thb Conduct of the Understanding was originally designed
by Locke as an additional chapter to the great Essay. April 10,
1697, he wrote to William Molyneux: "I have lately got a little
leisure to think of some additions to my book, against the next
edition, and within these few days have fallen upon a subject that
I know not how far it will lead me. I have written several pages
on it, but the matter, the farther I go, opens the more upon me, and
I cannot yet get sight of any end of it. The title of the chapter will
be " Of the Conduct of the Understanding," which, if I shall pursue
as far as I imagine it will reach, and as it deserves, will, I conclude,
make the largest chapter of my essay." For some reason the new
chapter did not appear in the next (fourth) edition, nor was it
published in Locke's lifetime. He never revised it, and in its
incomplete form the treatise was included in the Posthumous
Works of Mr. John Locke, edited probably by Peter King, though
published anonymously. The editor remarks generally of all the
essays contained in this volume that " for the greatest part they
received not the author's last hand, being in a great measure little
more than sudden views, intended to be afterward revised and
farther looked into, but by sickness, intervention of business, or
other preferable inquiries, happened to be thrust aside and so lay
neglected." "The Conduct of the Understanding" continues
the editor a little later, speaking particularly of the present essay,
" he always thought to be a subject well worth consideration. As
any miscarriages in that point accidentally came into his mind, he
used sometimes to set them down in writing, with those remedies
he could then think of. This method, though it makes not the haste
to the end which one could wish, is yet perhaps the only way that

123

Digitized by VjOOQlC

124 CONDUCT OF THE UNDERSTANDING.

can be followed in the case; it being here, as in physic, impossible
for a physician to describe a disease or seek remedies for it till he
comes to meet with it. Such particulars of this kind as occurred to
the author at a time of leisure he set down in writing, intending if
he had lived to have reduced them into order and method, and to
have made a complete treatise ; whereas now it is only a collection
of casual observations, sufficient to make men see some faults in the
conduct of their understanding, and suspect there may be more,
and may perhaps serve to excite others to inquire farther into it
than the author hath done."

Mr. King, if he was the editor of the posthumous edition men-
tioned, has here indicated the especial value and pointed out the
occasion of the evident faults in the essay. That it was planned as
an appendix to the Essay is clear, though the theme of the smaller
treatise is rather a method of self-education than an analysis of
knowledge or a classification of the faculties. Here we have an
attempt to set rules for directing the mind in its quest for knowl-
edge. It is composed of wholly practical warnings against prevalent
faults and of equally practical bints for correcting them. We are
cautioned over and again not to admit prejudice, so often perhaps
that the iteration becomes occasionally tiresome. Even in that
event the repetition is not necessarily a philosophical defect, though
it may be a literary blemish. As a whole, the treatise is devoid
of any literary unity, unless it may consist in the author's ever
evident purpose of assisting the understanding to a right conduct
of its own affairs; it is irregular and incomplete; sentences are
begun that never end, and there are some ends that have no ap-
parent beginnings. The meaning, however, is usually clear, and
the imperfect sentences are so easily remodelled that often this task
has been left for the student to perform. A few sections might
have been omitted without any serious loss to the reader, but the
whole is terse and offers ample reward for the student's careful
study. It is not an exercise in style, nor does it offer passages of
imaginative beauty or eloquent prose ; it is distinctly a contribution
to the literature of philosophy, and must be approached from this
point of view, if the student would avoid futile effort and wasted
time.

almost any book of logic. See especially Venn's Logic of Chance.
