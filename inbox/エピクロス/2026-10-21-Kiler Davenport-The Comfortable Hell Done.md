Introduction

A few months ago a stick-figure animation started bouncing
around the internet. No budget, no studio, no brand deal —
just crude little line people on a screen, narrated in a flat
voice, laying out an idea that refused to sit quietly. Millions
of views. The kind of thing that shows up in your group chat
at midnight with a message like "this broke my brain" and
you watch it with the sound low so you don't wake anyone,
and by the end you're staring at the ceiling.

Here's the idea, compressed: we are living in the most

comfortable era in human history, and we are also the most
depressed, addicted, lonely, and medicated generation that
has ever existed. The video's punchline lands like a slap: this
isn't a coincidence. It's a business model.

I don't know who you are, but I know the feeling. You've

felt it at 2 a.m. with the phone glowing in your face. You've
felt it in the grocery cart full of things nobody on earth is
hungry for. You've felt it scrolling past a thousand faces
you'll
never
meet,
learning
things
about
strangers'

breakfasts you will carry to the grave for no reason. The
comfort is real. The emptiness is also real. And the sentence
that bothers you — the one you can't quite file away — is
that maybe the two facts aren't just sitting next to each
other. Maybe one of them is feeding on the other.

This book is my attempt to check whether that sentence is

true. Not with vibes. With the receipts.

So here's what we're going to do. We're going to start with

the paradox itself: the uncomfortable numbers, the strange
graphs, the fact that a medieval king lived worse than you
do and yet we are the ones on the prescriptions. Then we're
going to take the machinery apart, piece by piece. The food
that was engineered in a lab to be impossible to stop eating.
The feeds that were engineered by some of the smartest

video says "business model" and a business model leaves a
ledger. And at the end of every trail, the same question
keeps showing up: if all of this is just how the world is now,
why does it feel so bad?

But — and this is the part that matters most — we're not

stopping at the machinery. Because the machinery can tell
you what is happening to you. It cannot tell you what it
means, and it cannot tell you what to do about it. For that,
we need older tools. We need the philosophers.

Here's the thing nobody told me when I first watched that

reel: the thesis isn't new. It's twenty-three centuries old.
Aristotle spent his whole career arguing that a life of
comfort isn't a life of flourishing — that pleasure is the
wrong target and everyone aiming at it misses. The Stoics
drew a hard line between what the world does to you and
what you do with what the world does to you, and insisted
that the second one is the only freedom there is. Epicurus —
the most misunderstood philosopher in history, whose name
got turned into a word for food snobs — built his entire
philosophy around interrogating desire itself: which wants
are real, which are manufactured, and who's getting paid
when you obey the fake ones.

These people lived without electricity. They are,

Let's start with a man who had everything.

England, sometime in the 1300s. January. The most

powerful man in the country wakes up in the dark, because
the dark is what January mornings are made of, and no
amount of power changes that. His bedroom is a stone room
in a stone castle. There is a fire in the hearth, or the remains
of one. It has done its best. Its best was not enough.

He is cold. Not metaphorically cold, not "I forgot my

jacket" cold. Cold in his bones, cold in the mattress, cold in
the air he breathes out in little white puffs while his
servants hurry in with his clothes. He is the king of England
and he needs help getting dressed, because his clothes are
complicated and the room is freezing and his fingers don't
work properly yet. His fingers don't work properly because
he is, by our standards, old — forty-five, maybe — and his
joints ache in a way that will never get better, because
nothing gets better. There is no getting better. There is
getting worse more slowly, if you're lucky.

The mattress is straw. Not a metaphor, not a folksy detail

— straw, in a cloth sack, that has been slept on since autumn
and smells like it. There are lice. There are always lice. The
king has lice. Everyone has lice. The lice do not respect the

Here's where the 700-million neighborhood actually lives:

that's roughly the territory of all mental disorders

earth — on the order of a billion people — lives with some
mental disorder. Depression is about 280 million of that.
Anxiety is about 301 million. The rest is everything else. So
the reel took the number for "everyone struggling with
anything" and hung it on depression alone. Corrected, the
claim still hits — a billion people is not a small thing — but
precision is the whole game here. If we're going to argue
that the comfortable era is making us sick, we don't get to
inflate the patient count. The truth is bad enough. It doesn't
need our help.

Before we go further, a necessary detour: how do you

even count sadness? Because every number in this chapter
rests on an act of measurement, and the measurement is
weirder than it looks.

Nobody draws blood for depression. There's no scan, no

swab. What we have are surveys — researchers asking
large, carefully chosen groups of people the same questions,
year after year: how often have you felt hopeless, how's
your sleep, have you lost interest in things you used to
enjoy. The answers get scored, the scores get aggregated,
and out comes "prevalence." It's self-report all the way
down. People describing their own inner weather to a
stranger with a clipboard, or more likely now, to a web form
at midnight.

This is obviously imperfect. People lie, people

misremember, people interpret "often" differently. A
teenager in 2024 has a vocabulary for her feelings that a

difference matters, because the machine you're up against
doesn't deal in tips either. It deals in habits — yours — and
habits are only dislodged by deeper ones.

The first discipline is the pause. The Stoics noticed
