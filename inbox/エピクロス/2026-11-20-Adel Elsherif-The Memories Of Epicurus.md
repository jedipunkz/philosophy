Introduction: In The Memories of Epicurus

In this short book, I’ve tried to do something simple yet profound: to remove the
misconceptions that have long clouded the philosophy of Epicurus.

Too often, he’s been caricatured as a preacher of indulgence, a lover of luxury, a hedonist
without restraint. But the truth is far more beautiful, and far more useful.

These are the pillars of a tranquil life. We’ve explored his theory of knowledge, rooted in the
senses, and contrasted his ideas with those of other schools—Stoics, Cynics, Cyrenaics,
and Platonists—to reveal his unique clarity and compassion.

We’ve walked through the Garden, not just as a place, but as a living ideal. It was a school
open to all: rich and poor, free and enslaved, men and women alike.

A sanctuary of reason, friendship, and peace.

This book is more than a guide—it’s a memoir of Epicurus. Through fictionalized scenes
and dialogues, I’ve tried to make him speak again.

Not as a distant figure in dusty texts, but as a living voice. These stories are not fantasy;
they are rooted in his teachings, his spirit, and his humanity.

We want you to feel Epicurus—not just read about him. To sit with him under the fig tree, to
hear his laughter, to watch him offer a simple meal and a profound truth.

To immerse yourself in his memories, and through them, find your own path to happiness.

May this book help you attain tranquility, whatever your condition. May it guide you gently
toward the life Epicurus envisioned: simple, fearless, and free.

In addition to philosophical reflection, this book offers practical advice on applying
Epicureanism in everyday life, and explores the biology of happiness.

Note: While this memoir is a work of fiction, it is deeply inspired by the life, sayings, and
ideas of Epicurus. Every scene and insight is grounded in his philosophy and legacy.

The Language of Tranquility, Epicurean Terms Explained

To truly grasp the philosophy of Epicurus, one must first learn the language he spoke, not
justin words, but in ideas.

These terms are not mere jargon; they are keys to a worldview built on peace, pleasure,
and freedom from fear.

Each concept opens a door to a life lived deliberately, simply, and joyfully.

Below are some of the most essential terms in Epicurean thought—each one a stepping
stone toward serenity.

Ataraxia (Atapaéia)

A state of serene calm and freedom from mental disturbance. For Epicurus, this was one
of the highest pleasures: a mind untroubled by fear, anxiety, or irrational desire.

Aponia (dmtovia)

The absence of physical pain. Alongside ataraxia, aponia completes the Epicurean vision
of happiness—not through indulgence, but through the quiet joy of being free from
suffering.

Pleasure (ndovin / hédoné)

Epicurus called pleasure the beginning and end of a blessed life. But he distinguished
between kinetic pleasures (active sensations) and katastematic pleasures (stable states
like ataraxia and aponia). The latter were deeper and more enduring.

Tetrapharmakos (tetpadappakoc)

The “Fourfold Remedy,” a philosophical prescription for peace of mind:
1. Don’t fear God

2. Don’t worry about death

3. What is good is easy to get

4. What is terrible is easy to endure

Physis (@Uotc)

Nature. Epicurus believed that understanding nature—especially through atomism—was
essential to freeing ourselves from superstition and fear.

Epibolé (€rBoAn)

Mental focus or directed attention. Epicurus used this term to describe how we engage
with sensations and ideas, forming judgments and beliefs through conscious reflection.

Prolepsis (mpoAnWitec)

Preconception or innate idea. These are basic mental templates formed from repeated
experiences, which help us recognize and understand new things, like our intuitive sense
of justice or friendship.

Dikaiosyné (Suwatoovvn)

Justice. Epicurus saw justice as a mutual agreement not to harm or be harmed. It’s not
absolute, but contextual, what’s just is what fosters peace and mutual benefit.

Desire (m8 uUpia / epithymia)

Epicurus divided desires into three types:

- Natural and necessary (e.g., food, shelter)

- Natural but unnecessary (e.g., luxury foods)

- Neither natural nor necessary (e.g., fame, power)

Epicurus rebelled against nearly every dominant tradition of his time—he rejected the
authority of the gods, criticized popular philosophies like Platonism, and boldly challenged
the intellectual elite. His radical ideas earned him many enemies among fellow
philosophers.

Epicurus was born to Athenian parents on the island of Samos in February 341 B.C.—a
date known with uncommon precision for ancient figures.

His father, Neocles, was an Athenian schoolteacher, a profession lightly regarded in their
society.

His mother, Chaerestrate, was known for performing rites of purification in the homes of
the poor. Epicurus sometimes assisted her by reading charms, a childhood experience
that may have contributed to his lifelong hatred of superstition.

Epicurus had many enemies during his lifetime, which led later scholars to believe the
slanders spread by his foes. In truth, he was a remarkably simple man.

His hedonism was not about excessive eating or drinking—it was about enjoying small
pleasures and cultivating peace of mind.

When he was dying and gravely ill, he requested only a piece of cheese and a little bread.

Philosophical Influences on Epicurus

Epicurus's philosophy was a complex synthesis, drawing positive inspiration from some
thinkers while defining itself in opposition to others.

He is considered to have revived the tradition of lonian thinkers, which had been
interrupted by Socrates and Plato, with lonian thought serving as his chief positive
influence and Platonism as his chief negative influence.

Democritus and Leucippus (Atomists): Epicurus espoused and revived the atomic theory,
inheriting and elaborating the ideas of invisible and indivisible bodies from these lonian
thinkers.

He also derived aspects of his psychology (theory of Images), cosmogony, and theology
from Democritus.

Despite some later criticisms, Epicurus generally spoke of Democritus with respect.

Plato: Epicurus's relationship with Plato was primarily one of opposition and critique.

Although he studied with the Platonist Pamphilus in Samos for about four years during his
youth—a period likely arranged by his parents due to Platonism's orthodox status—
Epicurus later became a strong anti-Platonist.

He rejected Plato's hypostatized Reason as the norm of truth and opposed Plato's rigidly
regimented political systems.

His own classification of desires originated from a Platonic hint. More than half of his forty
Authorized Doctrines directly contradict Platonic teachings.

His concept of Prolepsis (Anticipation) directly countered Plato's anamnesis
(recollection).

Aristotle: Aristotle was a significant, though often unacknowledged, influence,
particularly in epistemology and biology.

Aristotle's shift of focus from inorganic to organic nature, revealing a “terrestrial order” of
nature, was tacitly recognized by Epicurus, who subsequently emphasized nature as the
norm of truth.

Epicurus's theory of innate ideas (Anticipations) might have been influenced by Aristotle's
study of the embryo.

His theories on pleasure and justice also drew from observations of animal behavior, an
area of Aristotle's interest.

Formation of the Garden School

Epicurus established his school in Athens in 306 B.C., conducting his teaching within a
house and garden that he owned.

This property, purchased for eighty minae—a sum less than what a sophist might charge
for a single course—was often referred to as a “little garden” (hortulus), suggesting its
modest size.

The house stood within the city walls in the Melite district, while the garden lay just outside
the old Dipylon Gate, along the same road that led to Plato’s Academy.

Epicurus’s decision to create this private space for instruction was a deliberate and
strategic choice, allowing him to avoid persecution and the interference of public
authorities.

The Garden valued leadership, reverence for superiors, and the fostering of love and
friendship among its members. Epicurus, as its head, took the title of hegemon, meaning
“leader” or “guide.”

He welcomed pupils of all ages, including women and children—a notable departure from
the typical focus on adolescent males in other schools of the time.

He also rejected the term “schoolteacher,” which he associated with a coercive style of
instruction.

10

Instead, he preferred titles that reflected guidance and persuasion, in harmony with his
conviction that “human nature is not to be coerced but persuaded.”

The Garden of Epicurus

The Garden: Community, Practices, and Daily Life

Epicurus’s school, commonly known as The Garden, was a unique philosophical
community established in Athens around 307-306 B.C.

It was not merely a place of instruction but functioned as a structured society with distinct
social, economic, and philosophical practices.

Its influence extended far beyond its own time, shaping later philosophical traditions and
even leaving an imprint on early Christian communal life.

I. Community Structure and Membership

The Garden was organized around principles of leadership, reverence for superiors, love,
and fellowship, resembling a Hellenic household or even a “secular church.”

Epicurus, known as the Master or hegemon (leader/guide), presided over a diverse
membership: resident adults, adolescent students, and women such as Mammarion,
Hedeia, and Erotion.

Notably, slaves were integral members of the community.

They lived within its premises and contributed to its operations, especially in manuscript
copying and publishing.

Prominent disciples like Metrodorus, Polyaenus, and Hermarchus served as
Kathegemones (“Guides”), assisting in teaching and governance.

Epicurus’s vision for The Garden was democratic in spirit
It was opened to anyone willing to listen, rather than an esoteric sect restricted to the elite.

Yet it also cultivated a deep sense of unanimity and mutual care, functioning as a “family
of brothers” with Epicurus as the symbolic “father.”

Central to this ethos was friendship (oikeiotés, meaning “membership in the family”),
which Epicurus called the most precious acquisition for a blessed life.

11

Members were encouraged to practice respect, forgiveness, and love for one another,
directly challenging the competitive and status-driven norms of the outside world.

ll. Practices and Daily Life

Frugality and Self-Sufficiency

Life in The Garden was marked by simplicity. Happiness was sought not in abundance but
in freedom from pain and moderation of desires.

Epicurus famously remarked that they were content with “half a pint of wine” and “for the
most part drank water.”

Unlike the Pythagoreans, he discouraged pooling all possessions into a common stock,
seeing it as a sign of distrust.

The community was resourceful—during the siege of Athens, Epicurus kept members alive
by literally “counting out the beans.”

They believed that life’s greatest ills arose from internal passions and ignorance, and that
virtues such as temperance and modesty could overcome poverty, slander, and
misfortune.

Education and Philosophy

The Garden offered an integrated curriculum of:
- Canon (method of knowledge)

- Physics (study of nature)

- Ethics (principles of choice and avoidance)

This program rivaled the Platonic Academy.

Epicurus emphasized persuasion over coercion in teaching. Instructors were “leaders” or
“guides,” not authoritarian “schoolmasters.”

12

Elementary pupils, called (“in course of preparation”), were trained in moral discipline, a
term later echoed in the Christian catechumens. Classes were held in various corners of
the Garden from early morning until late in the day.

The ultimate aim was ataraxia (tranquility) and aponia (freedom from pain), achieved
through understanding the nature of the gods, the inevitability of death, and the value of
friendship. Epicurus rejected

Plato’s reliance on abstract Reason, instead grounding truth in direct experience—
sensations, anticipations, and feelings.

Social and Ritual Gatherings

Regular philosophical symposiums—often called “love-feasts” (agapai)—were held,
reminiscent of early Christian communal meals. These took place on the twentieth of each
month, a day with quasi-sabbath sanctity in the Greek calendar ().

On such occasions, the usual austerity was relaxed, with fine wine and quality food served.
Epicurus also instituted ritual offerings at the tombs of deceased members and
encouraged the writing of pious memoirs, granting a form of immortality through
remembrance.

Daily Schedule

While no fixed timetable survives, similar pre-Christian communities such as the Essenes
divided the day into three equal parts: labor, recreation/instruction, and sleep. Meals were
taken at a common table, with designated cooks, buyers, and servers. The Garden likely
followed a comparable rhythm, emphasizing mutual support and self-sufficiency over
charity.

Recreation and Arts

All “innocent recreations” were permitted, including music, though licentious poetry was
discouraged. Epicurus valued intellectual pursuits but urged that they serve the study of
virtue and human nature. For example, Polyaenus, a skilled geometer, was encouraged to
devote more time to moral philosophy.

13

Physical Setting

The school comprised Epicurus’s private house in the respectable district of Melite and a
modest but beautiful garden outside the Dipylon Gate, on the road to the Academy.

The house doubled as a publishing center, where slaves copied manuscripts. Communal
spaces included a vaulted, circular hall in a temple-like building, adorned with paintings
and a statue of Venus Urania, as well as a library where Epicurus and Metrodorus studied
and transcribed texts. Shaded paths and quiet corners provided an ideal setting for
contemplation and discussion.

Ill. Core Philosophical Attitudes

Epicurus promoted a democratic philosophy aimed at bringing happiness to all.
His Tetrapharmakon (“Fourfold Remedy”) taught:

1. Do not fear the gods.

2. Do not fear death.

3. What is good is easy to obtain.

4. What is terrible is easy to endure.

Epicurus also advised living “unknown” (lathe bidsas)—avoiding public ambition and
political entanglements—to preserve tranquility. Ethical conduct, self-knowledge, and the
cultivation of inner peace were the cornerstones of his teaching.

More about the school:
Inclusivity:

The Garden’s acceptance of women and even slaves was radical for its time, challenging
prevailing Athenian social hierarchies.

Contrast with Other Schools:

Unlike the Stoics, who embraced public duty, Epicureans sought withdrawal from political
life, focusing on personal and communal well-being.

14

Legacy: The Garden’s emphasis on friendship, shared meals, and ritual remembrance
foreshadowed aspects of monastic and early Christian communities.

Main Difference Between Plato and Epicurus

Plato and Epicurus offered two sharply different visions of philosophy’s purpose and
method.

- Plato placed Reason above all, holding that truth resides in eternal, incorporeal Forms.

The senses, to him, were unreliable; only dialectic could lead the soul upward toward the
Form of the Good.

Virtue was pursued for its own sake, and just life was inseparable from one’s role as a
citizen in a well-ordered state. His ideal society was a highly structured republic, governed
by philosopher-kings, with the harmony of the city reflecting the harmony of the soul.

- Epicurus grounded truth in Nature, tested by the evidence of the senses, anticipations,
and feelings.

He favored minimal government, withdrawal from politics, and life ina small, equal
community bound by friendship, where women, slaves, and foreigners could be
philosophical equals.

In essence: Plato sought the perfection of the soul through the perfection of the state;
Epicurus sought the peace of the soul through freedom from the state.

While most of Plato’s works have survived, the vast majority of Epicurus’s writings are lost.

Ancient historians claimed he wrote more than Plato, yet today we possess only fragments
and letters, a silence in the record that leaves his living voice largely to our imagination.

Canon of Truth : Epistemology

Introduction: A Philosophy Rooted in Nature

Epicurus, the ancient Greek philosopher of the Garden, built his epistemology not on
abstract reasoning or metaphysical speculation, but on the direct, unfiltered data of
nature.

His "Canon"—the criterion of truth—was a radical departure from Platonic idealism and
even from Aristotelian logic, which he respected but ultimately sidelined in his search for
certainty.

For Epicurus, truth begins and ends with the senses. Everything else—logic, interpretation,
belief—is secondary and prone to error.

The Infallibility of the Senses

Epicurus held that the senses never lie. They are the body's direct interface with reality,
and their reports are always true in the context in which they occur.

Example: The Tower Illusion

- From a distance, a square tower may appear round.

- The sense-impression ("roundness") is not false—it reflects the conditions of perception.
- The error arises when the mind judges, "The tower is round," ignoring the context.

This distinction between aisthésis (sensation) and doxa (judgment) is central.

Sensation is raw and reliable; judgment is interpretive and fallible.

Logic: Useful but Not Foundational

Epicurus did not reject logic outright. He used it instrumentally—for teaching, clarifying,
and defending his views—but he did not see it as a source of truth.

19

Example: The Sorites Paradox ("What is a heap?")

- Logical puzzles like this may entertain or challenge the intellect.

- But they do not contribute to ataraxia (peace of mind) or eudaimonia (happiness).
- Epicurus would say: “Knowing what a heap is won’t help you sleep better at night.”

Instead of chasing abstract definitions, Epicurus urged us to focus on what can be directly
observed and felt.

Atoms and the Limits of Sensory Proof

Interestingly, Epicurus did propose theories—like atomic physics—that could not be
directly verified by the senses. But he justified these through analogy and inference from
observable phenomena.

Example: Atoms

- We cannot see atoms, but we observe their effects (e.g., motion, change, decay).
- Epicurus argued that the atomic theory best explains these sensory facts.

- Thus, even speculative reasoning must be grounded in empirical data.

This shows that while Epicurus avoided metaphysics, he allowed reason to extend sensory
knowledge, so long as it remained tethered to it.

Pleasure as the Evident Good

Epicurus famously claimed that pleasure is the highest good, and he refused to argue for it.
Why?

Because, he said, it is self-evident. Every living being seeks pleasure and avoids pain.

To demand a proof for this is to ignore the most basic data of experience.

Example: A Child and Honey

Achild reaches for honey instinctively.

No one teaches the child that sweetness is good, it is felt directly.
Epicurus saw this as proof that pleasure is a natural guide.

Those who deny this need not be argued with; they simply need to observe themselves
more honestly.

20

Epistemology as Ethical Armor

Epicurus’s epistemology wasn’t just theoretical, it was therapeutic.

By trusting the senses and rejecting superstition, we free ourselves from fear and anxiety.
Example: Fear of the Gods

Superstitious beliefs about divine punishment cause immense suffering.

Epicurus taught that the gods, if they exist, are indifferent to human affairs.

This conclusion is based on the lack of sensory evidence for divine intervention.

Thus, epistemology becomes a shield against irrational fears.

Philosophy of Clarity and Calm

Epicurus’s Canon is a call to return to the body, to nature, to what is directly given. Ina
world of endless interpretations and abstract systems, he offers a simple but profound
alternative: trust your senses, seek pleasure, and live free from fear.

His epistemology is not just a theory of knowledge, it’s a guide to happiness.

Difference between the epistemology of Stoic and Epicurean philosophy.
Epicurus believed that truth begins with the senses.

For him, the senses are infallible, they report reality as it appears, without distortion. If a
tower looks round from a distance, that’s not a mistake of the senses; it’s a faithful report
of how the tower appears under those conditions.

The error arises when the mind judges, “The tower is round,” without further investigation.
Epicurean epistemology rests on this principle: sensory data is pure, and mistakes come
from interpretation.

Therefore, the path to truth is to trust the senses and avoid abstract reasoning that leads to
superstition or fear.

In contrast, the Stoics placed reason at the center of their epistemology.

They acknowledged that the senses provide impressions, but these impressions are not
automatically trustworthy. Instead, the mind must evaluate them through reason.

23

The Stoics introduced the concept of impressions, those that are so clear and distinct that
they compel rational assent.

Only these impressions can be considered true. For example, if you feel pain in your leg,
the impression “lam in pain” might be true, but the judgment “This pain is unbearable and
life is terrible” must be examined and possibly rejected by reason.

Aclassic Stoic example involves emotional response. Suppose someone insults you.
The initial impression might be “I’ve been wronged,” followed by anger.

But the Stoic would pause and ask: is this impression in accordance with nature and
reason? Is the insult truly harmful, or is it merely a disturbance of ego?

By applying rational scrutiny, the Stoic discards the false judgment and maintains
equanimity.

Epicurus would say that such emotional turmoil can be avoided by understanding the
natural limits of pleasure and pain. If you know that death is nothing to fear, and that pain
is either brief or manageable, you won’t be disturbed. But he wouldn’t rely on logical
argument to prove this, he’d point to the direct experience of pleasure and the absence of
pain as self-evident guides.

The Stoics, however, believed that living in accordance with nature meant living rationally.

Their epistemology was deeply tied to their ethics: to be virtuous, one must know what is
truly good, and that requires a rigorous process of evaluating impressions.

They saw logic not just as a tool, but as a necessary discipline for achieving wisdom.,

So while Epicurus sought peace of mind through sensory clarity and minimal reasoning,
the Stoics pursued moral clarity through disciplined rationality.

Both aimed at tranquility, but they took very different roads to get there.
Epicureanism: A Proto-Scientific Approach
Epicurus built his philosophy on empirical observation and naturalistic explanations.

He adopted atomism from Democritus, proposing that everything in the universe—
including the soul—is made of tiny, indivisible particles (atoms) moving through the void.
This was a materialist view of the world, rejecting supernatural causes and divine
intervention.

Empirical foundation: Epicurus believed that knowledge comes from the senses, which
provide direct and reliable data. He emphasized observation over speculation.

24

Rejection of metaphysics:
He dismissed abstract reasoning that couldn’t be grounded in sensory evidence.
Natural explanations:

He explained phenomena like thunder or earthquakes through physical causes, not divine
wrath.

Fear reduction:

His goal was to eliminate fear, especially fear of gods and death, by understanding nature
rationally.

In many ways, Epicureanism anticipated the scientific method: observe, hypothesize, and
explain phenomena based on evidence.

Stoicism: Rational but Metaphysical

Stoicism, while deeply rational, is more metaphysical in its foundations. The Stoics
believed the universe was governed by divine reason (Logos)—a kind of cosmic
intelligence.

They saw nature as purposeful and ordered, and human reason as a reflection of this
divine structure.

Reason as the path to truth:

Stoics emphasized logical analysis and cognitive control.

Metaphysical assumptions:

They believed in fate, providence, and a rational cosmos.

Ethical focus:

Their epistemology served moral development more than scientific inquiry.

While Stoicism influenced modern psychology (especially cognitive behavioral therapy), its
cosmology and epistemology are less empirical than Epicureanism.

Verdict: Epicureanism Is More Scientific
Epicurus was closer to a natural scientist than a metaphysical.

His commitment to sense data, atomism, and natural causes makes his philosophy more
compatible with modern scientific thinking. Stoicism, though brilliant in its ethical and
psychological insights, leans more toward rational idealism than empirical science.,

Epicurus’s Conception of the Gods

29

Existence and Knowledge

Epicurus maintained that the existence of gods is certain because the knowledge of them
is a “universal idea” or “pre-notion” (prolepsis) common to all humanity, implanted by
Nature itself.

This knowledge is not derived from physical senses, but directly by the mind through
“mental images” or “apprehensions” (/ epibolé tés dianoias), often experienced in
dreams.

Nature and Attributes
The gods, according to Epicurus, are “blessed and incorruptible.”

He specifically used the term “incorruptible” rather than “immortal,” implying that while
they are theoretically subject to dissolution, they avoid it in practice. These divine beings
possess perfect happiness and are free from all trouble, care, and anxiety.

Form and Abode

Epicurus described the gods as corporeal beings with human (anthropomorphic) form—
though larger, more beautiful, and ethereal in nature. Their dwelling place isin the
intermundia—the spaces between worlds—where they remain untouched by the dangers
and concerns of any single world.

Inaction and Non-Interference

Crucially, Epicurus taught that the gods do not interfere in human affairs or govern the
world. They neither cause trouble nor offer favor, feeling neither anger nor affection. Such
emotions, he argued, imply weakness and are inconsistent with their serene nature.

Piety and Worship

30

Epicurus allowed and even practiced public worship and religious ceremonies—not out of
fear or hope for divine intervention, but as a form of reverence for their excellence and as a
means of achieving ataraxia (tranquility). He emphasized that the proper attitude toward
the gods is eusebeia (reverence), not fear.

Why the Fear of Gods Is Not Justified

Epicurus argued against the fear of gods for several fundamental reasons:

1. Inconsistency with Divine Nature

He asserted that “fear, anger, and favor are incompatible with the perfect blessedness and
incorruptibility of the gods.”

To attribute such emotions or worldly control to them would imply toil and weakness—
alien to their serene existence.

2. Origin of Fear in Ignorance

Fear of the gods arises primarily from human ignorance of natural causes. Celestial events
like thunder, lightning, comets, and eclipses—or terrestrial disasters like floods and failed
harvests—were mistakenly attributed to divine wrath. Epicurus believed that scientific
understanding of these phenomena dispels this superstitious dread.

3. Detrimental to Human Happiness

Fear of the gods is one of the chief causes of mental disturbance (taraché), preventing
individuals from achieving ataraxia (peace of mind) and aponia (freedom from pain)—the
ultimate goals of Epicurean philosophy. Such fear leads to anxiety, superstition, and a life
of constant appeasement that obstructs true happiness.

4. Emphasis on Human Agency and Free Will

31

Epicurus stressed that human actions are largely within our control and subject to praise
or blame—not predetermined by divine will or fate. Belief in divine interference
undermines free will and leads to a “slavery to necessity.”

In this fictional story, Epicurus wanted the man to understand that the gods require no
material offerings—only respect. Perhaps the man realized his sons needed those gifts
more than the temple did.

In reality, Epicurus did participate in religious offerings, even though it contradicted his
views on divine indifference. He did so for pragmatic reasons.

Epicurus held a nuanced stance on religious practices:

1. Public Participation: Despite his beliefs, Epicurus advised his followers to take part in
public rituals “reverently and properly.” This was a strategic move to avoid accusations of
impiety, especially in a society where philosophers like Socrates had been persecuted.

2. Reinterpreting Rituals: Epicurus didn’t see rituals as divine transactions. Instead, he
believed they could help the mind receive “simulacra” of the gods—mental images that
inspired tranquility. Prayer, for Epicureans, was a form of communion, not a request for
intervention. He revered the gods for their excellence, not their power.

3. Community Offerings: Within his school, Epicurus organized voluntary contributions to
sustain the Garden. His will included provisions for offerings in honor of deceased loved
ones. Monthly gatherings and birthday celebrations featured symbolic “offerings” and
philosophical banquets, fostering fellowship and reflection.

4. Limits of Prayer: Epicurus taught that it was “idle to seek from the gods what a man is
capable of providing for himself.” While one might reflect on life’s duration, true peace of
mind must be earned through reason and self-awareness.

Atomic Theory in Epicurean Philosophy

For Epicurus, atomic theory was not merely an academic exercise, it was the foundation
for cultivating the right mindset and achieving the ethical goal of a tranquil and happy life.
Understanding atoms was essential to living “the good life,” because it addressed the root
causes of human fear, confusion, and suffering.

Fundamental Components of Reality

Epicurus taught that the universe consists of two things: solid bodies and void (empty
space).

- The solid bodies are atoms—indivisible, eternal, and indestructible particles.

- He argued that if matter could be infinitely divided, it would eventually be annihilated.
Therefore, atoms must exist as the ultimate building blocks of reality.

The word atom itself means “indivisible.” These particles are described as:
- “Hard bodies free from any admixture of void”

- “Solid, massy, hard, impenetrable particles”

Their solid singleness gives them strength, allowing them to form countless combinations
without being worn away.

Primary Properties of Atoms

39

Atoms possess only three intrinsic properties:

- Shape: Atoms come in a vast variety of shapes—smooth, hooked, angular—which
account for the diversity of perceptible things. While the number of shapes is immense, it
is not infinite. However, for each shape, there is an infinite number of atoms.

- Size: Atoms vary in size but are never infinitely small. They are far below the threshold of
human perception, yet not so large as to be visible.

- Weight: All atoms have weight, which causes their primary motion.

Importantly, atoms do not possess secondary qualities like color, sound, smell, warmth,
or consciousness. These emerge only when atoms combine to form larger, perceptible
bodies.

Epicurus viewed atoms as “dead matter,” and the challenge was to explain how sensation
and consciousness arise from these non-sentient particles.

Constant Motion and the Swerve

Atoms are always in motion:

- In the void, they fall straight downward due to their weight, moving at equal and incredibly
fast soeeds—“quick as thought.”

- Epicurus rejected Aristotle’s view that heavier bodies fall faster; in a vacuum, all atoms
fall at the same rate.

When atoms form compound bodies, they continue their internal vibration.
A unique Epicurean concept is the swerve (clinamen):

- Atoms can spontaneously and slightly deviate from their path at uncertain times and
places.

- Without this swerve, atoms falling in parallel lines would never collide—thus, nothing
could ever be formed.

This swerve is crucial for explaining the formation of matter and for introducing free will
into a universe otherwise governed by deterministic laws.

Understanding the Universe and Human Life

40

Epicurus used atomic theory to explain both the natural world and the human condition:

- Formation of Worlds: The random motion and collisions of atoms, aided by the swerve,
lead to the formation of compound bodies and infinite worlds—some like ours, some
entirely different.

- Dispelling Fear of the Gods: Epicurus taught that if gods exist, they are made of fine
atoms and reside in the intermundia (spaces between worlds).

They do not interfere in human affairs.
This understanding removes fear of divine punishment or intervention.

- Dispelling Fear of Death: The soul, too, is made of fine atoms dispersed throughout the
body. At death, these atoms scatter, and sensation ceases. ”

- Freedom of Will: The atomic swerve breaks the chain of strict causation, allowing for
human freedom and moral responsibility.

Learning About Atoms: A Moral Imperative

Epicurus developed a systematic teaching method:

- He began with epitomes (condensed summaries), such as the Letter to Herodotus, which
laid out the most general principles.

- His pedagogy moved from general to particular, using the Twelve Elementary Principles of
physics. Chief among them:

“The universe consists of solid bodies and void.”

For Epicurus, atomic theory was not just scientific—it was ethical and liberating. It offered
a rational explanation of the world, freed the mind from superstition, and laid the
groundwork for ataraxia, peace of mind.

Atoms, the Body, and the Birth of Awareness

The idea of atoms is no longer mere philosophical speculation—it has become a
cornerstone of modern science. From quantum mechanics to neuroscience, the material
nature of the universe is affirmed time and again.

Epicurus’s insight that everything, including sensation and thought, is composed of
physical particles anticipated discoveries that would emerge centuries later.

Today, neuroscience confirms that consciousness arises from the brain, a corporeal
structure composed of billions of neurons and trillions of synaptic connections.
Awareness is not a disembodied form, it is the product of dynamic interactions between
physical systems. Studies show that regions like the prefrontal cortex, thalamus, and
reticular activating system are essential for generating alertness, perception, and self-
awareness.

Without a functioning body, particularly a functioning brain, awareness ceases. This
supports Epicurus’s view that the soul is material, and that sensation ends when its atomic
structure dissolves.

Although modern physics no longer accepts the classical notion of void—replacing it with
quantum fields and the fabric of spacetime—it continues to affirm that matter behaves
according to physical laws, not divine intention.

Epicurus’s atoms may differ from the particles described in contemporary physics, but his
core idea remains strikingly relevant: the universe is neither moral nor purposeful—it is
neutral.

And by understanding its nature, we free ourselves from superstition, fear, and the illusion
of cosmic judgment.

Epicurus on Pleasure
A Philosophy of Tranquility, Not Indulgence

43

There is widespread misunderstanding about Epicurus’s conception of pleasure.

Many imagine his Garden as a place of excess, filled with sex, wine, expensive food, and
orgies. In reality, Epicurus’s philosophy was far more restrained and thoughtful.

His idea of pleasure can be best understood through three dimensions: negative, simple,
and active, all aimed at cultivating a tranquil and happy life, not unbridled indulgence.

1. Negative Pleasure: Freedom from Pain and Trouble

Epicurus believed that once this condition is achieved, the “tempest of the soulis
dispersed,” and the individual feels no further need to perfect the soul or body.

This view contrasts sharply with the Cyrenaic school, which valued only kinetic pleasures,
those involving motion or excitement—and dismissed the absence of pain as neutral.

Epicurus, however, saw static pleasures, freedom from pain and mental disturbance—as
the foundation of lasting happiness.

He acknowledged active pleasures like joy and exultation, but emphasized that they must
arise from a stable, undisturbed state.

Central to achieving this negative pleasure is the removal of fear, especially fear of the
gods and fear of death.

Epicurus taught that death is “nothing to us,” because it is the deprivation of sensation,
and what lacks sensation cannot harm us. Understanding this eliminates the craving for
immortality and allows us to enjoy mortal life.

Similarly, a proper understanding of the gods reveals them as blessed and incorruptible
beings who do not interfere in human affairs, dispelling fear of divine punishment or favor.

2. Simple Pleasure: Contentment with Natural Needs

Epicurus advocated a simple lifestyle, believing it sufficient for true happiness. He lived
almost ascetically, content with bread and water, and encouraged his disciples to satisfy
only natural and necessary desires: health, food, drink, clothing, and shelter.

These are easy to obtain and bring no harm.

44

He explicitly distinguished these from luxurious or sensual pleasures, which he argued
lead to disease, anxiety, and regret. As he wrote:

“Plain-tasting foods bring a pleasure equal to that of a luxurious diet once the pain of need
has been removed.”

The gratification of genuine hunger and thirst provides maximum pleasure, which cannot
be surpassed by artificial stimulation.

This reflects the idea of natural ceilings of pleasure, beyond which pleasure cannot be
increased, only varied.

Epicurus advised:

“If you wish to make Pythocles happy, add not to his riches, but take away from his
desires.”

For him, contentment with little was a cornerstone of happiness:
“Poverty, when measured by the natural purpose of life, is great wealth.”

3. Active Pleasure: Enrichment Within Tranquility

While static pleasure is the foundation, Epicurus also valued active pleasures, those
involving motion and emotional engagement. He wrote:

“Joy and exultation are considered as active pleasures involving motion.”
These pleasures enrich life but must be pursued wisely. Examples include:
- Philosophical study: Pleasure arises from understanding and insight.

- Escape from calamity: The joy of surviving danger is intense and memorable.
- Memory of past joys: Recalling a well-spent life renews the spirit.

- Public spectacles: The wise man may enjoy them with moderation.

- Friendship: A source of the “purest pleasure,” both practical and emotional.

“Friendship goes dancing round the world, proclaiming to us all to awake to the praises of
a happy life.”

45

Epicurus emphasized the “calculus of advantage” weighing pleasures and pains to choose
the path that brings the most lasting happiness. Sometimes, short-term pain may be
chosen for greater long-term pleasure.

By integrating these three dimensions—negative pleasure (freedom from disturbance),
simple pleasure (natural satisfaction), and active pleasure (enriching experiences)—
Epicurus constructed a nuanced philosophy of happiness.

Far from advocating indulgence, he offered a path to peace, clarity, and fulfillment,
grounded in reason and nature.,

On Pain

The Endurance of Hard Times Epicurus and his followers offered a unique perspective on
enduring suffering and hardship.

Their philosophy emphasized mental fortitude, a clear understanding of life’s limits, and a
rational view of pain..

The goal was to free individuals from the greatest disturbances of the mind—especially
fear of the gods, eternal misery, and death.

Epicurus taught that pain is inherently limited, and therefore, more endurable than people
often assume. He wrote:

“Pain does not last continuously in the flesh.”

- Chronic pain, while longer-lasting, allows for moments of pleasure and is rarely
unbearable.

“Great pains quickly put an end to life; long-enduring pains are not severe.”

This framework suggests that no pain is perpetual or insurmountable, making its
endurance logically and emotionally feasible.

“Even if the wise man is put on the rack, he is happy.”

This shows how mental joy and philosophical reflection can override physical suffering,
allowing even the hardest trials to be endured with dignity.

“Poverty, when measured by the natural purpose of life, is great wealth; but unlimited

“Plain foods bring pleasure equal to luxurious ones once the pain of need is removed.”

This philosophy teaches that contentment with little eliminates much of the suffering
rooted in excess and craving.

Transforming Hardship Through Philosophy

Epicurus’s teachings show that pain and hardship are not to be feared, but understood.

Through reason, simplicity, and mental clarity, even the most difficult experiences can be
transformed into manageable—or even negligible—parts of life.

The Calculus of Advantage
Epicurus’s Method for Living Wisely

Pleasure with Precision

Epicurus’s ethical system is often misunderstood as a call to indulgence. In truth, itis a
call to discernment. At the heart of his philosophy lies a methodical tool for decision-
making: the Calculus of Advantage, also known as the Hedonistic Calculus.

This framework is not about chasing every pleasure, it’s about choosing wisely, with the
goal of maximizing long-term happiness and minimizing pain.

Core Principles and Mechanisms
Measuring and Balancing

Epicurus taught that we must weigh pleasures and pains against each other, scrutinizing
their consequences. The process is subtractive: we subtract pain from pleasure to
determine the net benefit.

51

Example:

A mani is invited to a lavish banquet. He knows the food will be rich and delicious, but he
also knows he tends to overeat and suffer stomach pain afterward. Using the calculus, he
declines the invitation, choosing a simple meal at home that brings pleasure without pain.

Consequences and Foresight

Epicurus urged us to ask:

“What will be the result for me if the object of this desire is attained—and what if it is not?”

Example:

Awoman considers taking a high-paying job that requires constant travel and long hours.
She foresees financial gain but also loneliness, stress, and loss of time with family. She
chooses a lower-paying job with a balanced lifestyle, prioritizing peace over profit.

Sober Reasoning (Phronesis)

This is practical wisdom, the ability to investigate the reasons behind every choice and
avoidance.

Example:

Ayoung man is tempted to retaliate against someone who insulted him. But through sober
reasoning, he realizes that revenge will only prolong his anger and disturb his peace. He
lets it go, preserving his mental tranquility.

Control of Experience

The calculus empowers individuals to retain control over their experience, cultivating
freedom from external pressures.

52

Example:

A student feels pressured to pursue a prestigious degree for status. But she reflects and
chooses a field she genuinely enjoys, knowing it will bring lasting satisfaction rather than
fleeting approval.

Application in Epicurean Life
Desire Management

Epicurus classified desires into three types. The calculus helps determine which to satisfy
and which to reject.

Example:

Aman craves expensive wine but realizes that simple water quenches his thirst and brings
equal pleasure once the pain of thirst is gone. He chooses simplicity, avoiding the stress of
luxury.

Continuous Pleasure

Epicurus taught that pleasure can be continuous when pain and disturbance are absent.

Example:

A retired woman lives modestly, tending her garden, reading, and enjoying quiet evenings
with friends. She experiences stable pleasure—not from constant excitement, but from the
absence of discomfort and the presence of peace.

Enduring Pain for Greater Good

Sometimes short-term pain leads to long-term pleasure.

53

Example:

Aman undergoes dental surgery. The procedure is painful, but he endures it knowing it will
relieve chronic discomfort and restore his ability to enjoy food and conversation.

Self-Sufficiency (Autarkeia)

Contentment with little leads to freedom.

Example:

A traveler learns to enjoy simple meals and basic shelter. When luxury is unavailable, he
remains cheerful. When it is available, he enjoys it more deeply, having learned not to
depend on it.

Virtue and Justice

Virtue is chosen for the sake of pleasure—not as an abstract ideal.

Example:

A merchant chooses to deal honestly, even when deception could bring short-term gain.
He knows that justice brings peace of mind, trust from others, and long-term happiness.

Distinction from Misunderstandings

Calculus is nota license for excess. Epicurus warned that indulgence often leads to pain.

Example:

54

A man binge drinks at a party. The next day, he suffers a hangover and regrets his choices.
Epicurean wisdom would have advised moderation, preserving pleasure without
consequence.

Critics misquoted Epicureans, such as Metrodorus’s remark about “the pleasure of the
stomach.” In context, this referred to the unity of bodily and mental pleasure, not crude
indulgence.

Pleasure as Wisdom

Epicurus’s Calculus of Advantage is a method for living deliberately.

It teaches us to pause, reflect, and choose the path that leads to lasting peace. In a world
of instant gratification and emotional chaos, this ancient tool remains strikingly modern.

To live wellis to choose well. And to choose well is to think clearly—subtracting pain,
adding joy, and walking the path of tranquil pleasure.

Misconceptions about Epicurus

Epicureanism was frequently misrepresented as a philosophy that promoted sensual
indulgence.

However, Epicurus himself lived an almost ascetic life, content with basic food and water.
His doctrines emphasized moderation and the "higher pleasure of a simple life."

While his frankness in naming pleasure as the ultimate goal and grounding friendship in
mutual advantage led to charges of egoistic hedonism, his missionary zeal to bring
happiness to all people, including the poor and ignorant, suggests an altruistic hedonism.

55

He consistently stressed the importance of self-command and guarding against passions
that could disrupt inner peace.

This pursuit of pleasure is not one of fleeting sensual indulgence, but a profound and
carefully calculated state of tranquility of mind (ataraxia) and freedom from bodily pain
(aponia).

1. Pleasure as the Sole End and Criterion

Foundation in Experience:

Epicurus argued that all living creatures naturally seek pleasure and avoid pain from birth,
making pleasure the inherent and universal “beginning and end of the blessed life.” This is
an experiential fact, not an abstract ideal or moral obligation.

Sensation as Criterion:

Just as sensation is the infallible criterion of truth in physics, the feelings (pathe) of
pleasure and pain are the immediate and direct indicators of good and bad in the moral
realm.

Kinetic vs. Static Pleasure: or Active vs Negative.
Epicurus distinguished between two types of pleasure:

- Kinetic pleasure — the pleasure of movement or active gratification of desire (e.g., eating
when hungry). These are considered “impure,” as they are often accompanied or followed
by pain and the desire for renewal.

The Hedonistic Calculus:

True pleasure requires careful calculation. One must choose pleasures that do not lead to
greater discomfort and be willing to endure pains if they result in greater, more lasting
pleasure.

This continuous, long-term view of life distinguishes Epicurus from the Cyrenaics, who
focused on momentary pleasure. The limit of pleasure is reached as soon as pain due to
want is removed; further additions only diversify, not increase, true pleasure.

56

2. The Blessed Life and Overcoming Fear

The blessed life is characterized by health of the body and tranquility of the mind. This
state is attainable by anyone, regardless of wealth or status, since “the wealth demanded
by nature is both limited and easily procured.”

Role of the Mind:

While all pleasure originates in the flesh, the mind plays a crucial role in mitigating present
distress by recalling past pleasures and anticipating future ones.

This power of projection gives the mind greater control over emotions than the body has
over its sensations.

Dissipating Fears:
A primary condition for mental tranquility is freedom from the two greatest fears:

- Fear of the gods: Men must understand that the gods are blessed, immortal beings who
do not interfere in human affairs or the workings of the world. This knowledge frees the
mind from dread of divine retribution or arbitrary intervention.

- Fear of death: Death is the complete cessation of sensation and consciousness, and
therefore “nothing to us.” It cannot cause pain because sensation ends with life;
anticipation of it is groundless.

Importance of Natural Science (Physiologia):

To achieve lasting tranquility, one must understand the nature of the universe. Natural
science is the “handmaid” to ethics, dispelling mythical stories and the fear of gods and
death by demonstrating that the world operates by natural laws rather than divine will.

3. Virtues as Means to Pleasure

57

For Epicurus, virtues such as temperance, courage, and justice are not ends in themselves
but instrumental to achieving pleasure. They are chosen “for the sake of pleasure,” just as
medicine is chosen for health.

Prudence (Phronesis):

Considered the “supreme possession of life,” prudence enables a person to make right
judgments in choosing and avoiding actions to maximize pleasure and minimize pain. It
teaches that “it is not possible to live pleasantly without living prudently, honourably, and
justly.”

Temperance:

Chosen because it brings peace of mind and contributes to the perfect pleasure of
tranquility by avoiding excessive and unnecessary desires.

Courage:

Involves choosing pains to avoid greater pains. The Epicurean has a strong motive for
courage, understanding that the greatest pains are short-lived or ended by death, and
lesser pains have intervals of rest.

Justice:

Not derived from a natural moral instinct, but from a social contract based on mutual
advantage. Men agree “not to harm or be harmed” to secure freedom from fear of others,
which would otherwise disrupt mental peace. Injustice itself is not inherently evil; the real
harm lies in the fear of being detected and punished.

4. Social Relations
Public Life:

The wise man avoids public life, power, and ambition, as these are sources of “injury and
disturbance to blessedness” and create anxieties. True happiness lies in living
“unnoticed.”

58

Friendship:

Highly valued as “by far the greatest” means to the blessed life. While initially motivated by
the practical need for safety and mutual advantage, friendship can evolve into a deeper,
unselfish enjoyment for its own sake. Epicurus held that “it is more pleasant to do good
than to receive it.”

Love:

Passionate love is condemned as an “idle desire” that causes “goading restlessness” and
disturbs peace of mind.

Family Life:

Marriage and having children are generally discouraged due to the “many
unpleasantnesses and distractions” they bring from more serious philosophical pursuits,
though Epicurus showed personal affection for children.

Epicureanism is an uncompromising egoistic hedonism — the individual’s pleasure is the

of the term “Epicurean.” Epicurus strongly refuted this, emphasizing inner tranquility over
bodily indulgence.

beings and physical reality.,

“To doubt that which is evident is not in our power. To believe that which is not evident is

termed “the addition of opinion.”

Fate did not preserve most of Epicurus’s original writings, but fortunately, his devoted

Nature of Things.

Written with reverence for his teacher—whom he called “the greatest man ever”—

Titus Lucretius Carus, born in Rome in 99 B.C. and dying at the age of forty-four in 55 B.C.,
is remembered as one of the world’s great poets.

Yet his legacy has long been shadowed by rumors of mental illness.

One story claims he was driven mad by a love potion, possibly administered by his wife,

However, this tale emerged centuries after his death and lacks credible support.

And perish all in vain?

Why not prefer to make an end of life and labour too?”

Elsewhere, he observes:
“Oft again, from fear of death,
Disgust of light and life seizes on men,
And with a saddened heart they do themselves to death.”

These lines reflect not madness, but a profound philosophical grappling with mortality,
fear, and the human condition.

He was clearly disillusioned by the “squalid passions and disputes unloosed on every
side” in the world around him.

Perhaps Lucretius struggled with depression, as many sensitive minds do.

reviewed and helped refine it, an endorsement that speaks volumes about its clarity and
power.

The story of Lucretius’s madness may well have been a rumor, possibly propagated by

De Rerum Natura boldly rejects superstition, divine intervention, and fear of the afterlife,

As you will see in his poem, Lucretius’s mind was not broken, it was ablaze.

He saw the universe not as a realm of divine punishment, but as a vast, self-governing
system ruled by natural laws.

His courage to write such truths in poetic form, at a time when dogma reigned, is nota sign
of madness but of rare philosophical integrity. If anything, the rumors of his instability
reflect the discomfort his ideas caused in those who feared the freedom he offered.

Lucretius's Life According to Masson

Masson portrays Lucretius's life as largely mysterious, with few concrete details, leading to
ongoing scholarly debate.

St. Jerome's Chronicle records his birth around 94 B.C., his madness caused by a love-
philtre, his composition of books during intervals of his ailment (which Cicero later
corrected), and his suicide at age forty-four.

This dating is often reconciled with Suetonius's account (who states Lucretius died on the
day Virgil assumed the toga virilis in 54 B.C.) by favoring an earlier birthdate around 99 or
98 B.C., as suggested by scholars like Munro.

The narrative emphasizes a potential "mental disturbance" in Lucretius, inferred from the
love-philtre story and his reported madness.

Scholar Stampini suggests this points to an underlying organic illness, with the poem itself
showing signs of mental strain or melancholia, supported by Lucretius's descriptions of
dreadful visions in both waking and sleeping hours.

72

Despite his reclusive nature, Lucretius is believed to have belonged to a noble family,
evident from the independent tone of his dedication to Memmius, a prominent statesman.

His circle of friends likely included influential figures such as Cicero, Atticus, M. Brutus,
and C. Cassius, though widespread confirmation for the latter three is not explicitly found
outside of Borgius's Life of Lucretius.

He dedicated his major work, De Rerum Natura, to Memmius, whom he admired and to
whom he aimed to spread the "light" of Epicurean philosophy.

His conversion to Epicureanism likely occurred later in his life, and his commitment was so
thorough that Epicurus's philosophy entirely dominated his spirit.

This intense devotion led him to spend quiet nights composing his poem, a labor Masson
describes as physically and mentally exhausting.

The unfinished state of his poem, abruptly broken off, further suggests a life cut short by
unforeseen circumstances, possibly his nervous disease or suicide.

Lucretius's Ideas and Philosophy According to Masson

Lucretius's philosophical contributions, as interpreted by Masson, are intrinsically linked
to Epicurus and are primarily articulated in his monumental poem, De Rerum Natura.

Atomic Theory:

Lucretius eloquently expounded the atomic theory of Democritus as developed by
Epicurus.

Nature of Atoms: All things are composed of indestructible, solid "first-beginnings" or
"seeds" (atoms), which are exceedingly small and of many different shapes.

World's Origin:

The world is created from the random collision and combination of these atoms in infinite
space andtime,

"without the hand of the Gods."
Declination (Clinamen):

Acrucial element is the atomic declination or "swerve" (clinamen), a slight, unpredictable
deviation in the atoms' downward path.

73

This swerve allows atoms to meet and combine, forming the world, and, more importantly,
provides the "basis for free-will in humans,

" liberating the mind from absolute necessity. Lucretius argues that if free-will exists in
man, it must also exist in the matter from which man is composed, applying the axiom
"nothing can come from nothing."

Soul's Nature:

The soulis corporeal and mortal, made of exceedingly fine, smooth, and round atoms. It is
dispersed after death, leading to an "annihilation of sensation."

The mind (animus) and soul (anima) together form a single nature and are interlaced
throughout the body.

Critique of Religion and Superstition:

Lucretius launched an uncompromising attack on the "superstitious fears" and "false
religion" of his day, which he believed enslaved mankind and caused immense suffering,
as exemplified by the sacrifice of Iphigenia.

Nature of Gods: He adopted the Epicurean view that gods exist but are perfectly tranquil,
blissful beings dwelling in the "intermundia" (spaces between worlds), entirely
unconcerned with human affairs or the governance of the world.

They are perceived through "subtle images" that float into the minds of the pious.

Purpose of Physics: The primary goal of studying physics and understanding natural laws is
to dispel these irrational fears and demonstrate that all phenomena occur through natural
causes, not divine caprice.

This principle of "law in Nature" is a key contribution.
Ethics and Happiness (Ataraxia):

Lucretius's ethical teachings, derived from Epicurus, aim at achieving happiness, defined
as "freedom from pain in the body and from trouble in the mind" (ataraxia or tranquillity).

Political Quietism: He advocated withdrawal from public and political affairs, as they
introduce anxiety and dependence on others, hindering the attainment of peace of mind.

Importance of Friendship: Friendship is a cornerstone of Epicurean life, offering security
and support.

Control of Desires and Temperament: He emphasized the "eradication of excessive
desires, ambition, avarice, and luxury," which are sources of misery and mental turmoil.

74

He believed that through reason and training, individuals could largely overcome inherited
negative temperaments and live a life

"worthy of the Gods."
Poetic and Scientific Contributions
Lucretius is unique in using epic poetry to convey scientific and philosophical doctrines.

Scientific Method: He possessed a "distinct grasp of scientific method" and a "strong
scientific imagination," using vivid illustrations to explain complex atomic theories.

"Flaming Walls of the World":

His iconic phrase, flammantia moenia mundi, encapsulates the grandeur and mystery of
the universe while being rooted in Epicurean cosmology.

Influence:

His poem profoundly influenced subsequent Roman poets like Virgil and Horace, shaping
their understanding of nature and the human condition, even if they later diverged from
strict Epicureanism.

Masson also points out subtle contradictions, noting that despite his materialistic creed,
Lucretius's deep compassion for humanity and his appreciation for the world's beauty
often elevate his work beyond mere Epicurean dogma, suggesting a "truer Lucretius"
emerging from his intense poetic spirit.

Lucretius’s Insights on Modern science

Masson’s work, drawing on the philosophies of Epicurus and Lucretius, presents a
compelling case for the ancient origins of concepts akin to modern natural selection and
evolution.

He suggests that Ancient Greece can claim to have originated and developed the doctrine
which Darwin later revived as Natural Selection.

Tracing the roots of this doctrine, Masson begins with Empedocles, from whom Epicurus
adopted certain ideas on the origin of life and species. Empedocles believed that
organisms arose through

“fortuitous combinations of the elements” and that there was a “final preservation of the
fittest, while the others perish as they are produced.”

His vision, however, was cruder than later developments, imagining, for example,
individual organs wandering independently before combining, an idea Epicurus rejected.

75

Lucretius, in De Rerum Natura, offers what Masson sees as remarkable anticipations of
Darwinian principles.

He describes the early earth producing a variety of “monstrous creatures,” many of which
were “unable to preserve themselves from danger, or to secure food,” and thus perished.

Only a few well-adapted forms survived. Animals that lacked natural advantages or
usefulness to humans eventually “died out in the fight for existence.” Masson observes
that Lucretius “sees as clearly as any Darwinist the pitilessness of Nature” and
demonstrates “a firm grasp of this central doctrine of Darwinism.”

In explaining the origin of the world, Lucretius describes atoms

“driven together and tormented by constant shocks from all eternity,” attempting “motions
and unions of every kind” until they

“fall at length into the arrangements out of which this world of ours has been formed.”

This recalls Democritus’s metaphor of “atomic sifting,” in which atoms “run like to like”
and are “sifted out” to form worlds and living things.

Lucretius firmly rejects the notion of a “Divine Mechanist fashioning the World from
without,” instead presenting Nature as a “self-working Power.”

This view aligns with modern materialist perspectives that detach the Creator from the
universe and locate the formative power within matter itself.

Despite these parallels, Masson stresses important differences between Lucretius’s and
Darwin’s theories. Lucretius repeatedly and emphatically asserted the permanence and
distinctness of all natural types.

His concept of species did not include the capacity to vary in response to environmental
pressures; he believed that

“a living creature must cease to exist on the slightest change of its form or type.”

On this point, Masson notes, Lucretius “would at once have joined battle with Lamarck
and Darwin.”

Unlike Darwinian evolution, which involves the gradual transformation of species into one
another, Lucretius’s theory did not embrace such modification.

Instead, he emphasized the earth’s “marvellous procreative power” to produce a diversity
of organisms, with only the suitably framed ones surviving.

Masson also connects Lucretius’s ideas to later scientific materialism.

76

In his famous Belfast Address, Professor Tyndall described how modern materialism
explains the origin of life through “evolution,” seeing in matter “the promise and potency of
every form and quality of life.” Tyndall’s account of natural selection depicts nature

“daily and hourly scrutinizing throughout the world every variation... rejecting that which is
bad, preserving and adding up that which is good.”

Yet Tyndall differed from Darwin in suggesting that matter itself possessed the inherent
potency for life from the outset, rather than requiring a single “primordial living form” as
Darwin proposed.

Similarly, Clifford suggested that the first molecule of protoplasm was “produced by a
coincidence and preserved by natural selection.”

Although Lucretius did not articulate natural selection in the fully developed form later
presented by Darwin, his atomic theory,

His insistence on nature’s self-sufficiency, and his observations on the survival and
extinction of living forms provided significant conceptual groundwork that foreshadowed
later evolutionary thought.

Epicurean philosophy places friendship at the very heart of human happiness and well-

Epicurus’s letters revered by his followers “as if epistles of an apostle” along with his

“Win the good-feeling of friends, which is the true wealth.”

“The impelling motive for revealing to men the way to happiness.”

Epicurus acknowledged:

“Sweet is the memory of a dead friend.”

He advised expressing grief not through lamentation, but through meditation on their lives.

Within the Epicurean brotherhood, he promoted an “immortality of remembrance,”

“Pious memoirs of the deceased associates, ”

Friendship is essential for satisfying the “craving for human love” and preventing

“Enlarge the sphere of our affections.”

81

In summary, Epicurus saw friendship not merely as a social bond, but as a philosophical

Epicurus’s Revolt
Against the Intellectual Giants of Greece

Epicurus was no stranger to controversy. Many philosophers insulted him for his direct and
uncompromising opinions, and he, along with his followers, often responded with sharp
criticism and satire.

As the Egyptian proverb says, “There is no smoke without fire.” Epicurus and his school
leveled pointed critiques against several of Greece’s most revered thinkers, including
Socrates and Aristotle.

Criticism of Socrates

Epicurus and his followers condemned key aspects of Socrates’ philosophical method and
public persona:

- Socratic Irony: Epicurus rejected Socratic irony, viewing it as a form of dishonesty—a
deliberate concealment of a “devastating skill in debate” that often led to the humiliation
of interlocutors.

This ethical objection marked a major divergence from Socratic practice, as Epicureans
valued parresia—absolute frankness and transparency.

- Satire and Ridicule: Colotes, an Epicurean disciple, ridiculed Socrates in his satirical
writings on Greek philosophers.

84

The Epicurean school was known for using biting language and satire against prominent

Criticism of Aristotle

Epicurus and his school also expressed strong philosophical opposition to Aristotle:

- Refutative Writings: Hermarchus, Epicurus’s successor, authored Against Aristotle,
signaling a direct challenge to Aristotelian thought. Metrodorus, another leading
Epicurean, wrote Against the Logicians and Against the Sophists, which likely included
critiques of Aristotle’s contributions to logic and dialectic.

- Ethical Teachings: Epicurus’s concept of pleasure sharply diverged from Aristotle’s view
of happiness.

While Aristotle defined the Chief Good as “living well” through the full exercise of virtue,
Epicurus argued that pleasure is the “root of all good” and “the beginning and the end of a
happy life.”

He rejected Aristotle’s idea of a hierarchy of pleasures culminating in intellectual
contemplation, insisting instead that all pleasures belong to a single class.

- Logic and Dialectic: Epicurus dismissed both Aristotelian logic and Platonic dialectic,
believing they relied too heavily on abstract argumentation and led away from practical
understanding.

He championed simplicity and clarity in philosophical expression, in contrast to Aristotle’s
complex analytical style.

- Physical Theories: Epicurus took a unique position in natural philosophy, selecting and
modifying ideas from earlier thinkers to suit his own framework.

85

He prioritized physics over ethics and objected to certain physical theories, particularly
those that neglected ethical concerns or promoted determinism.

Hermarchus wrote On Empedocles, and Epicurus himself authored critiques of physicists,
favoring thinkers like Anaxagoras and Archelaus.

- Scientific Disputes: Epicurus adopted Democritus’s atomic theory and cosmogony,
which Aristotle had famously dismissed. This scientific divergence further deepened the
philosophical rift between the two schools.

Broader Opposition

Epicurus and his followers also expressed strong criticism toward Plato and the Platonists,
as well as the Stoics. Their opposition was not merely personal—it was rooted in
fundamental philosophical disagreements about ethics, metaphysics, and the role of
reason in human life.

Epicurus’s revolt against the intellectual elite of Greece was not driven by disdain for
learning, but by a commitment to clarity, honesty, and the pursuit of peace.

His philosophy challenged the dominant traditions of his time, offering a radically different
vision of happiness grounded in pleasure, friendship, and freedom from fear.

Epicurus’s Revolt Against Plato and Platonism

Platonism was, in many ways, the philosophical punching bag for Epicurus. His opposition
to Plato was not incidental—it was foundational.

The structure of Epicureanism was shaped in direct response to Platonic thought, and
Epicurus positioned himself as a penetrating critic of Platonism.

Rejection of Platonic Dialectic

Epicurus explicitly rejected Platonic dialectic, calling it a “superfluity” and criticizing its
reliance on abstract verbal argument. He viewed it as:

“Walking around uselessly and harping upon the question, ‘What is the meaning of
good?’”

His rejection was both ethical and intellectual. He denied the four core assumptions of
dialectic:

86

- That reason alone is the criterion of truth
- That sensations are unreliable
- That phenomena are unstable

- That eternal ideas are the only real existences.

Critique of the Theory of Ideas

As a materialist, Epicurus accepted only atoms and the void as real. He rejected Plato’s
eternal Forms as unreal abstractions.

He ridiculed the notion that:
“Horseness” is a real existence, while actual horses are mere shadows.

He also mocked Plato’s explanation of aging—attributing it to the “detrition of angular
atoms”as absurd, since Epicurean atoms were considered indestructible.

Opposition to Platonic Education (Paideia)

Epicurus opposed the entire Platonic curriculum, especially geometry, rhetoric, and
dialectic. He advised:

“To sea with your swift ship, blessed boy, and flee from all education (paideia).”

He praised students who had “kept themselves pure” by avoiding these studies,
particularly geometry and arithmetic, which he saw as promoting belief in the divinity of
heavenly bodies—an idea he considered harmful to peace of mind.

Gods and Cosmology

Epicurus criticized Plato’s association of divinity with sphericity, asking why gods couldn’t
take other beautiful geometric forms like cubes or pyramids.

He found the idea of a “spherical god hurtling through space” to be a strange and
misguided conception of happiness.

87

He condemned Plato for focusing exclusively on celestial phenomena and treating
heavenly bodies as divine, calling this view “inimical to peace of mind.” He labeled Plato a
“mythologer” for accepting astral myths while ignoring terrestrial realities, and considered
belief in astral gods both “absurd” and “blasphemous.”

Ethics and Virtue

Epicurus challenged Plato’s idea of an ascending series of pleasures culminating in
intellectual contemplation. Instead, he emphasized the essential unity of pleasure, arguing
that all pleasures belong to the same class.

He criticized Plato for recognizing only “peaks of pleasure,” which made sustained
happiness impossible.

He rejected the “harassing hopes of reward” tied to belief in immortality and the afterlife,
and viewed Plato’s notion of true knowledge—apprehension of eternal ideas—as a form of
skepticism, due to its dismissal of sensory experience.

He called Platonic justice a “specious pretense” and rejected the idea of “absolute
justice.” He also derided Plato’s undemocratic social theory that divided citizens into men
of gold, silver, and iron.

Specific Figures and Satirical Epithets

Epicurus referred to Plato as “the Golden,” likely mocking his elitist social divisions or his
taste for splendor.

He called Plato’s followers “high-steppers” and “hangers-on of Dionysus.”

Hermarchus, Epicurus’s successor, wrote Against Plato, while Colotes, another
Epicurean, satirized ancient philosophers including Plato, with surviving fragments
targeting works like Lysis and Euthydemus.

General Opposition

Epicurus considered Platonism and skepticism among his chief abominations. He
positioned himself as an Antiplatonist, and more than half of his Forty Authorized
Doctrines directly contradict Platonic principles.

88

He classified Plato’s philosophy as metaphysical, separating phenomena and form from
matter and substance—an approach Epicurus saw as misleading and incompatible with a
life of peace.

Epicurus’s Criticisms of the Stoics

Epicurus’s relationship with Stoicism was marked by philosophical antagonism, though
this tension developed after his lifetime.

The two schools were not contemporaries in origin or early influence.

Chronological Discrepancy

The traditional grouping of Stoics, Epicureans, and Skeptics reverses the actual historical
order. Epicurus’s philosophy was a fully developed system before Zeno, the founder of
Stoicism, began teaching.

The feud between the schools was largely initiated by Chrysippus, the second founder of
Stoicism, after Epicurus’s death. Thus, Stoicism had no direct influence on Epicurus’s life
or thought.

Ethics and Morality

Epicurus reacted strongly against what he saw as exaggerated and dangerous Stoic
doctrines, including:

- The belief that virtue and pleasure are essential opposites

- The doctrine that all sins are equal, denying any difference in degree between faults and
vices

He rejected the Stoic ideal of a rigid, unemotional morality based on an inflexible cosmic
order. Instead, Epicurus recognized emotions as natural and integral to human life. While
Stoics praised self-sufficiency, Epicurus offered a more nuanced view, linking it to freedom
from unnecessary desires, not emotional detachment.

Logic

Epicurus dismissed Stoic logic, criticizing it for relying on abstract verbal arguments that
distracted from practical understanding. He referred to logicians—including Stoics—as:

89

“The destroyers.”

He opposed their insistence on deductive reasoning alone, favoring a more empirical and
sensory-based approach to knowledge.

Physics and Theology
Epicurus’s teachings implicitly challenged Stoic physics and theology.

While Stoic physics were sometimes seen as weak, their conception of God as a universal
Providence was considered more grand than Epicurus’s non-interventionist divine beings.

Epicurus’s broader “war against religion” encompassed many prevailing theological views,
including those later adopted by Stoicism. Notably, the Stoic term pronoia (providence) is
absent from Epicurus’s writings, suggesting that this doctrine had not yet emerged during
his time.

Direct Attacks and Misrepresentations

Stoic philosophers were persistent critics of Epicurus:

- Diotimus the Stoic falsely attributed fifty lewd letters to Epicurus in an attempt to
discredit him

- Posidonius and Epictetus also attacked Epicurus, with Epictetus calling him a “filthy
talker” and abusing him roundly

- Stoic critics often misrepresented Epicurean doctrines, especially his views on pleasure.

They reduced his concept of pleasure as freedom from pain to absurd caricatures—such
as comparing it to a guest enjoying a cocktail.

Ironically, the Stoics also borrowed freely from Epicureanism, notably adopting the
concept of prolepsis (anticipation), a key element in Epicurean epistemology.

Epicurus’s criticisms of Stoicism were rooted in deep philosophical differences, especially
regarding ethics, logic, and theology.

90

While the Stoics emphasized virtue, duty, and divine order, Epicurus championed
pleasure, clarity, and peace of mind. The rivalry between the schools shaped centuries of
debate, and their opposing visions of the good life remain influential to this day.

Epicurus’s Core Mission: Defend Happiness Through Certainty

Epicurus believed that human happiness depends on knowledge, and skepticism—
especially Pyrrhonism—undermined that by denying the reliability of reason and sensation.

To Epicurus, this wasn’t just an intellectual disagreement; it was a threat to mental peace
and ethical living

His Canon: A Weapon Against Doubt

To counter skepticism, Epicurus built his Canon of Truth, which grounded knowledge in:
- Sensations: Direct experiences of the world

- Anticipations (prolepsis): Innate mental impressions or preconceptions

- Feelings: Pleasure and pain as guides to value

This was a radical move: instead of abstract dialectics, Epicurus trusted empirical reality
and subjective experience as the foundation for truth.

His Philosophical Counterattacks

Epicurus didn’t just build his own system—he actively refuted skeptical arguments:

- He exposed the flaw in rejecting all sensations: if you deny them, you lose the very tools
needed to judge deception.

- He argued that errors come from judgment, not sensation itself.

- He showed that contradictions in perception (like the bent oar) are resolved by deeper
sensory analysis, not by abandoning trust in the senses.

His Critique of Other Philosophers

91

Epicurus saw skeptical tendencies even in thinkers like:
- Plato, who dismissed sensory data in favor of eternal forms

- Democritus, whose relativism (“by convention”) threatened objective truth

Even though Epicurus borrowed from Democritus’s atomism, he rejected the idea that
reality was unknowable or merely conventional.

Refutative Writings and Direct Engagement

Epicurus and his followers didn’t shy away from confrontation.

They wrote polemics against skeptics, dialecticians, and sophists—anyone who sowed
doubt or confusion.

His work Against Eurylochus was a direct attack on Pyrrhonian skepticism, and Metrodorus
joined the fray with his own critiques.

Serenity vs. Skepticism

Ultimately, Epicurus believed that skepticism breeds turmoil, while certainty breeds
peace. His goal wasn’t just intellectual clarity, it was ataraxia, the serene state of mind that
comes from understanding the world and trusting your experience of it.

Happiness and Genetics

|. Rethinking Happiness

Happiness has long been considered a product of life circumstances, personal choices,

Epicurus taught that true happiness lies in ataraxia (peace of mind) and aponia (freedom
from bodily pain) states achievable through reason, simplicity, and friendship. These ideals
emphasize mental clarity and emotional stability over material wealth or fleeting

Modern science adds another layer: genetics. Recent research, including the study
Happiness and Genetics — Nature, Nurture, and the Epicurean Mind, suggests that our
genes play a significant role in shaping our emotional baseline. This doesn’t mean
happiness is predetermined—but it does mean that biology influences how easily we
experience joy, resilience, and contentment.

ll. The Genetic Blueprint of Well-Being

Behavioral genetics research, including twin and DNA studies, reveals that approximately
30-40% of our happiness is heritable.

106

This is known as the happiness set point—a genetically influenced baseline around which
our mood tends to fluctuate.

Key genetic factors include:

- 5-HTTLPR, a gene affecting serotonin transport, linked to mood stability and emotional
sensitivity.

- Dopamine-related genes, which influence reward processing, motivation, and pleasure.
- Oxytocin, a hormone associated with trust, bonding, and emotional warmth.

- BDNF (Brain-Derived Neurotrophic Factor), which supports neural plasticity and
resilience; low levels are associated with depression.

- Cortisol, the stress hormone, which rises in response to chronic stress and contributes to
anxiety and sadness.

Professor Meike Bartels’s twin studies show that even identical twins raised apart often
share similar levels of well-being, highlighting the genetic component. However, genes are
not destiny. Environmental factors, personal habits, and philosophical beliefs interact with
our biology to shape our lived experience.

Key Insight: You may inherit a predisposition for optimism or vulnerability, but your
choices, relationships, and mindset can override or enhance these traits

Ill. Hormones and Neurochemicals: Activating Joy and Easing Sadness

Our emotional states are shaped by a dynamic interplay of hormones and
neurotransmitters. Understanding how these systems work—and how to activate them—
can help us cultivate happiness and manage sadness.

- Serotonin, often called the "feel-good" neurotransmitter, regulates mood and promotes
calm. It can be boosted through regular exercise, exposure to sunlight, a balanced diet,
and meditation.

- Dopamine drives motivation and reward. Activities like goal-setting, creative engagement,
and novelty stimulate dopamine release, enhancing pleasure and focus.

- Oxytocin is released during social bonding, physical touch, and acts of trust. It fosters
emotional warmth and connection, reinforcing the value of friendship.

- Endorphins are natural painkillers that reduce stress and elevate mood. They are

The Legacy of Epicurus

From Atoms to Ethics

Epicurus’s ideas were radical in their original context—and remain quietly revolutionary
today. Though much of his original writing was lost over time, especially during the early
Christian era when Epicurean texts were suppressed, his legacy endured through the
voices of others, most notably the Roman poet-philosopher Titus Lucretius Carus.

Lucretius’s epic poem De Rerum Natura (“On the Nature of Things”) preserved and
expanded Epicurean thought, passing it through antiquity, the Renaissance, and into
modernity.

Epicurus’s philosophy was not merely a guide to pleasure or a manual for living—it was a
complete cosmology, epistemology, and ethical system that challenged prevailing
religious and metaphysical doctrines. His materialism, empiricism, and ethical clarity laid
foundational groundwork for early scientific inquiry and modern moral philosophy. His
teachings continue to influence secularism, humanistic values, and the pursuit of mental
well-being.

His school—the Garden in Athens—was revolutionary in its inclusivity, welcoming women,
slaves, and foreigners. It was one of the first philosophical communities to embody
egalitarian ideals, offering a model of intellectual democracy centuries ahead of its time.

Lucretius: The Poet of Atoms

Titus Lucretius Carus (c. 94-55 BCE), the most celebrated Roman Epicurean, immortalized
Epicurean philosophy through poetic mastery in De Rerum Natura. This six-book epic was
a sweeping defense of Epicurean physics, metaphysics, and ethics, aimed at liberating the
mind from superstition and fear.

- Lucretius vividly described a universe composed of indivisible atoms moving in the void,
firmly rejecting divine intervention and teleology.

- He proposed proto-Darwinian ideas, describing how nature selects what survives—an
evolutionary perspective predating Darwin by nearly two millennia.

113

- His critique of superstition and religion as sources of irrational fear echoed and expanded
Epicurus’s quest for ataraxia—serene freedom from anxiety.

Lucretius’s work influenced Renaissance humanists like Giordano Bruno, Enlightenment
thinkers such as Voltaire, and early modern scientists who revered naturalistic
explanations. Though his atomic theory was later eclipsed by Newtonian physics, his
worldview anticipated the scientific method: observe, explain, and reject unfounded
metaphysics.

Epicurus and the Birth of Scientific Thinking

Epicureanism was among the first comprehensive philosophies to explain the world purely
through material causes, denying the interference of gods in natural processes. Key
teachings include:

- All phenomena have natural causes rooted in the interaction of atoms.

- The soulis material, composed of fine atoms, and dissolves with the body at death—
removing fear of an afterlife.

- Sensory experience is the foundation of human knowledge, highlighting Epicurus’s proto-
empiricism.

Epicurean principles influenced early disciplines such as meteorology, biology, and
medicine.

The founder of the Alexandrian school of medicine, Herophilus, likely drew from
naturalizing principles akin to Epicurean thought.

During the Enlightenment, Epicureanism was revived by thinkers like Pierre Gassendi, who
reintroduced atomism in a Christian-compatible form. His work inspired scientists like
Robert Boyle and Isaac Newton to pursue explanations free of superstition. Epicurus’s
empirical mindset and rejection of supernatural causation make him a proto-scientist in
spirit, contributing enduring methods for understanding nature.

Epicurus and the Rise of Utilitarianism

Epicurus’s ethical philosophy, which centers on pleasure as the highest good, profoundly
influenced later moral thinkers and laid the intellectual groundwork for utilitarianism.

- Jeremy Bentham (1748-1 832) and John Stuart Mill (1806-1873) built upon Epicurus’s core
idea that moral rightness is defined by actions that maximize happiness and minimize
suffering.

- Bentham’s hedonic calculus can be seen as a direct intellectual descendant of
Epicurus’s systematic method of weighing pleasures and pains.

- Mill’s distinction between higher and lower pleasures parallels Epicurus’s differentiation
of kinetic (active) pleasures and katastematic (stable) pleasures, emphasizing the quality
of happiness, not just quantity.

While the utilitarians departed from strict Epicurean doctrines, they inherited key themes:
rational hedonism, pleasure as a natural guide for ethics, and the centrality of human well-
being.

Influence on Modern Thought

Epicureanism’s quiet but powerful influence persists across multiple modern disciplines:

- Secular Humanism embraces Epicurean dismissal of divine punishment, advocating
reason, compassion, and ethical living independent of religious doctrine.

- Cognitive Science and Psychology reflect Epicurean insights about the origins of human
fear, desire, and mental disturbance, anticipating therapeutic approaches to anxiety and
wellbeing.

- Neuroscience and Philosophy of Mind draw from Epicurus’s materialist view that the
mind is physical—a foundation for contemporary debates on consciousness, free will, and
ethics.

- Minimalist and Wellness Movements echo Epicurean values of simplicity, self-sufficiency
(autarkeia), and the pursuit of inner peace over external excess.

- Democratic and Egalitarian Ideals find early expression in the Garden’s radical inclusivity,
offering a philosophical precedent for modern social justice movements.

A Legacy of Liberation

Epicurus’s mission was freedom from pain, fear, and ignorance. His Garden was a
philosophical sanctuary open to all who sought a well-lived life, transcending class,

innovators, and modern thinkers, Epicurus’s voice still echoes across millennia.

He taught that true happiness lies not in wealth or power but in tranquility, intellectual
