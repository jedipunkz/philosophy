Philosophy

The Stoics, like the Epicureans, give philosophy a practical
purpose: it is the education of the mind and soul in virtue.
Knowledge is not valuable in itself, but rather insofar as it leads to a
virtuous life, which is the only happy life. Except that this purpose
cannot be achieved except through science: true morality
presupposes true knowledge.

With this position the Stoics thus distinguish themselves from
those who esteem knowledge for knowledge's sake and place the
perfection or highest form of human activity in the contemplative

life.

On the contrary, the Cynics despise science and make
philosophy consist solely in strength of mind and the practical

exercise of virtue.

According to the Stoics, virtue, in its most general sense,
includes: rectitude of thought that aims at truth and knows how to
guard against error; exact knowledge of what is, of the nature of
things; and practical and moral rectitude.

We thus have a trilogy: logic, physics, and ethics. Hence the three
parts of philosophy: logic or dialectics, physics, and ethics, which
had already begun to be distinguished in the academic school.

The most important part, of course, remains Ethics; but the
earliest Stoics, conceiving the parts of philosophy as a whole,
opened the way to theoretical research, which they cultivated with
no less diligence than the Peripatetics. To demonstrate this
connection, they use several comparisons: they liken philosophy to
an egg, of which logic would be the shell, ethics the white, and
physics the innermost part; or to an animal, of which logic would be
the bones and tendons, ethics the fleshy parts, and physics the soul;
or to a fertile field, which is perhaps the most successful
comparison: logic would be the hedge that protects it, ethics the
fruits, and physics the earth and trees.

The meaning of all these comparisons is what we have said:
rectitude of thought that protects the wise man from errors,
knowledge of nature as the basis and root of virtue, and finally the
moral life which is the fruit and perfection of all wisdom, indeed

wisdom itself in its most concrete meaning.

The Logic.

The Stoics were probably the first to use this word to designate
the science of thought. However, they understood it in a very broad
sense. Logic is the science of the Logos, which is both thought and
speech. They also included grammar and rhetoric (grammar as part

of dialectics, rhetoric as the art of continuous discourse).

The important part of the Stoic Logic concerns the theory of
knowledge, and more specifically the question of the criterion of
truth. Human knowledge begins with sensation. Originally, the
Stoics say, when man is born, the soul is like a tabula rasa, and the
first characters imprinted upon it are those that come from
sensations. Thus, the representation of objects arises in us, which is
an affection produced in us by the object, by the representable
object.

This imprint of objects on the spirit causes a modification of
the soul and, comparing the capacity of this to be modified (even by
several objects at the same time), to that of the air when many

together speak and shout.

From these first impressions are formed the images that
constitute memory, and from the multitude of similar images,

experience, as Aristotle had already taught.

From perceptions and images we form general ideas, concepts,

some of which form naturally and spontaneously.

For the Stoics, the criterion for evaluating the objective validity
of our knowledge is important, starting precisely with sensible
knowledge which is the starting point and the basis of all other
knowledge.

The Stoics know that some of our representations are true,
others false: and some can be said to be true in one sense and false in
another (as when Orestes sees his sister Electra and believes her to
be a fury): the question therefore is whether there is a criterion for
distinguishing true representations from false images or phantoms.

The Skeptics denied that this criterion existed; the Stoics
admitted it and placed it in the so-called cataleptic representation: a
sensible representation that posits and recognizes as evident the
object from which it originates, thus guaranteeing its full conformity
with the object itself.

Such a representation is impressed and sealed in us by an object,
and is so determined that it reproduces all the particular properties
of the object, and is such that no other object could produce it.

Naturally, one must know how to observe, avoid, or eliminate
possible causes of error, look at the thing from multiple angles,

change the distance, and so on.

When a representation is like this, it imposes itself on us and
provokes our assent and we believe that through it we apprehend

something real.

It may be that we give assent even to things that are not true,
because assent is an act of our will, but in the case of the Stoic
representation, assent is not arbitrary: the representation has such
characteristics that it forces us to assent, almost pulling us by the
hair. The result is knowledge, learning, understanding, the taking
possession of objects, of reality.

The criterion of truth (or of the objective reality of our
representations) lies, according to the Stoics, in the intuitive
evidence that provokes assent in us and makes us believe in the
reality of that object.

Furthermore, the Stoics, like the Epicureans before them, were
on the path to recognizing that the criterion of reality does not lie in
the single representation, but in the coherence of all the data of

experience.

The Principles of Physics

The Physics of the Stoics included not only the science of
nature properly speaking, and of man as part of nature (cosmology
and anthropology), but also Theology and in general what we would
call Metaphysics, the science of the first principles and the ultimate
reasons of the universe: it is therefore the science of reality in its
totality, an integral part of philosophy as the theoretical foundation

of moral life.

The Stoics begin from the principle, already formulated by Plato,
that what is real is what can act or be affected: from which they
conclude, like the Epicureans, that all realities are bodies. They
admit that there are two principles of all things: an active principle
and a passive principle. The passive principle is matter or substance,
conceived as stripped of all form and quality, inert, and motionless
by itself; the active or dynamic principle is a force always in action,

informing matter, giving it quality, moving it, and organizing it.

One cannot exist without the other. The Stoics call this force
reason, ot even God, a divine force. However, this cosmic reason, or
divine force, or active principle of the world, is itself corporeal in

nature: it is not an immaterial or spiritual force.

The Stoics, faithful to their dogma that all reality is body, needed

to conceive of a corporeal substance so subtle and ethereal, and so

12

to speak, spiritualized, that it acts like a spirit. And of all things that
exist, the most mobile, the most active, the one that penetrates and
invests everything, is fire, which Heraclitus already posited as the
first principle and substance or symbol of the substance of the
world. Fire is everywhere. Heat is the principle and condition of all
life. And then the springs of hot water, the volcanoes, the stars that
shine in the firmament, the sparks that spring from beaten stone, tell
us that fire hides in every matter, penetrates everywhere.

Of course, this cosmic fire should not be confused with our
crude flame, which destroys and consumes. No, it is a life-giving fire
that penetrates and mixes with universal matter, pervading it all,

circulating throughout the world like honey in the cells of a beehive.

It is a fiery spirit, a warm breath, and is also called a creative or
artistic fire, which proceeds in an orderly and methodical manner to
generate things. And they also call it ether, which is fire itself in its
purest and most subtle form. And this divine essence dominates and
vivifies universal matter, mixing with it in a kind of sui generis
blend, whereby the substances are intimately united without
merging, entering whole into one another while each retains its
distinctive properties, much like burning iron, when struck on the
anvil, and the fire penetrates it entirely; or better still, like the
organized body and the soul that pervades it and vivifies it in all its
parts.

13

Cosmic fire is the animating spirit of the world. And they add
another name: they also call it seminal reason: it is like a developing
germ, and contains within itself the seeds of all particular beings, so
that by developing it gives rise to that ordered variety, that

harmonious construction that constitutes the world.

These seminal reasons recall the Platonic ideas and the forms
that Aristotle spoke of, not separate from reality but immanent in it:
they are the generative and formative forces of things, all unified or
dependent on the single force that is the animating spirit of the
world: it is a conception that is both rational and organic, in that
cosmic reason and the world itself are concetved as the

development of a living being.

And just as in a living being all the parts are animated and the
soul is present everywhere, and the organism is like a delicate
instrument whose single string, when touched, makes all the others
vibrate; so God, or cosmic reason, is everywhere; it is the world
itself in its rational character and in its infection; it is an immanent
God who identifies himself with the world; and the whole world is
like an immense, immortal living being, of which all the parts

conspire together and correspond to one another.

From their point of view, the Stoics also attempt a cosmogony,
which does not differ greatly from that imagined by the pre-
Socratics, especially by Heraclitus.

14

The world was born when, from the primitive fire, the universal
substance was transformed first into air and then into water; the
coatsest part of this condensed into earth, and the finer part
vaporized, and this, increasingly fine, became fire, that ethereal fire
which, moving in circles, gave rise to the celestial spheres and the

stars.

Thus, we must distinguish principles from elements. Principles
are eternal, while elements (fire, air, water, and earth) are
transformations of the primordial fire, and can also perish, except,

of course, fire from which everything derives.

From the different mixtures of elements, combined in different
proportions, particular things are born; and their relative position
determines the shape of the world: the earth with its seas at the
center, then the sphere of air, then the celestial spheres.

The universe has a spherical shape and is finite, it is a continuous
whole, but it is surrounded by infinite emptiness, and finite as it is, it
has a round shape: this is the origin and meaning of that round and
fickle God that we find in Cicero, and that the Epicureans mocked:
the spherical shape is, as Plato had already taught, the most perfect

and the most suited to circular motion.

And just as the world was formed from the primitive fire or
divine substance, so it returns to it. When the revolutions of the

world and the stars have completed their course, and after a certain

15

number of years the planets find themselves all together occupying
the respective points from which they originally started, and all
possible events have occurred; thus, the current cycle of events
having been exhausted, the great year, as they called it, is completed
(let's say that it is, according to certain calculations, 12,945 years),
then the differentiated matter and all particular existences are
reabsorbed into the primitive substance: the world is consumed in a

universal conflagration.

The global process ends in catastrophe, with the return of all
things to their divine source. Our existence and our current world

are like an episode in this divine life.

An episode, however, that repeats itself periodically, because the
universal conflagration is not an annihilation but a return of things
to their original state: the divine substance remains, containing the
seed of new rebirths, of a renewal or palingenesis of the world: a
new cycle of existences begins, a new process that unfolds
identically to the first; the same celestial spheres that complete their

revolutions and the same earth inhabited by the same men.

Religion

Thus will Socrates and Plato and their friends return: it was a

Pythagorean idea of the eternal recurrence of the same things.

What is important in the Stoic doctrine is the concept of an
internal law that determines all the details of the world process:
nothing happens without a preceding cause, and the order and series
of causes is perfectly determined: the rigorous connection of causes
and effects constitutes an inviolable necessity: which, however, is
not a mechanical and blind necessity resulting from the movement
and combination of the simple parts of matter, but is a necessity
founded in reason and an expression of the reason of the world:
providence, which works according to ends and orders everything
for the best.

The world is a rational order, in which every particular thing has

its place and the world is a coherent whole.

It is a conception that is both deterministic and teleological; and
when the Stoics' religious interest prevails or they want to approach
the popular conception, they end up identifying their metaphysical
principle with the will of Zeus.

Thus we have a series of expressions, God, Reason, Nature, Fire

the artificer or artist, Seminal Reason, Necessity, Destiny,

17

Providence, Divine Will, which signify the same principle either in
itself or in its various relations with the multiple particular

existences that make up the universe.

For this reason, their physics is both cosmology and theology.
The natural force that produces and manifests itself in the world is
divine reason, God himself. When this animated whole is conceived
in its unity, it can be called either God or the world: this is the so-
called pantheism of the Stoics.

A point that deserves to be highlighted is that reason in human
beings cannot be explained unless we admit that it has roots in

universal reality and that there is a cosmic reason.

The Stoics, in opposition to the Epicureans and contemporary
skeptical schools, were defenders of the concept of divine
providence and religious faith. Hence their attitude toward popular
beliefs and the gods of polytheism. Naturally, they rejected the
anthropomorphisms and mythical fables recounted by the poets,
but saw in the popular gods partial and diverse expressions of the
single, universal divinity they professed.

They seek a physical reason for traditional fables: therefore, an
allegorical interpretation of myths, according to the different
elements in which the Gods act or the efficacy they exert:

sometimes also resorting to etymological interpretations.

18

Zeus, which comes from living, represents universal life; and the
divinity is called Juno the air, Poseidon the sea, Demeter the earth,
Hephaistos (Hephaestus) the fire.

In the same spirit, they considered and treated divination or
prophecy, founded for them on the rational connection of all things
and phenomena, which makes the world a whole in agreement with
itself, on what was called the sympathy of nature, by which an event
can be an indication or sign of another future. This, of course, does
not protect them from superstition and the criticism of less

credulous adversaties.

Finally, another consequence of the Stoics' pantheism and their
teleological conception is their optimism, their obligation to
demonstrate the perfection of the world, after having used this
perfection and its beauty to demonstrate the existence of divinity.
Naturally, they faced objections and difficulties: the existence of evil
and pain. Hence their Theodicy, the justification of providence.

Their general answer is that providence concerns the whole, not
the single parts and not the individuals as such, but only insofar as
they are part of the whole and contribute to its harmony and
perfection: in the same way that in a beautiful comedy there may be
details that are not beautiful, but which nevertheless give a certain

grace to the entire composition.

19

Mote specifically, they consider evil (or what appears to be such)
not in isolation, but in relation to the whole. It can be considered
either as an inevitable consequence of conditions created for good
or as a means itself to achieve good: good is born from evil or is a

means to good. This is the belief of the Stoics, as of all optimists.

Moral evil is more difficult to explain, and here they help
themselves as best they can, for example, by saying that good and
evil are rationally connected and one cannot be conceived without
the other. The Stoics expended all their acumen to reconcile the
reality and necessity of things with their rational faith. The
fundamental idea of their Physics is that nature, the universe, is a
nature full of reason and divine: it is an entire ordering of ends in its

perfection.

The goal of man, part of this whole, is to rise to the
contemplation and imitation of this perfect world: to realize within
himself the reason that is the reason of all things. This is why
Physics is the metaphysical foundation of Stoic Ethics.

They distinguish natural beings into four classes: inorganic

beings, plants, animals and men.

The soul

The Stoics conceive of the soul as a warm and tense spirit
(effluvium), which has its seat in the heart, feeds on the vapors of
the blood, and sends its air currents throughout the organism,
spreading and penetrating every part of it. It is itself a body and is
mixed with the body, with that mixture that overcomes the
impenetrability of bodies, so that two substances can penetrate each
other without losing their distinctive properties: in the same way as
fire or divine spirit circulates in the world, holding it together and

enlivening it with its varying degrees of tension.

That the soul is corporeal in nature was something they had to
admit, first of all, by the general principle of their ontology that all
reality is a body, and then more specifically by considering the life of
the soul in relation to the body. The soul suffers with the body when

the latter is ill, wounded, or cut.

Then there are the bodily changes produced by emotions
(shame, fear). Chrysippus adds another consideration: death is the
separation of the soul from the body; the soul could not be joined
and then separated from the body if it were not itself corporeal.
Finally, there is another experiential fact: the transmission of the
quality of the soul from parents to children: just as appearance or

illnesses are inherited, so are intellectual or moral qualities.

21

According to the Stoics, the soul is born with the body: in
generation, a part of the parents’ soul is transmitted to the embryo:
until the embryo is formed, it has only a vegetative function: after
birth, under the action of external air, through a kind of
condensation and cooling, the animal soul develops , which in
humans is the rational soul. This does not mean that reason enters
man from the outside, into an organism that was not previously
equipped with it: no, the idea of the Stoics is that the vital spirit that
is transmitted from parents to children is already in itself a
substance endowed with reason, such that a rational being can
derive from it, except that reason is still latent in the embryo, the
vital spirit acts only as a vegetative nature, and only on contact with
the air is it transformed into an animal soul, and this, developing,
becomes a tational soul in man: which does not happen
immediately, but still requires time, because they teach that the soul
is originally a blank slate and needs the first fourteen years of life to
develop all its powers: the newly born child is not yet in fact a
rational being, he is only potentially so: only through sensations,
representations and with the exercise of his activity does he become
what he must be, a being endowed with reason, which therefore, if it
was latent in the embryo and imperfect in the child, cannot be said
to be something of extrinsic to man, the soul which is added from
outside to his original nature, but was already implicitly inherent in

that seminal substance from which he was born.

22

Although spread throughout the body, the soul has a central seat
in the chest, or more precisely in the heart (someone must have also
thought of the brain), from where the air currents that regulate the

various functions originate.

Hence the different parts of the soul, as they call them, which
are these eight: the five senses, language, the generative capacity,
and, above all, the governing principle from which all those other
parts or functions branch off and extend to their proper organs like

the tentacles of an octopus.

This multiplicity of functions does not alter the unity of the
soul. The guiding force is, as it were, the source of all other faculties.
It is reason that perceives through the senses, gives its assent to
representations, forms concepts, and transforms impulses or
instincts into conscious actions. It is through this central force that

we say "I" and feel ourselves to be persons.

The Stoics insist on this unity of the person, which for them is
constituted by reason. They, at least the earliest ones, admit neither
the tripartite division of the soul found in Plato nor the dualism
(Platonic and Aristotelian) between the irrational and rational parts
of the soul. For them, the human soul ts essentially rational, so that
man not only participates in the divine life like all other natural
beings, but, endowed as he is with reason, has a greater affinity, a

more intimate kinship with the divine essence. And this divine

23

character of man is all the stronger the more man allows reason to

dominate and realize itself.

Hence that idea about immortality that we find attributed to
Chrysippus: virtuous souls last longer than others, after separation
from the body: according to Cleanthes all souls, but according to
Chrysippus only those of the wise, last until the final combustion, in
which, like all other beings, they will be absorbed into the divine

substance, which alone remains immortal and eternal.

Meanwhile, we know that this divine life of the world and the
entire course of events is governed by a law that admits of no
exceptions: the law of destiny, of the necessary concatenation of
causes and effects. Not even man and his actions can escape this
law: they are part of that chain of events. On the other hand,
however, the Stoics have a keen sense of human activity, personality,
and responsibility, and must somehow acknowledge the freedom of
the will.

According to Chrysippus, we must also consider man's
voluntary actions which presuppose the affirmation of the agent's

own will, his personal and direct participation.

The motion of a cylinder on an inclined plane is explained by
the original impulse and the inclination of the plane on which the
body moves: but this is not enough: the manner and continuation of

the motion also depend on the nature of the cylinder.

Chrysippus' conclusion is that the wicked are wrong to resort to

The end of life

Even for the Stoics, as for all ancient moralists, the moral
problem presents itself in this form: what is the ultimate end of life,
the highest good, and everyone agrees that this end exists, and it is
happiness.

What does happiness consist of? 1 According to Antisthenes,
founder of the Cynic school, happiness consists in virtue, which is
self-sufficient, is the only true good of man, makes the wise man
free from external things and from superfluous needs or appetites
which are an artificial result of culture, makes him live according to
nature, not according to the conventions and foolish opinions of
men: an intuition of an energetic and insolent life which, if it was
attractive for the strength of soul and the exclusive love of virtue
that it preached, was also repellent for the contempt for the science
that it flaunted and the impudence or contempt for propriety of
which its followers boasted.

The most popular type of Cynic remained Diogenes of Sinope,
with his barrel, his lantern and his frank speech: he was called a
slightly mad Socrates.

26

Thus the Stoics reconnect with the Cynics and assimilate their
spirit, tempering and renewing it with a whole metaphysical doctrine

of man and the world.

The Stoics start from this principle: that man, like every living
being, is born with a tendency or instinct; the fundamental one,
which is self-love, the tendency to preserve oneself and one's own

state ot constitution.

The constitution of a being is its nature, the arrangement and
relationship of its component parts. Self-love is for every living
being like a recommendation made by nature for that being's

preservation.

The Stoics add: it is not true what some say (alluding to the
Epicureans) that the first impulse, the fundamental instinct of living
beings, is the pursuit of pleasure. Pleasure is like an addition, a result
of satisfied natural activity, which has achieved its proper goal:
repeating Aristotle's idea, that pleasure is the fulfillment of the act, it
is not the primary end of nature, but something that is added to the

act as youth is its bloom.

What, then, is the ultimate goal of human activity and therefore
the supreme principle of conduct? The Stoics' hallowed formula is

that the goal is to live in accordance with nature.

In fact, living according to nature when speaking of man means

for the Stoics precisely the same as living according to reason, which

vA,

is the essential and guiding part of his constitution, and therefore

becomes the regulator and moderator of his impulses.

If man were to abandon himself to an irrational impulse, he
would act in a manner unconformable to his nature, he would not
preserve his constitution. In a broad sense, this also means living in
conformity with the universal constitution of things, with the
reason that governs the world: the human individual is not
something detached or separated from the whole, but is part of that

whole that is the world, subject to the same law.

The Stoics are not content to say: there is among men a
common reason that allows them to understand each other and to
regulate their lives according to certain norms; but they add: this
reason is a cosmic principle, the active principle of the world, nature
itself in its intimate essence, and human reason is a particular case, a

derivation of this universal reason that governs the world.

From here came the expression "natural law," "law of nature," to

mean moral law, which later became so populat.

Virtue and affections

This life according to reason is virtue, the supreme end, the
perfection of the rational being. And virtue, thus understood, is not
only the most worthy and best, the most beautiful and the most
useful, but it is self-sufficient, the perfect good, the only true good
there is, the only thing in which happiness consists.

All other things are indifferent, and among these indifferent
things are life and death, health and sickness, wealth or poverty, all
external goods and honors, the good or bad opinion that others
have of us.

The wise man, the virtuous man, carries within himself alone
the conditions of his happiness. The worst enemy of reason, that
which is incompatible with virtue and disturbs happiness, are
affections, passions, and attachment to the false goods of life.

Affects are not a part of the soul, the so-called irrational part
opposed to reason, but false judgments, false opinions, value
judgments about deceptive goods. The expressions the Stoics use to
define affect reveal the uncertainty and difficulty arising from their
exclusively intellectualistic interpretation of affective life: affectives
are sometimes called judgments or opinions; sometimes violent and

excessive impulses.

29

Affects are disturbances of the soul, weaknesses, illnesses, and
the wise man does not allow himself to be overcome by them, but
dominates them, fights them, eradicates them from himself,
prevents them by refusing his assent to the objects that would

provoke them.

This is the apathy of the wise man. And it's not that he's

insensitive: he's not denied regulated and reasonable affections.

The wise man, therefore, is a serene and even spirit, untroubled
by unreasonable affections. His wife Xanthippe said of Socrates
that he always had the same expression when he left home in the

morning and when he returned in the evening, and vice versa.

Socrates had already said that virtue is science, based on clear
ideas, and can be taught. In defining virtue, the Stoics now
emphasize its intellectual aspect, considering prudence, the correct
assessment of things and true goods, as the primary virtue. One
cannot be courageous without being just, nor temperate without
being prudent.

Naturally, what is said of virtue must be said of vice. A man who
falls into the water and remains a few inches from the surface

drowns no less than one who falls to the bottom of the sea.

All men can be divided into two classes: the mad and the wise,

and there is nothing in between: whoever does not obey reason is

mad. The wise man is the ideal of perfection, right reason made

human. He is the only one free, rich, happy, equal to the gods.

Preferable and non-preferable things

If the Stoics had remained faithful to their ethical rigor and their
paradoxes, they would have had to give up exercising a broad
practical effectiveness, they would not have been able to have the

following they had.

In fact, when they say that health or illness, wealth or poverty,
and all external things are indifferent to the happiness of the wise,
they mean that they have no moral value in themselves. But this does
not mean that they have no value from the point of view of human
desires.

They themselves teach that man is moved by impulses, the
fundamental one being self-preservation; and therefore he is led to
love and seek things that benefit him and to avoid those that can
harm him. They affirm that reason is destined to be the architect
who gives form, order, and regulation to impulses. This means that
there is a material or substratum of moral life upon which reason is
exercised, and which is made up of human desires and the objects to

which they are directed.

od

Thus, the Stoics, approaching and adapting to common sense,
were led to a series of attenuations of their doctrine, and first of all
to admit a difference in value between the things they had declared
morally indifferent. They distinguish indifferent things into three
classes: preferable, non-preferable, and then those absolutely
indifferent, which have no importance either for the body or the

mind.

The first class includes life, health, physical strength, beauty,
sufficient wealth, good reputation, and especially spiritual qualities
such as skill, intelligence, and a good memory; these are useful
things that are not to be despised. The second class includes the
opposite qualities: poverty, illness, weakness, dishonor, etc. And
then there are things that are truly and absolutely indifferent, such as
having an even or odd number of hairs or spending one coin rather

than another of equal value.

Once they had entered upon this path, the Stoics, as they
distanced themselves from the Cynics, were led to deal with a
multitude of practical questions and even to engage in casuistry to
reconcile their principles with the demands of life. It is folly, says
Chrysippus, to take no account of health or wealth; and he discusses
what are the legitimate and appropriate means for the wise man to
acquire them. And subtle minds as they are, they make distinctions:
the wise man, strictly speaking, lacks nothing, but nevertheless many
things are convenient to him, he uses them, he utilizes them. This

oe

distinction between the good in an absolute sense and relative goods
ot preferable things leads to a second consequence in the evaluation

of human actions and therefore of duties.

From the strict point of view of ethics there should be only two
kinds of actions: virtuous and vicious, but there is also natural life,
with its instincts, needs and interests directed towards those things
which, without being goods, are preferable things.

Thus, between virtues and vices, there are intermediate actions
that the Stoics call expedient. In this way, the Stoics also mitigate the
aspect of moral duty by bringing it into the realm of expediency.

A third concession the Stoics are forced to make to common
sense is to admit improvement, the possibility of progressing in
virtue, thus admitting a third intermediate class between the wise
and the foolish: that of men who progress in virtue. Thus, with
these attenuations and mitigations, the Stoics return to the great
educational tradition that stems from Socrates, always maintaining
their faith in the absolute value of moral good and the ideal they
personify in their sage.

These concessions to common sense are not to be understood
in a chronological sense: they are already found in Zeno and the
eatly Stoics, and are concessions born from the need to adapt the
moral doctrine of the Cynics to the needs of human conscience and

life.

Stoic Ethics

Stoic ethics presents a double tendency or character: on the one
hand, an individualistic tendency in that it proclaims that the wise
man is self-sufficient, independent of all external goods, unshakable
in the consciousness of his virtue and happiness; and on the other
hand, a social and universalistic character in that it teaches the
subordination of the individual to reason, which is the universal law
of the world, from which arises the natural society that exists among

all beings participating in reason.

The first tendency, which links Stoicism to its Cynic origins, is
seen in the specific precepts regarding individual morality and,
above all, in their attempted justification for suicide. They say that
the wise man can reasonably renounce his life either for the benefit
of his country and friends, or in the case of excessive suffering,
bodily injury, or incurable disease. Another source specifies five
reasons of occasions for taking one's life: rendering eminent service,
when forced into a shameful action, poverty, illness, and the
progressive weakening of the spirit. Suicide is, in certain cases, for
the wise man the supreme affirmation of his moral freedom and
virtue, when circumstances beyond his control make it impossible

or difficult for him to live in accordance with his ideal.

34

He does not take his own life because he fears pain or in general
for hedonistic reasons, but because life (which in itself belongs to
the class of indifferent things) no longer offers him the conditions
that make it desirable and usable for good.

‘The Stoic considers the external circumstances that invite him to
kill himself with a supreme act that makes him superior to external

circumstances.

The individualistic tendency of Stoic ethics is counterbalanced
and tempered by the social tendency. Here, Stoicism reveals its
broad aspects. Man is not a singular individual, isolated and
independent from others, but part of an ordered whole. The more
he obeys reason, the freer he is, and reason does not isolate him, but
unites him with other rational beings, subject to the same law. And
reason teaches each individual to subordinate his goals and conduct

to the goals and good of the whole.

The Stoics had the liveliest sense of human solidarity and just as
there is a common and universal reason, so there is a common law

that must regulate human relationships.
They say that what is right is by nature, not by convention.

The idea of natural law, or rational law, which dominated
European thought for so many centuries, was developed by the
Stoics. Law is founded on reason: the sharing of reason, this kind of

rational kinship between men, is the foundation of justice and social

community. Natural law emanates from divine reason, from cosmic

reason; it is this same reason that takes the form of command.

We have no duties or legal relationships with animals, but we do
with humans, with all humans. The Stoics do not propose the
abolition of slavery, but they sense its iniquity, proclaiming with
rationality the dignity of the person and the equality of all men.

They look not so much at the actual inequalities that exist among
men, but at the use each makes of his faculties in whatever
condition he finds himself, and they know that a slave can be free, as
Epictetus was, while many free men are in reality slaves. And after or
together with justice, benevolence. Nature and reason desire that
man be a friend to man and desire to be loved by him. These are the

two truly cardinal virtues of social life: justice and charity.

The Stoics say excellent things about particular societies, about
the friendship that they admit and celebrate especially among the
wise, about the family that they want pure and founded on affection,
and also about the State, which the wise man can concern himself
with when he can do good and there is no reasonable impediment

that distracts him from political life.

The society they speak of is the universal society of all rational
beings, for which there are no frontiers or distinctions between
Greeks and barbarians, or historical or national differences. This is

the so-called Stoic cosmopolitanism.

The same moral and religious inspiration, the idea that man's

If we look at Stoicism itself, at its origins, what is characteristic
of it is the identification it places between nature and reason,

between happiness and virtue.

The Stoics promise man happiness provided he seeks it in virtue
and conceive of virtue as living according to nature or reason,

because nature itself is reason.

Hence the difficulties that beset the system. The Stoic ideal of
the sage has an artificial character not because it is too perfect (a
moral ideal can never be perfect enough) but because it is conceived

outside the conditions of life.

Man is not only reason, but also feeling and passion, and the
Stoics would like to eliminate this entire irrational part of the soul
and reduce it to reason. One might ask: can this ideal ever become a
reality? If we admit, with the Stoics themselves, that this ideal never

existed, it can never become reality; the entire system founded on

The Stoics believe they are providing a metaphysical foundation
