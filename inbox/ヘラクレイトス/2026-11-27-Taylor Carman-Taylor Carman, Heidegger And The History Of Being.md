Abstract: In the 1920s, Heidegger’s philosophical work centered on
the question concerning the meaning of being. What does it mean

(for anything at all) to be? By about 1936, his thoughts had moved to
a different question concerning what he now called the truth of being,
by which he meant the manifestation – or event (Ereignis) – of being as

such, with whatever meaning it might have in different epochs of Western
history. The “history of being” is the history of the human understandings

of being that have grounded Western thought and culture from
Greek antiquity, exemplified especially in ancient philosophy and

literature, to the modern age, which is defined by the rise of experimental

science and machine technology. It has always been the hidden task of

great thinkers, Heidegger argues, to articulate those understandings
of being and to bear witness to the unspoken – indeed, unthought –

happening of the truth of being.

Keywords: Heidegger, metaphysics, technology, Plato, Descartes

Preface

Heidegger’s conception and elaboration of a history of being
(Seinsgeschichte) belongs entirely to his later work.1 Exactly when the
Early Heidegger became the Later Heidegger is a matter of scholarly
debate, and while the “turn” (Kehre), as it is called, from the one to the
other did not happen overnight, there are good reasons to assign the most
important and enduring changes in his thinking to the years 1935–1938. It
was then that Heidegger began working out his interconnected ideas of the
history of being, the forgetting of being, the truth of being, the overcoming
of metaphysics, and finally Ereignis.

Those ideas, which emerged in the years between his failed rectorship

at Freiburg University in 1933–1934 and the outbreak of war in 1939,
were the crystallization of a movement in Heidegger’s thought away from
the individual-oriented existential phenomenology of his magnum opus
of 1927, Being and Time, toward broader cultural and historical topics –
especially art, science, poetry, and technology – which preoccupied him in
the decades that followed.

Heidegger’s history of being can sometimes just sound like the his-

tory of philosophy, with special attention to the Presocratics and meta-
physical thinking, which began with Plato and culminated in Nietzsche’s
pronouncement of the death of God. Heidegger reads the metaphys-
ical tradition against itself, or against its own self-understanding, but
he remains focused on what are widely regarded as the great works of
Western philosophy.

That description of his project, however, is not quite right. It is true

that in his later work Heidegger devotes most of his attention to canon-
ical texts. He does not, however, read them for their explicit doctrines,
their ingenious arguments, or their technical innovations, but instead
obliquely as embodying and expressing deeply felt and widely shared
understandings of being that have constituted a series of grand histori-
cal epochs – classical and Roman antiquity, the Catholic Middle Ages,
and finally modernity (beginning with Descartes). The understanding of
being that underlies and unifies an age, he insists, emerges not in the
manifest content of philosophical propositions or theories but between
the lines of what the great thinkers of the tradition have written and said.
More important than what such thinkers say is what they do not say (or

1 The Truth of Being

Heidegger’s philosophical thought revolves almost entirely around what
he calls “the question of being” (die Seinsfrage). In its most concise form,
the question can be posed (in English) in three words, What is being? The
question is not, What is there? – that is, what exists and what doesn’t – but
rather, What does it mean (for anything) to be? The question at the center
of Being and Time, Heidegger’s early magnum opus, is thus, What is the
meaning of being? (SZ 2ff). By the late 1930s, however, Heidegger’s think-
ing had moved from the question concerning the meaning of being to what
he called the question of the truth of being. What is the difference?

To ask about the meaning of being (what it means for something to

be) is to ask what it is for entities (things that are) to “show up” or man-
ifest themselves. The question concerning the meaning of being is thus a
question concerning entities in their being. Now because (as we shall see
in §4) Heidegger insisted that truth (in Greek, ἀλήθεια) originally meant
disclosedness or unconcealment (Unverborgenheit) rather than correctness
or accuracy, the question concerning the meaning of being is equivalent to
the question concerning the truth of entities, that is, how entities show up
or manifest themselves.2

One might object to the suggestion that the meaning of being is just

what it means for things to “show up” or manifest themselves. “Showing
up,” after all, means showing up to or for someone, which sounds like
something merely subjective, whereas being is surely objective and
entirely independent of anyone’s experience or understanding of it. But

But do entities always show up or manifest in the same fundamental

Copernican astronomy the sun and the moon were planets (wandering
stars), but the Earth was not and could not possibly have been. And

entities (not just gods but angels and demons), monotheism reconceived

beings, who had been “mortals” for the Greeks, became “creatures” for

secularized modernity. Negative numbers existed in ancient Chinese
mathematics, whereas until the sixteenth century they remained a kind
of metaphysical embarrassment to European mathematicians, for whom
the very idea was as absurd as the idea of a horse having a negative
number of legs.

Notice that these historic changes in the fundamental ways in which

entities do (and can) manifest themselves were not mere changes in the
qualities or features entities were supposed to have, but transformations
in what it was for entities to be (or not to be). They were changes not in the
entities themselves but in the intelligibility of their ways of being. A weight
suspended and swinging from a pivot can change “ontically,” as Heidegger
says, by slowing down and coming to a rest. But it can change ontologi-
cally – that is, in its being – by migrating from the world of Aristotelian
physics, in which it is a thing being violently pulled away from its natural

This is the idea that motivates Heidegger’s concept of a history of

being. Not only do entities change, but being itself – that is, what it is
to be – changes from one age to another. There is not just one constant,
historically universal meaning of being, but many meanings, coming and
going, appearing and disappearing. But notice, to say that “there are”
many meanings or ways of being is to say that there is something – namely
being – that itself has different ways of manifesting itself. The meaning of
being is the way in which entities show up as what they are: the meaning
of being is the truth of entities. What it means to be defines or determines
how entities can (and cannot) be manifest. But being itself also shows
up or manifests itself differently in different times and places. And just
as being is what entities (so to speak) “have in common,” so too differ-
ent manifestations of being must be different manifestations of something
that remains (in some sense) the same. That being itself is showing up
at all – which is to say, that the unconcealment of anything as such is
happening, with whatever meaning it might have – is the truth of being, or
what Heidegger also calls Ereignis.7

Heidegger’s idea of a history of being, then, is the idea that what changes

in human history is not just things but the ways in which things can (and
cannot) show up for anyone in their being. For a history of being to be
more than just a chronicle of heterogenous or incommensurable ways in
which entities in different cultures have been organized and interpreted,
there must be a thread running through them, as it were, tying them
together as belonging to a single history. A putative history of “secretar-
ies” that included government officials, typists, and foldout desks could
fairly be dismissed as having no single, coherent subject matter. It would
be a list of things but not a history of anything. An interior minister and a

2 The Forgetting of Being

This history of the meanings (hence also of the truth) of being is thus the
history of various understandings of what it means to be. Understandings
of being have expressed themselves in artistic, literary, religious, polit-
ical, and intellectual works of all kinds – in ancient Greek poetry and
philosophy, in Roman law, in medieval theology, and in modern science
and technology. Each of these historical contexts of meaning is what
Heidegger calls a world. A world, constituted by an understanding of
being, determines what kinds of entities can and cannot show up in it.9
What holds the series of worlds together as a series – that is, what con-
stitutes their belonging together as instances of the same kind of event
or occurrence (Ereignis) – is that in each of them, notwithstanding their
differences, being as such makes an appearance (shows up) with some
particular, more or less unified, meaning.

Understandings of being constitute worlds by specifying not only what

it means for anything to exist but also what fundamental kinds of things
there are and can (or cannot) be. In Western philosophy since Plato,
understandings of being have therefore exhibited two aspects: in addi-
tion to existing, entities are also always something or other; in addition
to their that-being (Daß-sein), they also have a what-being (Was-sein).10
Plato’s concept of form (εἶδος) or idea (ἰδέα) is the first explicit articula-
tion of the what character of entities (essence) in sharp contrast to the
that of being (existence). A form is the eternal, unchanging whatness of a

with increasing intensity up to and including Hegel and Nietzsche. For

this, after all, is what has traditionally distinguished philosophy from both
common sense and the sciences. Metaphysics is a priori in relation to the

3 Before Metaphysics

Metaphysics, according to Heidegger – as he came to use that word in
his later works (after 1935) – began with Plato, whose concept of form or

as a watershed not only in the history of philosophy but also in the history

Socrates but the Presocratics, he insisted, who marked the true beginning

wrong, but it is misleading. Our word “physics” derives from φύσις, but
the two terms belong to such completely different discursive contexts as to
have virtually nothing in common ontologically. They belong to different
understandings of being, different epochs in the history of being, different

kinds, natural law, natural ingredients, not to mention physical features,
physical training, or the fundamental laws of physics.

4 Plato’s Concept of Truth

The birth of Greek rationalism in the classical age was an intellectual
and  cultural revolution of extraordinary proportions. For Heidegger,
what was most significant about it was not the variety of particular
artistic or scientific innovations but the shift of metaphysical perspective
that made those innovations possible, especially in the works of Plato
and Aristotle. “The ‘philosophy’ that begins with Plato,” he says, “has
the distinguishing mark of what is later called ‘metaphysics’” (GA9: 141;

Plato did not, of course, invent the distinction between true and false,

nor was he the first philosopher to thematize it. Parmenides, living a cen-
tury earlier, distinguished being from nonbeing, and ἀλήθεια from δόξα,
the latter being nothing but a confusion of the true and the false.53 But
whereas Parmenides’ monism threatened to render entirely meaningless
any reference to the opposite of truth, Plato pronounced the distinction
emphatically and with no apparent methodological scruple. Knowing
what is true is like hitting a bull’s eye, Socrates says in the Theaetetus,
whereas in false judgment, “like a bad archer, you shoot wide of the mark
and miss” (194a). Either you “get it” or you don’t. When you do, you have
what Gestalt psychologists call an “Aha! experience” – that flash of insight
in which you grasp something, be it a mathematical proof or a joke.

The target that a true judgment hits and a false judgment misses is

what Plato calls a form or idea (εἶδος, ἰδέα). Both words derive from εἴδω
(to see), and Plato uses them interchangeably. They are the source (via
Latin) of our words video, idol, and (obviously) idea. They literally mean
something seen. More particularly, they refer to the visible aspect or fac-
ing side of something seen; less literally, they can mean pattern, shape, or
gestalt. Visual observation or contemplation is also the literal meaning of
θεωρία, Plato’s word for the view the intellect has of the forms – and from
which we get theory and theoretical, words virtually synonymous with the
proper content and attitude of traditional philosophy.54

intelligible and the visible.55 What is intelligible is fixed, eternal, and
unchanging; what is visible is constantly fluctuating and unstable, hence
unintelligible. It is tempting to suppose that Platonic forms are (roughly)
what we call concepts – namely, the contents of thoughts, rather than of
perceptions – and that Plato was helping himself to the visual language of
εἶδος, ἰδέα, and θεωρία merely as a metaphor, appealing to our intuitions,
as it were, which tend to be mired in the sensuous, in order thereby to ele-

5 The Scientific Image of the World

The concept of truth as correctness can plausibly be called simply the
scientific concept of truth, since it was in classical Greece that something
like science – or more precisely, the metaphysical idea of a kind of
inquiry that valued correctness above all else, particularly the vaga-
ries of myth, tradition, and convention – emerged in Western culture.
The criterion of correctness, that is, lies at the heart of the very idea
of scientific knowledge, but it also made possible the sophistication of
Roman law, the subtlety and refinement of medieval theology, and even-
tually the extraordinary increase in the rational planning of virtually all
aspects of modern life.

That aspiration to accuracy and precision does not harmonize eas-

ily with other kinds of thought and inquiry that rest fundamentally on
intuition, emotion, judgment, and interpretation. Such nonscientific skills
are constitutive not just of the arts and the humanities but also of the
social sciences, whose objects – namely ourselves – cannot be rendered
intelligible by mathematical and experimental methods. Still, modern
Western (and increasingly global) culture is and remains a predominantly
scientific and technological culture, and science and technology rest on the
valorization of correctness that began with Plato and was then radicalized
in the Scientific Revolution, especially by philosophers like Descartes.

Though it is essentially a reaffirmation of the Platonic idea of cor-

rectness, however, the Cartesian concept of truth is not only that. For
Descartes, truth means more than just grasping an eternal form or idea:
it is the certainty possessed by the subject vis-à-vis objective reality as a
whole, which includes both the visible and the intelligible, the temporal
as well as the timeless. What is true for Descartes is not, as it were, sim-
ply the arrow of thought hitting an external target but rather the “idea”
in the mind of the subject: “there can be no truth or falsity in the strict
sense except in the intellect alone,” he says.56 Put simply, whereas the
Platonic ἰδέα is an external criterion of truth, transcending our grasp of
it, a Cartesian idea is an internal mental representation whose truth lies
solely in its own intrinsic “clarity and distinctness.” For Descartes, and
indeed for the modern understanding of being generally, Heidegger says,
truth is not just correctness but certainty (Gewißheit).

What is essential to the subject-object dichotomy, after all, is precisely

its relationality, namely that everything is interconnected and defined by
its interconnectedness with everything else. In modern physics, Heidegger
says, “the subject-object relation as mere relation first takes precedence
over the object and the subject, and is to be secured as standing reserve”

rendering them knowable, measurable, and essentially susceptible to
organization and manipulation. What is essential to both is the math-

integrated resources for ever-increasing exploitation and growth.

In a word, no. And to see why not, bear in mind both what Heidegger

says on the first page of the “Technology” essay and what he says at its
conclusion. “In what follows we shall be questioning concerning technol-
ogy,” he writes at the outset, “and would like thereby to prepare a free
relation to it.” What is a free relation? One that “opens our existence
(Dasein) to the essence of technology” (GA7: 9; QT 3). And what is the
essence of technology? It is, as we have seen, the “enframing” (Ge-stell)
of everything as standing reserve (GA7: 23; QT 19). As such, the essence
of modern technology “is itself nothing technological” (GA7: 24; QT 20),
that is, “nothing like a machine” (GA7: 27; QT 23). The essence of tech-
nology, that is, lies not in the tools or instruments or even the policies and
procedures that figure prominently and are therefore familiar to us both in
our practices and in our common sense. It lies instead in something deeper
and harder to grasp, namely the understanding of being that underlies and
informs what we normally say and do unreflectively.

What does it mean to prepare a free relation to technology, and then

for that free relation to open our existence to the essence of technology?
The essay would be disappointingly banal, if its aspiration amounted
to nothing more than reconciling us to the material, the machines, the
instruments, the paraphernalia – in a word, the entities – that populate
our technological environment. Simply accommodating ourselves to the
ontic stuff of modern life sounds like a rather mediocre aspiration com-
pared to “opening our existence” to the essence of technology. The essay
might seem dubiously ambitious, however, if its putative aim were to free
us entirely from the technological understanding of being that underlies
and pervades our ordinary self-understanding. Indeed, the very idea that
anyone, individual or group, could effect a wholesale revolution in the
history of being goes against Heidegger’s insistence that human beings
do not – and cannot – single-handedly or collectively, choose or control
or transform their understanding of being by any voluntary effort. When
thinkers happen to stand at the thresholds of such historical changes, he
says, they do so as listeners or witnesses, not instigators of change.

An understanding of being “leads man on the way of a revealing” that

uncovers entities in their that and in their what (GA7: 28; QT 24). Such
a leading is a “sending” (schicken), and an understanding of being is a
“destining” (Geschick). The word Geschick can mean aptitude, expertise,
or skill, but also luck, fortune, or destiny. The first three of those mean-
ings pertain in an obvious way to modern technology, but they are not
what Heidegger has foremost in mind. Neither, however, does he mean
that technology is in any way predetermined, preordained, or necessary;

our health, our productivity, our pleasure, our jobs, our quality of life,
our weight – in short, our entire human potential.

understanding of being that is has somehow been fatally forced upon us?
No, for we can cultivate a free relation to the essence of technology by
receptively attuning ourselves to the way in which we are unthinkingly
attracted and committed a priori to the enframing of everything. And
that means coming to see our understanding of being not as our own
achievement but as something sent to us, as a kind of destiny. Calling
the technological understanding of being a “destiny” does not mean that
it was preordained or imposed upon us by some mysterious alien force,
but rather that we have found ourselves with it, just as we find ourselves
with our family, our home, our native language. We do not choose these
things, and we cannot undo them or replace them retroactively, fashion-

technological character as a way of revealing is revealed along with its
revealing of entities as mere standing reserve: “The danger is the epoch
of being coming to presence as enframing.” Simply put, Heidegger says,
“the danger is being itself” (GA11: 119; QT 43). The danger and the
saving power are both being as such, which is to say the truth of being –
namely, that there is being and that there is an understanding of being
immanent in our technological way of life. Another word for the truth

But what does that phrase mean, and what does Heidegger mean by it?

In ordinary German Ereignis means event, happening, incident, occur-

rence; likewise, the reflexive verb sich ereignen means to come about,
to happen, to occur, to take place. Heidegger insists, however, that by
Ereignis he did not mean “what we normally call some kind of happening
(Geschehnis), an occurrence (Vorkommnis),” that is, something that takes
place in the world at a specifiable time (GA11: 45; ID 36; cf. GA65: 256).
The art of translation always involves balancing our shared understand-
ing of words with what an author means by them. Given Heidegger’s
idiosyncratic (and often obscure) philosophical purposes, it is under-
standable that Ereignis should often be rendered simply as “event.”85 But
while that preserves the conventional definition, it tells us nothing about
what Heidegger is trying to do with the word philosophically.

The best concise interpretation, I believe, is that Heidegger uses

Ereignis as a rough equivalent of what he calls the truth (i.e., the uncon-
cealment) of being, which serves as the abiding condition of the possi-
bility of the various meanings of being that have defined Western history
from Greek antiquity to the present. It is, therefore, the thread that runs
through and holds together his account of the history of being, in rela-
tion to which the historically different understandings of being can all
be said to be understandings of being. This, I think, is what Heidegger
means when he refers in Contributions to “the saying of Ereignis as the
first answer to the question of being” (GA65: 6).
