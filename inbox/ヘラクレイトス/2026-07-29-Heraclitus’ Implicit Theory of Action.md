## Abstract

I argue that Heraclitus has an implicit theory of action: successful intentional action is dependent on a grasp of logos which is a causal account of all things in the cosmos. My analysis of Heraclitus hinges on the concept of gnōmē, which I will show is a manifestation of the juncture between understanding logos and intentional action. Gnōmē, which allows divine fire to steer ‘everything through everything’ in B41, consists of three parts: the capacity for forming a plan, the plan, and the delivery. I will illustrate how failure to perform successful action comes from a lack of gnōmē by focusing on two parts in particular — delivery and the plan. I will look at the relationship between understanding and control and how (a lack of) physical control impacts the delivery of a plan for the drunken man in B117, as well as how knowledge can influence the suitability of the plan itself by examining Heraclitus’ fragments on religion.

2. Practical Implementation
I outlined a connection between grasping logos and successful intentional action by way of a comparison between the epistemic state of a sleeping-agent, who has no access to logos, and an oblivious-agent, who despite being awake, treats the world as if there were no logos. This outline captures a general trend in Heraclitus where knowledge enables successful intentional action. Now I want to focus on exactly how this relationship manifests, and for this I will introduce a new concept, ‘gnōmē’, which roughly translates to mean ‘successful plan’.

2b. Gnōmē: B41 and B78
B112 and B114 have shown that intellectual functions are expressible as actions, but what is it that makes actions happen? This is the role of gnōmē:
Heraclitus DK B41: εἶναι γὰρ ἓν τὸ σοφόν, ἐπίστασθαι γνώμην, ὁτέη
ἐκυβέρνησε πάντα διὰ πάντων.

Wisdom is one thing, to know gnōmē, which helms all things through all.

B41 associates wisdom with knowledge of gnōmē. What is interesting is the relative clause: gnōmē is what steers things. The steering is described as a type of planned navigation, ἐκυβέρνησε, the helming of a ship, which may be taken as a gnomic aorist to give it an air of omni-temporality.36 Furthermore, the object, πάντα, is steered spatially, as indicated by διά with the genitive; gnōmē has the power to move things, all things. This fragment should be contrasted with the role of logos in B1, in particular, the phrase γινομένων
γὰρ πάντων κατὰ τὸν λόγον. In B1 logos is involved tangentially as an adverbial qualification explaining how things come to be, in B41 gnōmē is the subject of a finite verb, ἐκυβέρνησε and so gnōmē is actively steering and moving things. However, they both have the same scope of influence, πάντα. This reveals an interesting nuance to Heraclitus’ conception of knowledge. There is the passive grasp of logos, the account which describes or prescribes how all things come to be, and there is the active grasp of gnōmē, which controls all things, making them come to be. The question is: does knowledge of gnōmē
allow an agent to act and control things?
Simply put, yes. There are two ways to look at this. One is to think about divine control, and who has access to the wisdom. The other is to look at plural gnōmai, and how plans help less knowledgeable agents control things.
I will look at divine control when I look at successful delivery, and focus on gnōmai now:
