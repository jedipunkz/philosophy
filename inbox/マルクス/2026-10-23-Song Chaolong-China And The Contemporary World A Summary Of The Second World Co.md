KEYWORDS

On May 5, 2018, the bicentenary of the birth of Karl Marx, the Second World Congress on
Marxism was held at Beijing University, the first place in China where Marxism was
studied and discussed. More than 1100 participants were present, including more than
120 international scholars from more than 30 countries on five continents. During the
congress, the scholars took part in lively discussions on the relationship between China
and the contemporary world. Distinguished scholars including Samir Amin, former
Prime Minister of Italy Massimo D’Alema, Özgür Orhangazi from Turkey, Frédéric Boc-
cara from France, Jan Otto Andersson from Finland, Fathi El Koleib from Palestine,
Patrick Theuret from France, David Kotz, Michael Hudson, Dmitry Shlapentokh from
the US, Evita Jiménez from the Philippines, Adam Hersh from the Center for America
Progress, Adham Sayed from Lebanon and the Chinese scholars Li Shenming, Gu Hai-
liang, Yu Hongjun, Ding Xiaoqin, Xu Jiankang, Chen Shuguang and Song Lidan provided
illuminating opinions on the systemic crisis of capitalism in the modern world, as well as
comments and perspectives on Socialism with Chinese Characteristics.

I. Financial Capital and the Crisis of Capitalism in the Modern World

Addressing the systemic crisis of capitalism in the modern world, the scholars discussed
such topics as the financialization of capitalism in contemporary Western countries, the
predatory accumulation of financial capital, the nature of the capitalist crisis in the
West, anti-globalization tendencies in the Western world, the new directions of globaliza-
tion in the present century, and so forth.

Analyzing the financialization of capitalism in the contemporary Western world, Pro-
fessor Samir Amin from Egypt noted that present-day monopoly capital is controlled by
financial capital, and that financial capital is controlled in turn by a tiny minority of the
world’s population. For most of the developed countries, the system is not sufficiently
dynamic. Also, the gap between rich and poor cannot be narrowed by any so-called
rational policy, and there is no solution to the internal crisis of capitalism. Financialization
has arisen as a response to the crisis of capital accumulation in capitalist society. With the
gaps and disparities growing, new sectors are needed to absorb the surplus capital, which
leads to financialization. Nevertheless, financialization not only fails to solve the problem
of surplus capital, but also creates a host of new problems. Professor Stavros Mavroudeas
from Greece argued that contemporary capitalism is entirely dependent on financial capi-
tal. He maintained that under these circumstances, economists of all schools, whether
post-Keynesians, Marxists, or from the mainstream of economic science, are all compelled
to use the financialization hypothesis as the key to analyzing capitalist economic reality.
His hypothesis claims that financial capital is completely different from productive capital,
and is able to reconstruct other forms of capital. The dominance of financial capital is rea-
lized through shadow banking. In the context of financialization, the operations of com-
panies are directed toward maximizing the benefit to stockholders. Institutional investors
occupy the leading positions, and financial exploitation becomes the chief mechanism
through which exploitation takes place. Companies focus exclusively on the benefits accru-
ing to stockholders, which requires the managers to pursue short-term stockholder inter-
ests. This type of economic behavior is completely contrary to the long-term strategic
interests and overall development of the economic entities concerned. As financialization
proceeds, household debts come to be included in the operations of the financial industry,
and the middle class as well as the wage-earning class becomes caught in a web of exploi-
tation created by financial institutions such as credit and insurance corporations. The new
structure of financialized capitalism is authoritarian in nature, and this adds to the pro-
blems. Under the conditions of financialization, a major source of crisis phenomena lies
with the opportunistic practices of financial groups, practices which in turn affect the
entire real economy.

A second key feature of modern capitalism is the predatory accumulation of financial
capital. Former Prime Minister of Italy Massimo D’Alema pointed out that the greatest
wealth stays in the hands of the super-rich right from the point where it is generated,
but that the incomes of ordinary people have not increased, which has resulted in growing
social disparities and inequality. In the United States, profits have kept rising. In 1960, the
financial sector accounted for 14% of the total profits of American companies, while in
2008 this figure already reached 39%. Some 44% of the world’s wealth is owned by less
than 1% of its population, while 70% of the population holds only 3%. Today’s world
appears ever further from dealing with the antagonisms that result. The United States is
now resorting to protectionist and nationalistic measures to counter global competition.
Around the world, national and religious differences have been unscrupulously exploited,
bringing numerous deadly conflicts. Ozgiir Orhangazi, from Turkey, noted that the pro-
portion of American wealth represented by intangible assets such as copyrights, trade-
marks and patents has increased, feeding the growth of monopoly and helping to
explain why the profitability of most investments is low. The possession of such intangible
assets as patents allows particular enterprises to exercise monopoly powers across whole

industries, and to reap high profits. Ownership of copyrights and trademarks gives com-
panies the power to set prices in what once were competitive markets. In addition, intan-
gible assets become obstacles to market entry, something that is especially true in high-
technology industries. The cost of creating a copyright, for example, may be zero, but
copyrights are of great importance in the American economy, and make up a significant
proportion of asset equity. Companies that invest in intangible assets may achieve domi-
nant positions in the market, enjoying high profitability, though their investments also
tend to fuel stagflation.

Third, scholars addressed the nature of the crisis of Western capitalism. Samir Amin
maintained that we should distinguish between the concepts of crises within capitalism
and the crisis of capitalism. The crisis we are now experiencing is not a crisis within capit-
alism, but the crisis of capitalism. The solution to the crisis is for human beings to sur-
mount capitalism, and this requires overcoming capitalism’s systemic crisis. The
capitalism system has currently entered a long-term recession. This present crisis will
have huge impacts on human beings and their nations and societies, as well as on people’s
psychology. Imperialist countries such as the United States, Japan and the countries of
Western Europe still play leadership roles in the world, but since 2007-2008 they have
undergone a huge economic crisis. Currently, the world is not in an age of peaceful com-
petition, democracy and economic prosperity, but of violent conflicts that may, although
nothing is certain, lead to revolution. It was economic crisis that gave rise to Fascism and
that led to the Second World War. After that war, many countries in Asia, Africa and Latin
America became independent precisely through revolution, which represented their
response to economic crisis. Song Chaolong from China argued that a contradiction exists
between the productive logic of financial capital and the nonproductive logic. When a cri-
sis of productive financial capital occurs, Song Chaolong explained, an attempt is made to
solve the crisis through nonproductive accumulation; this, however, cannot solve the cri-
sis, but simply causes a more serious economic crisis to appear. The logic of the productive
accumulation of financial capital means that financial capital accumulates through revolu-
tions in the areas of production, circulation and credit. The nonproductive logic of finan-
cial capital means that financial capital represents an accumulation of parasitism,
deprivation and destructiveness resulting from monopolization of the power to determine
prices and land rents, from speculation in capital markets, and so forth. The inherently
contradictory logic of financial capital constitutes the root cause of economic crisis.

Fourth, participants in the congress discussed anti-globalization trends in the Western
world. The crisis of United States hegemony has plunged the United States into a reaction-
ary opposition to globalization. Song Chaolong contended that neoliberal policies have
provided impetus to the nonproductive accumulation of financial capital, have enlarged
the power of the parasitic group represented by the financial aristocracy, and have
increased the cost of industrial capital, while leading to the transfer of manufacturing
industries to developing countries and aggravating unemployment in the developed
world. These policies thus represent an important reason for the rise of the New Populism
in Europe and America and for the reversal of globalization. The hegemony of the United
States is declining, and the center of world power is gradually shifting. Professor Chen
Shuguang maintained that since 2008, and especially since Trump was elected as US pre-
sident in 2016, anti-globalization tendencies have been growing stronger, and the struggle
between unilateralism and multilateralism has intensified. Researcher Xu Jiankang from

China observed that over the past five or six hundred years of world history hegemonic
powers, when their dominance has been at its height, have advocated liberalization of
trade and investment; then, after their hegemony has declined, they have switched to sup-
porting protectionism. Trump’s policy of exacerbating contradictions in the world is thus
a manifestation of the decline of US hegemony.

Fifth, the scholars exchanged views on the new direction in which globalization is
trending, and the new era it is about to enter. Frédéric Boccara from France argued
that we need new systems in order to overcome financial problems. Human society
needs a new international direction, in place of the one established by the United States.
The crisis has brought about a transformation of the political and economic system, a cri-
sis that no government has so far resolved; in these circumstances, it is up to the world’s
communist parties to find answers. Li Shenming from China maintained that capitalism
has gone through three stages, the first of them being the era of commercial capitalism, the
next the era of industrial capitalism, and the third that of financial capital. Humanity is
now living in the era of financial imperialism, described by Lenin as parasitic and decadent
capitalism. In the next twenty or thirty years, the world will enter a period of great turbu-
lence and reorganization, even a revolution. Massimo D’Alema pointed out that the finan-
cial and economic crisis of 2007-2008 exposed the conflicts and vulnerabilities of
uncontrolled globalization. Many people began to adopt a skeptical attitude toward globa-
lization, and nationalists as well as other conservatives gained new audiences. The crisis of
neoliberal capitalism has led ultimately to a right-wing solution, demonstrated through the
actions taken by President Trump, and this may open a dangerous epoch full of conflicts
and tension, that may eventually result in a new cold war. Samir Amin noted that dialogue
on the basis of equality does not exist in the age of globalization. Inequality in society, the
gap between rich and poor, results in the rise of oligarchic politics. The media too are con-
trolled by capital, and have become the mouthpieces of financial oligarchies. The crises
also result in changes to the nature of imperialism. There is an imperialist group globally,
which is the collective imperialism of the United States, Japan and Europe.

ll. The Dysfunction of Neoliberal Ideology and Politics

In reflecting on and criticizing neoliberal ideology and politics, the scholars addressed
such topics as the masking by neoliberalism of the logic of capital; its aggravation of
the inherent contradictions of capitalism; the bankruptcy of the neoliberal myth of the
middle class; the effect of neoliberalism in plunging Western democracy into crisis; the
falseness of the neoliberal concept of the ultimate pattern of human history, and so forth.

Taking up the issue of the masking by neoliberalism of the logic of capital, Aleksandr
Buzgalin from Russia first suggested that from the viewpoint of liberalism, there are no
poor people in the world, only lazy ones. If you are poor, it is because you do not work
hard enough; either you do not want to be employed, or you do not want to start your
own business. In Russia, there are hundreds of thousands of ordinary teachers who
work in poor villages. They may earn only a few dollars a month, but they work very
hard. Meanwhile, there are rich people who commit numerous crimes. What accounts
for such a discrepancy? After the dissolution of the Soviet Union, and the restoration of
capitalism, the crime rate in Russia reached vast proportions. These facts cannot be
explained by the hypotheses of liberalism. Samir Amin observed that capital systematically

depoliticizes human beings; it makes them believe they are free, while they are just con-
sumers, not citizens or producers, which amounts to a flexible fascism. The reason why
it may be described as flexible fascism is that it does not need people to support it volun-
tarily, but merely to accept it passively.

Second, the participants in the congress explored the ways in which neoliberalism has
ageravated the inherent contradictions of capitalism. Massimo D’Alema pointed out that
from the end of the Second World War until the 1980s, European countries applied the
policies of Keynes and social democracy, with the effects including wealth redistribution
and gradual reductions in social inequality. Economic development was supported mainly
by investment and innovation, and this was beneficial for improving people’s living stan-
dards and promoting social mobility. In the neoliberal age of deregulation and globaliza-
tion, the social model has come to center on the redistribution of accumulated wealth. This
model is dominated by financial speculation. Under neoliberalism, finance is no longer a
tool serving the development of productivity, but instead leads to distorted development.
Financial speculation forces enterprises to accept the logic of short-term profits, which
makes it very difficult to plan for medium and long-term development. Increases in the
money supply do not lead to higher investment, and especially, do not generate strategic
investment. Speculative finance ultimately undermines the development of productivity
and of the real economy, thus aggravating the conflict and breaking the cycle between
economic growth and income redistribution. The shift of wealth from workers to the
financial oligarchy has had an impact on demand, lowering the consumption levels of
workers in America and Europe. To support demand, financial capital resorts to bolstering
consumption through low-interest credit policies; as a result, the debts of households in
the United States have increased abruptly, and financial bubbles have repeatedly arisen.
These bubbles eventually collapse, with dire consequences not only for the banks, but
also for the real economy. These were the circumstances that surrounded the financial cri-
sis of 2008.

Third, the scholars discussed the bankruptcy of neoliberalism’s myth of the middle
class. Liberalism, as a mechanism for its support, has nurtured the fantasy that the liberal
policy system will bring about a middle class society, and that the middle class is the foun-
dation of Western social prosperity, political democracy and liberal values. But in reality,
the rule of financial capital leads to social differentiation and the decline of the middle
class. Professor Ding Xiaoqin from China pointed out that since the financial crisis in
the United States, pensions in Western countries have been reduced to various degrees,
education funding has been cut, and university tuition and fees have risen faster than
the CPI (consumer price index) over the same period. All of these moves have directly
worsened the circumstances of the middle class. Song Lidan from China noted that
since the beginning of the great recession in 2008, a crisis of the middle class has emerged,
and that more than 90% of the people who have applied for bankruptcy in the United
States are from the so-called middle class. The gap between rich and poor has widened.
Nordic countries are often considered the most equal in the world, but more than 50%
of their national wealth belongs to the wealthiest 10%. In most European countries
since 2008, the proportion of wealth owned by the poorer half of the population has
been less than 10%, and generally not more than 5%. In 2014, the wealth of the 85 richest
people in the world was equal to the total wealth of the 3.5 billion who made up the poorer
half of the world’s population. The middle and lower classes have become poorer, and the

rule of financial capital makes the lives of the middle class almost unbearable. In the US,
69% of adults in 2016 had less than 1000 dollars in savings. The social structure of con-
temporary capitalist society still resembles a pyramid.

Fourth, participants examined the ways in which neoliberalism has plunged Western
democracy into crisis. Jan Otto Andersson from Finland explained that through globaliza-
tion, neoliberalism compromises the power of the state. In the modern state, it is necessary
to levy taxes on capital in order to guarantee education and medical treatment. But
because globalization weakens the power of the state, the ability of the country to regulate
crises is greatly reduced. If capital in practice is completely free, it becomes very difficult to
levy appropriate taxes on it, especially since capital is now extremely mobile, moving
rapidly about the whole world. Massimo D’Alema also discussed the equity aspects of
democracy. The issue of inequality is not just limited to the unfair distribution of wealth,
but involves the distribution of knowledge and power. According to Marx, one of the
characteristics of modern society is the conflict between political equality in the abstract
and the actual inequality inherent in the relations of capitalist production. The political
system does not rectify the malfunctions of the market, but instead reinforces them. Finan-
cial groups, including huge technology and data monopolies, control or heavily influence
national opinion, and this ultimately distorts the practice of democracy. Increasingly, the
American political system runs on the basis of one dollar one vote, rather than one person
one vote.

Fifth, scholars noted the falseness of the neoliberal concept of the ultimate pattern of
human history. Massimo D’Alema recalled that in 1992, after the fall of the Berlin Wall
and the end of the Soviet Union, American philosopher Francis Fukuyama published a
book entitled The End of History and the Last Man’, proclaiming that a new era had
dawned and that an irreversible trend had emerged. In the book, Fukuyama argued that
following the downfall of communism in Europe the market would end social conflict,
unifying the world on the basis of the cultural mode represented by liberal Western capit-
alism. This outlook was rooted in the so-called theories propounded in the 1980s by US
President Ronald Reagan and British Prime Minister Margaret Thatcher. Economic glo-
balization then developed on the basis of neoliberalism, with little reflection or criticism.
The world’s left was also influenced by the culture of neoliberal triumphalism, reflected
especially in the proposition of the so-called “third way.” Today’s world, however, has
almost nothing in common with the predictions made by the prophets of neoliberalism.
Fathi El Koleib from Palestine pointed out that while Fukuyama believed that capitalism
would represent the ultimate pattern of history, others definitely do not share this view.
On the one hand, liberal theorists have different interpretations of capitalism, and on
the other, the dissolution of the Soviet Union did not represent the collapse of socialism
itself.

lll. The Contemporary Value of Marxism

In recognizing the value of the theories of Marxism, the scholars argued mainly from such
perspectives as the value of Marx’s Capital’ for understanding capitalist reality in general,
and specifically, for gaining a grasp of financial capital; for assessing the validity of Marx-
ism’s critique of neoliberalism; and for reaffirming the enduring worth of the Marxist the-
ory of socialism.

The first topic to be addressed was the value of Capital for understanding capitalist rea-
lity. Samir Amin emphasized that Marx’s Capital provides a powerful scientific analysis of
capitalist society. Marx was a great thinker, and his writings continue to help us under-
stand the contemporary world. In the first place, Marx’s labor theory of value laid the
foundation for our understanding of modern society. Labor, along with the value of
labor, dominates every aspect of modern life. Second, capital in its pursuit of surplus
value has fallen into a fundamental contradiction: on the one hand, capital seeks con-
stantly to reduce wages, while on the other, it is compelled to try to increase the amount
of its investment. As a result, the system is not durable. Third, the thrust of capital
accumulation is toward creating a global market. Within capitalism’s global expansion,
Marx believed, polarization is an essential feature. In his old age, Marx considered whether
it might be possible to circumvent capitalism in the underdeveloped countries of the East.
These viewpoints of Marx are central to the basic logic required if we are to understand the
modern world. David Harvey was another who emphasized the theoretical value of Capital
for understanding capitalist reality. In Capital, Harvey noted, Marx identified the deep-
rooted structural contradiction of the capitalist system. Capital depends on the purchase
of labor. Because of the pursuit of surplus value, working hours are extended, and the sta-
tus of workers is degraded. Marx paid special attention to surplus value. In the first volume
of Capital, he also expounded on the relationship between human beings and nature. We
depend on nature to provide us with resources, but at the same time there are people who
treat nature destructively. With the development of urbanization, human beings have
implemented a great many transformations, and these are resulting in changes to the cli-
mate and environment. In the second volume of Capital, Marx devoted a great deal of
effort to explaining how value is realized. Important sections of the book discuss pro-
duction time, circulation time and turnover time. The more rapidly capital circulates,
the easier the capitalist concerned finds it to outcompete his or her rivals. The more
rapidly production occurs, the faster the rate of consumption needs to be, which implies
the need to encourage consumerism. The third volume of Capital expounds on the ques-
tion of distribution. In this area, a key issue for a country is the state of its finance system.
Within the finance system there is always a sort of invisible secret power that constantly
adjusts its own strength. Harvey pointed out that it is essential to challenge capital, and not
just to challenge particular aspects of capital, but the whole system. A different solution is
needed, and this is a question for the think tanks to reflect on. Although some environ-
mentalists imagine that environmental problems can be solved without challenging capi-
tal, this is impossible.

Second, the conference participants discussed the value of Marx’s Capital for under-
standing how financial capital functions. Massimo D’Alema pointed out that the analysis
of financial capital does not represent a core element within Marx’s works. It was at a later
stage in his life that Marx became interested in exploring the functions within the capitalist
system of the London financial industry, and began making a deliberate study of finance.
He took up the effect of credit in the third volume of Capital, which he did not complete.
The value of fictitious capital is generated entirely from the capitalization of income, and is
not supported by actual productive activity; in his analysis of fictitious capital, Marx thus
depicts an aspect of capitalism that involves neither production nor the employment of
human labor. In modern times fictitious capital has expanded enormously, and the finan-
cial capital of today is not the same as that described by Marx in the nineteenth century.

Nevertheless, Marx’s intuition helps us to understand the reality of globalization, the lais-
sez-faire nature of capital, and the implications of financial deregulation.

Third, the scholars addressed the validity of Marxism’s critique of neoliberalism. Mas-
simo D’Alema suggested that Marxism offers a unique lens for gaining insight into neo-
liberalism; it is only through Marxism that a globalized world can be interpreted.
Unregulated capitalism is dominated by the mechanisms of the market and profit, and
this leads to unsustainable conflicts, instability and the risk of war. The market cannot
solve the main problem we face in the twenty-first century, which is the unrestricted
and fast-changing nature of economic development. We need to make a comprehensive
analysis of economics and politics, and to overcome the dogmatism of neoliberalism. In
fields where the market cannot provide solutions, the political process will take over its
role, ensuring investment, redistribution, medical care, labor protection and so on. In
addition, international coordination needs to be strengthened. To use a traditional
expression, what we need is a new kind of internationalism. We need to rectify the distor-
tion brought about by international financial capitalism, and to find a reliable alternative.
A regulated globalization is needed, one that can promote harmonious development and
realize the compatibility between humankind and nature. We need to reverse the current
trend in the global economy, and in the meantime we are facing an unprecedentedly chal-
lenging task, one that is tougher than ever. In this period, we should seek to deepen our
understanding of Marxism. Marx lays stress on the importance of politics, and on the
impact that politics makes on the cultural level; he emphasizes the importance of state
authority for revamping the economy and ensuring vigorous growth. These represent
potent intellectual resources for the criticism of neoliberalism.

A fourth topic examined was the value of the Marxist theory of socialism. Samir Amin
observed that the political, economic, psychological and cultural conditions created by
financial capitalism require people to transcend capitalism and advance in the direction
of communism, as a more advanced stage of human society. Many people currently regard
communism as a Utopia, but it will be realized in the future. Massimo D’Alema pointed out
that as foreseen in Capital, the scientific and technological improvements achieved through
capitalism have proved essential for allowing further progress. Capital predicted the auto-
mation of the production process, the end of the basic function of direct labor in wealth pro-
duction, and the growing importance of knowledge and expertise. Artificial intelligence and
robotics can greatly reduce the time needed to produce commodities. However, under the
conditions of capitalism, the growth of productivity benefits only a few people, while the
reduction of labor time leads to the unemployment of millions. The revolution of science
and technology must be utilized to improve people’s quality of life, to reduce and reallocate
work time, and to increase people’s degree of freedom. These effects, of course, will also be
the consequences of political actions. The starting point for Marx was to adopt a critical
methodology in order to expose the contradictions of capitalism. Marx was a great thinker,
who developed an innovative approach to the study of human history, an approach that can
be further applied to interpreting social conflicts that have a significant impact on politics
throughout the world. The interpretation of Marxism should be freed from the bounds of
mechanistic thinking. We should read and frequently reread Marx’s works; only in this
way can we rediscover the richness of his thought, and explore the critical perspective it
offers for today’s society. In the face of numerous challenges, Marxism encourages us to
fight persistently to change reality and create the future.

IV. The Evaluation of and Perspectives for Socialism with Chinese
Characteristics

In making their evaluation of socialism with Chinese characteristics, the scholars mainly
discussed such topics as the productive logic of the socialist system; the potential of the
socialist system for overcoming the logic of unproductive accumulation that is innate to
financial capital; the ability of the socialist system to transcend the logic of financial capital,
its contradictions and its cyclical crises; and the ability of socialism with Chinese charac-
teristics to contribute to the international socialist movement. They noted the value of
socialism with Chinese characteristics as a model of modernization for developing
countries; the competitive strength of socialism with Chinese characteristics in the context
of globalization; and the great importance for China of putting forward the concept of a
shared future community of humankind.

The scholars began by pointing out the productive logic of the socialist system. Pro-
fessor Zhang Hui of Beijing University noted that China’s great achievements have fre-
quently been attributed to the continuing recognition in Marxist economics of the
primary role of the principle of production. When discussing the relationship between
production, distribution, exchange and consumption, Marx always stressed the impor-
tance of production, and in China’s economic strategies, production has always been
assigned the leading place. Especially since 1998, the rate of economic growth in China
has been more than three times the figure for the corresponding period of industrialization
in the United States, and more than twice the rate achieved during Japan’s industrializ-
ation during the 1950s and 1960s. What is the reason for China’s exceptionally rapid
development? It is that it always identifies production as the central principle. Professor
David Kotz argued that during the time of Mao Zedong, China established a mighty foun-
dation for its development after 1978. Professor Lin Yifu of Beijing University maintained
that the failure of socialist states such as the Soviet Union did not result from the essence of
socialism. After the Second World War, the socialist states were surrounded by capitalist
countries, and their development violated the principle of comparative advantage. The
rapid development of China has proved that under the socialist system, if a correct devel-
opment and transition strategy is applied, a socialist country can develop better than a
state that remains under the capitalist system.

Second, the participants in the congress discussed the ability of the socialist system to
overcome the logic of the unproductive accumulation of financial capital. Under the econ-
omic policies of liberalism, the accumulation of financial capital leads to rising prices for
real estate, to the prevalence of stock-market speculation and to the increasing strength of
the parasitic stratum. Only under the socialist system can we solve the problem of the
unproductive accumulation of financial capital. Samir Amin pointed out that although
China has experienced many dilemmas over the past 70 years, the peasantry, the proletar-
iat and the middle class have formed a coalition based on the non-commercialization of
land. China is under pressure from financial globalization, and international financial

V. The Work of the Compilation and Research Center of The Documents of
Marxism

The theme of the third sub-forum of the Second World Congress on Marxism was the
compilation and study of The Documents of Marxism. The latter is the name applied to
a major project under way at Beijing University. On May 2, when General Secretary of
the Communist Party of China Xi Jinping visited the School of Marxism at Beijing Uni-
versity, he remained for some time outside the offices of the project, and bestowed gener-
ous praise on its work.

Professor Gu Hailiang, Director of the Compilation and Research Center of The Docu-
ments of Marxism at Beijing University, explained how the Manifesto of the Communist
Party” was initially disseminated in China. The name of Marx was first heard in China
around 1899, and his portrait was first seen in the country in 1907, when Chinese auth-
orities selected 60 European statesmen, military figures, natural scientists and social scien-
tists, including Marx, for particular study. The earliest dissemination of the Manifesto of
the Communist Party occurred at Beijing University, and can be traced to 1919 when Hu
Shi, a student of the university, translated the first chapter. Under pressure, and fearing
that he would not be permitted to graduate, Hu Shi refrained from translating the sub-
sequent chapters.

Chen Hongjuan, Associate Professor at the School of Marxism of East China Normal
University, outlined to participants in the sub-forum the mode in which the Manifesto of
the Communist Party appeared in China during those initial years. A total of 17 articles,
dating from between 1899 and 1911, have been found that quote from the Manifesto of the
Communist Party, with the translated text amounting to only a few words. These early
references are to editions in Japanese, English and French. The translators held a wide
range of beliefs, and showed no concern for the inherent logic of the Manifesto. The target
audience consisted mainly of intellectuals, and the translated passages were in classical
Chinese, which the general public found difficult to understand.

Song Chaolong expanded on the process through which Marx’s ideas entered China. In
the final decades of the Qing Dynasty, increasing numbers of people in China were

becoming familiar with elements of Western learning. The Christian Literature Society,
composed of Western missionaries and open-minded Chinese students of Confucianism,
played an important role. At the same time, Marxist concepts were being introduced to the
country. In 1909 a book entitled Modern Intellectual Development’, based on various chap-
ters of Hector Macpherson’s A Century of Intellectual Development? was published by the
Commercial Press in Shanghai. Translated by Cornaby, a British missionary, and co-
proofread by Xu Weidai and Cao Cenghan, the book ended with an introduction to
Marx’s economic theories and views on socialism. Features of the book included the fol-
lowing: (1) It elaborated Marxist theory as an aspect of the history of development of mod-
ern Western thought; (2) It introduced Marxism from a position of conservative liberalism
that included support for constitutional monarchy; (3) It introduced Marxism as one
element within a political school that also included anarchism and radical utopianism;
(4) It introduced Marxism selectively, with the focus on a critical introduction to the
labor theory of value and to the proposal for the equalization of wealth; (5) It sought to
provide theoretical support and strategic encouragement for a non-radical revolution cen-
tered on a constitutional monarchy; (6) It was haphazard and uncertain in its translation
of the basic conceptual terminology of Marxist theory. Although the book thus contained
many flaws, it provided a relatively intensive theoretical introduction to Marxist theory for
mainstream intellectuals and publishing agencies during the period of constitutionalism at
the end of the Qing Dynasty. Reading it today yields important clues, dimensions and
points of view for studying the dissemination of Marxism in China.

Professor Wang Baoxiao of Shaanxi Normal University remarked that there are numer-
ous works that, like The Documents of Marxism, tell of the early period of the dissemina-
tion of the Marxist classics in China. By the 1920s, these classics were already present in
the country. As related by Professor Wang, scholars investigating the field had expected to
find that the earlier the dates of publication of Marxist texts in China, the easier the work
of later researchers would be. Instead, the situation proved more complex. Around 100
years ago, it was discovered, Marxism was dispersed to the Chinese people in various tex-
tual forms. In fact, more than 40 books written during this period touched on Marxism,
though mostly while addressing different topics.

As part of The Documents of Marxism project, Wang Lei, a lecturer at the School of
Marxism of Nanjing Normal University, has taken part in the editing of History of
Countries.° A textbook published originally in 1902, History of Countries was a positive
force in the dissemination of Marxism. Today, we think of the book as simplistic and
even misleading, but it still deserves to be valued as a pioneer. The publication of His-
tory of Countries, its dissemination in China and its effect on young students and intel-
lectuals as a textbook lent significant impetus to the early spread of Marxism in China.
As we seek to deepen the impact of Marxism today, we need to broaden our vision to
include long-term, wide-ranging studies of the early dissemination of Marx’s ideas.
During earlier studies in this field, scholars tended to discuss the background to the
early dissemination of Marxism in China on the basis of the New Cultural Movement,
the October Revolution, the May 4 Movement and other political developments. In
recent times, scholars have increasingly insisted on the method of long-term historical
research as a way of understanding the dissemination of Marxism over more than a cen-
tury of the Chinese history. The compiling of The Documents of Marxism is in line with
this way of thinking.

Xu Yang, Deputy Director of the Department of Compilation and Translation of the
Works of Marx, Engels, Lenin and Stalin in the Central Compilation and Translation
Bureau, addressed the sub-forum on the compilation and translation in China of Das
Kapital and its manuscripts. The title of the book Das Kapital is known to have been
first mentioned in China in 1899. Chinese scholars are said to have translated the first
volume in 1902, but no evidence is available to prove this claim. In the 1920s it was poss-
ible to read, in Chinese, the preface to the first volume. During the 1930s scholars trans-
lated Das Kapital, with the sublime goals of seeking truth and helping the nation survive.
The translators worked under difficult conditions at a time when the whole of society was
shrouded by white terror, and while suffering from a shortage of materials. They set them-
selves a formidable task. From the very beginning they worked from the German version
of Das Kapital, while also referring to the English and Japanese versions. They translated
rapidly, and the entire work had been published by March 1938. Theories of Surplus Value
was published in 1949. The translation of Das Kapital was a remarkable contribution made
to the field of ideas and culture by Chinese revolutionary intellectuals, who gave their wis-
dom, righteous ardour and even lives for the liberation of the nation and its people.

Xu Wenxing, lecturer at the School of Marxism of the Beijing University of Chemical
Technology, discussed the work of Shi Renrong, a translator from Yuyao, Zhejiang pro-
vince. Shi played an important role in the early dissemination in China of the works of
Friedrich Engels, translating Engels’s socialist works into classical Chinese. In Shi’s ver-
sion, these works by Engels took on obvious characteristics of Chinese culture both in ter-
minology and content. In translating passages by Engels dealing with collusion between
officials and businessmen, with class contradictions, and with conflicts between countries,
Shi exercised his subjective initiative as a translator, adding content related to China’s his-
torical background. In some cases, his purpose in adding content was to counter the pro-
paganda of anarchism. Shis translating epitomizes various characteristics of the
dissemination of Marxism in China in the early twentieth century. It undoubtedly had
limitations, being less accurate and less comprehensive, but those shortcomings can
never blot out the contribution it made, or its significance for the early dissemination
of Marxism.

of the Bolshevik Party’, Major Topical Issues in the International Labor Movement'’, and

Marxism only incidentally, and introduced various errors and distortions, their activity

VI. Conclusion

At the Second World Congress on Marxism, more than 120 foreign scholars shared their
viewpoints on the contemporary world and China. Taking a comprehensive view, the con-
gress covered five main topics: financial capital and the crisis of capitalism in the modern
world; the dysfunction of neoliberal ideology and politics; the contemporary value of
Marxism; the evaluation of and perspectives for Socialism with Chinese Characteristics;
and the work of the Compilation and Research Center of The Documents of Marxism.
The views expressed by the scholars on these topics are enormously meaningful for our
understanding of the modern world.
