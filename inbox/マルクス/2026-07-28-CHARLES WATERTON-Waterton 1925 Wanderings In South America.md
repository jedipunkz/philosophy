INTRODUCTION

CHARLES WATERTON, a naturalist distinguished more,
perhaps, by his sprightly pen and salient personality than
by any very outstanding contribution of fact or thought
to the annals of natural history, was the eldest son of
Thomas Waterton, of Walton Hall, in Yorkshire, the
ancestral seat of the family. Here, on the 3rd of June,
1782, he was born, and in the park surrounding the once
‘“‘erand old edifice’’-—pulled down, some time afterwards,
by his father, to make way, according to the Rev. J. G.
Wood, for an ugly stone box, like a workhouse—his remains
lie buried.

The original ‘‘most memorable house,’’ thus sadly intro-
duced to us, had been equally distinguished architecturally
and through its historical associations. Cromwell himself
had striven to demolish it, but unsuccessfully, though he
left a cannon-ball, fired by his own hand, in one of its old
fortified gateways, in witness of his intentions, It must
have been tough, this gateway, since even a Thomas
Waterton (most unfortunately head of the family at a
time when George the Third was head of taste) had perforce
(one can hardly conceive of it otherwise) to leave it standing,
though he got rid of everything else—the whole place,
beyond it, from turret to basement, including ‘‘an oak-
panelled hall, ninety feet in length.’’ By far the greater
part of this panelling was burnt, but a little of it, over, was
put to good use by the proprietor, as he no doubt considered,
in making a pigeon-house. This and his digging up a cannon-
ball, to hand down in the family, after he had pulled down
the family mansion, are the principal facts mentioned in
regard to the domiciliary activities of Waterton’s father.
Of his mother it is only recorded that she was the daughter
of Sir Henry Bedingfeld of Oxburgh, in Norfolk.

Waterton’s family was an ancient one. It had played its
part in history, upon the fields both of Agincourt and

As with some other long-descended English families, to
be born a Waterton in the eighteenth or early nineteenth
century was still to be born a Catholic, and, also, as a
corollary, still to suffer for it. Accordingly, during a certain
portion.of Waterton’s life, he was subjected, when in

humiliations and civil disabilities which he never after-
wards forgot. Indeed they were felt and remembered so
bitterly that, as Columbus, sent back in fetters from America,
kept them, after they were struck off, ever about him, and
was finally buried with them, so Waterton, after the Act
of Emancipation, continued to refuse certain honours,
now ready to fall on him, from which, before that tardy
measure of justice, he had been debarred. Two are men-
tioned, and sufficiently show the stedfastness of his char-
acter, being those of the Magistracy and Deputy-Lieutenant-
ship—possibly united. Persecution tends to awake en-
thusiasm in the repressed direction, and it is hardly,
perhaps, to be wondered at that Waterton, a most en-
thusiastic naturalist, but of the pre-evolutionary type,
grew up and continued, during the whole of his life, an
equally enthusiastic Roman Catholic, with political and
all other views in accordance. Indeed, in reading the
accounts we have received of his habits and general course
of life—how he slept on the bare boards of a room that had

neither bed nor carpet, with only. a blanket for covering
and a block of wood for a pillow, “‘retiring”’ (save the mark!)
at eight p.m., getting up at three, every morning, on the
summons of a very, punctual cock—his “‘minute-gun’’—
to pray for an hour in the private chapel adjoining, with
no return to bed, or rather boards, again (but that one can
understand better)—when one considers these things, one

The noble expanse of the park surrounding Walton
Hall, with its lakes, pools and marshes, its stretches of
woodland and general intermingling variety of rural feli-
cities, made a splendid territory for the tastes of a naturalist
to expand in, during these early years of Waterton’s life.
Then came school (at Tudhoe, near Durham), with its
cramping discipline, profusion of birchings and scantiness
of nourishment, leading, on one occasion, as recorded by
the ringleader himself, to a ‘desperate but unhappily
frustrated attempt on the larder. Here, too—much in the

laid the foundation of a later very creditable knowledge
and enjoyment of the ancient, or, at least, the Latin
classics, with quotations from which the Wanderings
in due course appeared besprinkled, but, to the scandal of
natural history, at this epoch, without the Latin name of
any species.

At length, to his lasting benefit and immensely to the
increase of his present happiness, Waterton passed out of
the hands of a flagellating Protestant clergyman into the
abler and more delicately discerning ones of the Fathers
of the Jesuit College _ at ‘Stonyhurst in Lancashire, and
here spent the next four impressionable years of his life,
from fourteen to eighteen. They were English Jesuits,
these, who had been driven out of Liége. Why we are not
told, but it ‘was not, presianelay for lack of educational

Natural history being here the dominant propensity,
this, under a less sternly, not to say savagely repressive
system than that which prevailed at Tudhoe, led, in con-
junction with the enlarging activities of its devotee, to
the usual results of breaking bounds, climbing trees, tres-
passing and other such contraventions of the rules of the
establishment: acts which, albeit, in themselves, innocent,
or, if faults, at most venial ones, yet set a disturbing
example and made trouble in the neighbourhood. The good
Fathers therefore, whilst repressing such youthful exuber-
ance, cleverly provided new material, the exploitation of
which did not lead to these results, but to others not merely
harmless, but useful, and converted Waterton the naturalist
into Waterton the rat-catcher, Waterton the ‘‘foumart,”’
or polecat-destroyer, Waterton the rook-pie-provider, and
so on. In short, it was the Dotheboy’s Hall system upon a
higher plane, or combined, perhaps one should rather say
—for there would seem to be no essential difference—with
benevolence and appreciative regard.

One point, however, is here raised which, curiously
enough, does not seem to have occurred to Waterton
himself (who was delighted with the posts he now occupied)
—I do not mean at the time, for that is understandable,
but even in after-life. He was and remained apparently
insensible to the fact that from natural history to rat-
catching is a derogation, though perhaps, as the first was,
then, and is even now largely conceived of, not a very
great one. That, however, should not have been the view
of the author of the Wanderings. I believe myself, that
such seeming non-perception may be explained, in a very
interesting manner, by reference to the political principles
which Waterton is known to have held throughout his
life, and which, having regard to their violence, we may
suppose that he was purposely nurtured in, or, at least,
had caught up by overhearing, if not through precocious
heredity. Chief amongst these was his hatred of Whiggery,
which had for him two principal and equal embodiments,
the Prince of Orange, namely (“Dutch William” he called
him), and the brown or Hanoverian rat, both of whom, in his

belief, which was fact-proof, had arrived in the same ship from
Hanover. There were no brown rats here, any more than there
had been a Dutch William, before that, by him, regretted
voyage. Now it is at least possible (to give under-statement
its full value) that Waterton’s hatred of this animal, and
the symbolical character which it had in his eyes, blinded
his judgment and made the killing of it assume a high and
holy character, more in the nature of a consecration than
a mere keeping down of vermin. Of this strong feeling, or
obsession one may Call it, the Jesuits, skilful in playing
on the foibles of character, would not have been slow to take
advantage, more especially as the college—an ancient and
ramshackle building—swarmed with these Whiggish pests.

The Rev. J. G. Wood, whose words (the italics are mine)
at least serve to show that the view here advanced does
not go beyond the evidence in support of it, says in his
biography of Waterton:

Yew was one of his favourite trees, and he was accustomed to
say that it would be perfect if its leaves were only furnished
with spikes sharp enough to keep out the cats, stoats, weasels,
and, his pet abhorrence, the brown rat, which he always called
the Hanoverian rat, and stoutly believed, etc.

—no need to repeat what has just before been said.

I rather fancy [he continues, unconsciously approaching my
conclusion, though he does not quite arrive at it] that the
Hanoverian origin of the brown rat must have been one of
Waterton’s early jokes, and that he gradually came to consider
it as a fact.

But if so, this would only have been through the steady
growth of fanaticism. One does not readily come to con-
sider as facts things unlikely in themselves and void of
all evidence, without the strong stimulus of the wish so to
consider them, and it is difficult to suppose that such a
man as Waterton, endowed with a real sense of humour,
and having keen powers of observation, would all his life
have kept on saying something which, as a joke, merely,
must have palled on all his friends and probably lost him
not a few. But if his heart was in it, if to him, even from
the beginning, it was no joke, but rather a fact of atrocity
which, as being always true, became at last a chronic

No need—any more than “‘to throw a perfume on the
violet’’—to italicise here, since it is sufficiently plain, by
the form of expression and words chosen, that “more is
meant than meets the ear.’’ I, at any rate, cannot doubt
that as Cleopatra, in fishing, identified each “tawny-finned
prey”’ that she drew up, with her more important one,
Antony, murmuring to it: ‘““Ah, ha! you’re caught,’ so,

of the youthful Waterton, ‘‘ Dutch William” was also dis-
posed of. Indeed, this must have been the case simply by
association of ideas, and, equally of necessity, the linked
image would have loomed larger.

Whatever one may think of the policy adopted by the
Jesuit Fathers of Stonyhurst to meet the embarrassing
case of their having a naturalist amongst them—lI condemn
it myself unsparingly, both as a body- and soul-killing one
—there can be no doubt that it was as well-intended on
their part as it was so received by their pupil, whose love
and reverence they entirely won, and, in many a better
way, deserved, over and above the main benefit conferred
upon him of an (otherwise) good moral and scholarly
education. Indeed, prospectively they may be said to have
saved his life, for he went from them a teetotaller, and so
severe were some of the illnesses he contracted in the course
of his travels, and so heroic his personal treatment of them
—a Dr. Sangrado-like combination of the lancet and

alliance, would have proved fatal. The promise, however,
made by him to one of the teachers, never to drink wine
or spirits, was adhered to all his life, and a glass of beer,

After leaving Stonyhurst, Waterton spent a year at
home, hunted there, went off to Spain, acquired Spanish,
absorbed Don Quixote, saw the Barbary apes at Gibraltar,
had the “black-vomit” at Malaya, sailed from that port
in spite of embargos, and, at the risk of life-long imprison-
ment, took a cold in the Channel, was seriously ill again
at Walton, but rallied, and hunted as before. Then came
the “‘tide”’ in his “affairs” which led on, at the end of eight
years, if not to “fortune,” which he had by birth (I at
least hold that he has who can spend £10,000 on a wall),
at any rate to fame and what goes with it. This came about
through his being employed by his father and uncle to look
after estates in Demerara, of which—as presumably, also,
of the slaves who worked them—they were the joint-owners.
At the end of the above-mentioned period, however, both
had passed onward to where the slave and the master—
or, rather, all the different kinds of slaves that together
make up the genus homo—are, or are supposed to be, equal.
The estates, thereupon, determined, the former super-
intendent of them also determined, on his part, to travel,
and, as a result of doing so, stepped, shortly afterwards,
from the position of being nobody into that of the world-
(or some considerable part of it) famous author of Wander-
ings 1n South America. This was, and has remained, his
magnum opus, and he deserved the reputation, enlivened
with a spray of hostility which it brought him. For if we
can forget that one odd failing which has been incidentally
touched upon—and most of us do forget it now—it is a
uniquely pleasing work, a work of charm, of graceful,
flowing picturesque narrative and description, enhanced
by touches of individuality and a sometimes discursive
or allusive, at other times an apostrophic, mode of writing,
which together create an atmosphere and form a style—
not much more like Sterne’s, J think, than Sterne’s is
like Waterton’s.

Nor is it only the ‘‘kind” or ‘“‘courteous reader” or

“traveller” who is thus frequently—pleasantly too—
addressed. For the nonce, a bird or quadruped is, also, or
will itself make bold to say its say. On the general warp
and woof of such a tapestry as this, easy and accurate
pictures ‘‘in little’? of the more salient ordinary actions
of this or that species—birds for the most part—‘‘stick
fiery’’—stay, that does not quite give it—'‘stick softly-
fiery off’’; for there is something in Waterton’s manner
of writing that beams, rather than flashes, where it lightens.
It is with a shaded pencil rather than a pen that he writes,
and sometimes with an indelible one. True, as hinted, such
pencillings do not, as a rule, present us with much at a
time, only with just what is there at the moment, without
waiting for more, the impression being largely conveyed
that the portrayer is more seriously occupied in walking
—or floating—on and on through the forest, though he
may occasionally loiter for a little, just to see what he
tells us. Such eye-lightings are scarcely to be called
“studies.”

Exceptis excipiendis, Waterton had little time really
to study the habits of the species he met with—it would
have involved too much stopping—nor did he think very
deeply concerning them—it was not the thinking time,
then, with naturalists—but he saw appreciatively and
precisely. To me, indeed, the Wanderings seems not so
much a work of natural history as a literary work with
that theme, amongst others, for a background, lightly and
discontinuously touched upon. It is a pomp and pageant
of the tropical forest, into the lively procession of which
all enters that it happens to pass—birds, monkeys, snakes,
peccaries, “‘tigers’’ and tapirs, ants and ant-bears falling
in and then out again, with the trees and the rivers, the
savannahs, hills, vistas, Indians and wourali poison—
the account of which last, in the hands of its producers and
users, is excessively interesting.

The exceptions above alluded to have principally, or
at least most importantly, to do with the sloth. This active,
happy creature as we now, thanks to Waterton, know it
(or ought to—there seems a tendency to forget), was famous,
before he ‘“‘came, saw and overcame” authority, for three
great leading deficiencies. It had been so badly thought
out, anatomically, that, through the disproportionate length

of its fore- and hind-limbs, and the absence of soles to its
feet, it could neither stand, walk, nor even, it was whispered,
lie down—at least not comfortably—as a consequence
of which creative blundering, it was both slothful (in
what attitude could not be guessed) and extremely low-
spirited—miserable, indeed. Not that all animal misery had
fallen upon the sloth alone. Amidst her many injustices,
Nature had not consented to such an injustice as that, but
provided (amply indeed) it with fellow-sufferers. For those
were still the days—(between Aristotle and The Origin of
Spectes)—when the relation of structure to habit was not
clearly perceived, when the stilt had to struggle through life,
under the “‘enormous defects”’ of legs much too long and
a neck much too short for it, when the avocet was plagued
with a beak that had maliciously been bent the wrong
way, and the poor proboscis monkey must submit to the
daily inconvenience and indignity of having to hold its
nose when it jumped, for fear it should hook on to the
branches of the tall forest-trees which, as though in derision,
had been immutably assigned to it for a dwelling-place.
These, and a host of others, were all unhappy, but the
sloth, with its general shapelessness and incapacity of
doing anything at all, as it seemed, was a culmination, so
that the “‘elegant”’ (always) but perplexed Buffon, con-
sidering its case and the whole question, was compelled
to ask why should not certain animals have been con-
demned to misery, seeing that nine-tenths of the human
race were? The only scientific ray of comfort in this ‘dark
profound ” was that afflicted species had their Latin names,
just like the other ones, under the narcotic influence of
which, naturalists, as a body, then, were inclined to say
Nunc dimitiis in regard to all other peculiarities and pro-
clivities exhibited by their bearers. Before the classic
label had been definitely chosen and agreed upon, rest-
lessness and irritability, with disputes and pedantic dis-
cussions, prevailed, but, once affixed, all—or almost al]
were satisfied. It descended like night upon them, and
they slept.

Waterton, however—in whose favour the “almost”
was necessary—uninfluenced by the Latin names, as one
not knowing them (for this seems probable), instead of
sleeping, set about seeing things for himself and soon put

matters to rights, in the process of doing which he had the
added enjoyment of profoundly upsetting his second pet
aversion, or ‘‘abhorrence,” the “closet-naturalist.’’ For he
sought out the sloth, and from beneath its Grecian draperies
so to speak—the name in this instance being a Greek one
—produced the naked, real animal, and lo, it was not the
sloth at all! ‘‘Fit fragor,’’ and all over Europe and America
sleep-loving naturalists were disagreeably woken up. For
the real animal was not miserable, nor had it any reason
to be. It could stand, lie, progress, even, quite well, in the
way that it wanted to—there was nothing the matter with
it. True, it had no soles—or even heels—but it didn’t want
them, seeing that its natural posture was hanging back

The armadillo is very common in these forests; he
burrows in the sandhills like a rabbit. As it often takes
a considerable time to dig him out of his hole, it would
be a long and laborious business to attack each hole
indiscriminately without knowing whether the animal

Indians carefully examine the mouth of the hole, and
put a short stick down it. Now if, on introducing the
stick, a number of mosquitos come out, the Indians know
to a certainty that the armadillo is in it: whenever there
are no mosquitos in the hole there is no armadillo. The
Indian having satisfied himself that the armadillo is
there by the mosquitos which come out, he immediately
cuts a long and slender stick and introduces it into the
hole. He carefully observes the line the stick takes, and
then sinks a pit in the sand to catch the end of it: this
done, he puts it farther into the hole, and digs another
pit, and so on, till at last he comes up with the armadillo,
which had been making itself a passage in the sand till
it had exhausted all its strength through pure exertion.
I have been sometimes three-quarters of a day in digging
out one armadillo, and obliged to sink half a dozen pits
seven feet deep before I got up to it. The Indians and
