INTRODUCTION

In the autumn of 1967, progressive people
throughout the world marked the centennial of
the publication of Karl Marx’s immortal work,
Volume I of Capital.

Marx’s associate and friend Frederick Engels
characterised this work as follows: “As long as
there have been capitalists and workers on earth,
no book has appeared which is of such impor-
tance for the workers as the one before us....”!

Capital is the firm foundation of the magnifi-
cent edifice of Marxism. Since its appearance
it has been towering like a cliff blocking the way
to all theories advanced by bourgeois ideologists
in defence of capitalism.

Capital, “the political economy of the working
class in its scientific expression”, signified a rev-
olution in economics, and was a gigantic step
forward in the development of Marxism’s other
components: Marxist philosophy, and the teach-
ing on proletarian revolution and the build-
ing of socialist society.

In Capital, Marx, utilising a vast store of his-
torical and statistical material, examined the
nature of capitalism as a socio-economic forma-
tion, discovered the laws of its development,
proved the transient character of capitalism and
theoretically substantiated the inevitability of its
replacement by a new and higher social system
through revolution. He wrote: “The monopoly
of capital becomes a fetter upon the mode of
production, which has sprung up and flourished
along with, and under it. Centralisation of the
means of production and socialisation of labour
at last reach a point where they become incom-
patible with their capitalist integument. Thus
integument is burst asunder. The knell of cap-
italist private property sounds. The expropriators
are expropriated.””!

Marx exposed the basis of capitalist exploita-
tion and disclosed the nature of class antagonism
as a result of which the class struggle between
the bourgeoisie and the proletariat in capitalist
society ends in victory for the workers and leads
to the abolition of classes and the building of a
new socio-economic formation free of all ex-
ploitation.

In Capital Marx scientifically substantiated the
historic mission of the working class—the builder
of a new, communist society.

The appearance of Capital played a vital role
in assuring the victory of the ideas of scientific
communism over petty-bourgeois socialist theo-
ries and doctrines, over the teaching of Lassalle
in Germany, Proudhonism and anarchism in Lat-
in countries, and reformism in Britain and the
USA, and laid bare reformist illusions and pseu-

do-revolutionary phrasemongering of petty-bour-
geois theoreticians. In his struggle against Narod-
ism and “legal Marxism’, to prove the validity
of Marx’s view concerning the revolutionary
prospects in Russia, Lenin used Marx’s and En-
gels’s major writings, and, first and foremost,
Capital, from which he quoted long excerpts hav-
ing first translated them into Russian. Marx’s
name became especially popular in Russia fol-
lowing the appearance of the Russian edition of
Volume I of Capital.

The publication of Volume I of Capital and
its translation into other languages promoted the
formation of national revolutionary parties of
the working class in many countries.

Marx’s economic teaching is an effective ideo-
logical weapon of the proletariat and its organi-
sations, of all oppressed peoples in their fight
against capitalist exploitation and social inequal-

ity.

This book recapitulates the history of publi-
cation, translation and spread of Volume I of
Capital from the day it was published, September
14, 1867, to 1895, the year Frederick Engels died.
In these years Volume IJ appeared in 17 editions
in eight countries and in nine languages. More-
over, in this period the second and third volumes
and a Russian translation of Volume II of Capital
were published. (See table on p. 10.)

This is the first attempt in Marxist historio-
graphy to deal with these matters in their inter-
relation, and, being a general review, it does not
claim to be an exhaustive study of the subject.

Marx and Engels always devoted much atten-
tion to the publication and translation of Capital
into other languages and to propagandising it in
the press. Work on Volume IJ was still in full
swing when Marx began revising Volume I for
publication in French and when Engels was edit-
ing its English version. Marx willingly advised
Herman Lopatin, one of the Russian translators
of Capital, and in turn took into account the lat-
ter’s remarks. Both Marx and Engels corresponded
extensively with translators, such as Nikolai
Danielson, Joseph Roy and Ludwik Krzywicki,
and gave them expert advice. Engels worked

Engels contributed greatly to the preparation
of the third and fourth editions of Volume I of
Capital for print. Until his dying day he consid-
ered it his most important task to propagandise
Marx’s brilliant work. He frequently reviewed it
for publications of diverse political trends. All
of this is an important part of the biographies of
the founders of Marxism and consequently merits
close study.

Some translations, among them the French and
English, have a long and complicated prehistory
whose numerous peripeteia Marx and Engels fol-
lowed with interest. They directly helped solve
most of the translation problems.

They were also concerned with the form in
which Capital was published. Marx twice con-
sented to have it published in instalments, thus
making its purchase easier for the workers. In
this respect the second German and the first
French editions which came out in instalments,
served as an example for the Polish and Dutch
editions.

Marx and Engels greatly assisted in preparing
abridged versions for publication, and introduced
corrections into manuscripts prepared by Gabriel
Deville, Johann Most, Ferdinand Nieuwenhuis
and others. The abridged editions of Capital,
popularly expounding the subject of the book,
in no small measure helped popularise and spread
Marx’s economic theory.

Of considerable interest are the methods which
were employed in different countries to popula-
rise Capital.

Among those who helped to spread the work
were Russian, French, German, Polish, English

and other translators, publishers and booksellers,
among them Herman Lopatin, Nikolai Daniel-

Brzezinski; Samuel Moore and Edward Aveling;
Otto Meissner, Nikolai Polyakov, Alexander
Cherkesov, Maurice Lachatre and Henri Oriol.
For many of them participation in the publica-
tion of Capital was the first step towards socialism.

Marx’s method of spreading the book by send-
ing author’s copies and separate instalments to
those who wanted to study his economic teach-
ing and bring it to the attention of their fellow
party members proved to be very effective in
conditions of the repressions to which the French
Government resorted following the suppression
of the Paris Commune and during the operation
of the discriminatory legislation against socialists
in Germany.

In view of the political situation at the time,
Marx, Engels and their supporters were not al-
ways in a position to popularise Capital exten-
sively, so they did the next best thing by publish-
ing separate chapters or excerpts in newspapers
in France and Germany as well as in other coun-
tries. The excerpts they selected for publication
dealt with the most urgent problems of the
working-class movement, as, for example, the
length of the working day. This was the theme
of the leaflet issued by the New York section
of the International, which was distributed
in the city in 1871 during a demonstration
for the introduction of an eight-hour working
day.

The propaganda of Capital was international
in character, because it was carried on by the

foremost representatives of the progressive intel-
ligentsia and the international working-class
movement, such as Friedrich Lessner, Friedrich
Sorge and Johann Becker; Russian revolutiona-

Plekhanov; brilliant leaders of German Social-
Democracy Wilhelm Liebknecht, August Bebel,
doctor Ludwig Kugelmann; functionaries of the
French Workers’ Party Paul Lafargue, Jules
Guesde, Edouard Vaillant, Gabriel Deville; Eng-
lish socialists Eleanor and Edward Aveling, Har-
ry Quelch and William Morris.

An important role in spreading Capital was
played by propaganda conducted in the form of
special lectures and reports delivered by Social-
Democrats in different countries. The text of these
lectures was often handed over to Marx for ap-
proval. This intensive propaganda paved the way
for spreading the book among progressive work-
ers and intellectuals.

The distribution of Capital was conducted in
various forms : the organisation of advance sub-
scriptions to Capital in Geneva, Paris and War-
saw; the printing of extra copies of separate in-
stalments of the second German edition and the
spread of dozens of reprints of reviews of the
book in Germany; the polemic aroused by the
book in Russia, which helped disseminate Marx’s
economic theory.

Thus, a diversity of ways and opportunities for
acquainting the workers with Marxist political
economy was employed.

The spread of Capital was closely bound up
with the growth of the international working-
class and socialist movement and depended on
its peculiarities in each country. Nevertheless,
despite the different historical conditions, the

14

appearance of Capital in one or another language
always stimulated the working-class and libera-
tion movements.

The information this book gives about the
editions of Capital published during the lifetime
of Marx and Engels illustrates Lenin’s thesis that
the victory of Marxism in the working-class move-
ment had in the main been completed by the
1890s. He wrote: “Even in the Latin countries,
where the traditions of Proudhonism held their
ground longest of all, the workers’ parties in ef-
fect built their programmes and their tactics on
Marxist foundations.”4

In 1867, Volume I of Capital was published in
an edition of 1,000 copies in Germany. The total
number of copies of the book published during
the lifetime of Marx and Engels was hardly
above 50,000. Marx’s name was not widely known
then. Today Capital and its popular versions are
published in different languages throughout the
world.

By January 1, 1967, the three volumes of Cap-
ital were published in the U.S.S.R. 165 times
in 18 languages in a total of over six million
copies.

Marx considered the elaboration of the eco-
nomic theory of the proletariat his principal task
and his duty to the party. It took him many
years to create Capital, his major work, which,
as Engels justifiably noted, in all its parts was
the lifetime effort of a brilliant thinker.

Marx was more than just a profound thinker;
he was also a great revolutionary in practice who
combined work on evolving the ideological weap-
on of the proletariat with active participation

in the revolutionary struggle. He worked on theo-

According to Marx himself, he became inter-
ested in political economy in the 1840s when, as
a young man (he was not yet 25), he became
one of the editors of the Rheinische Zeitung
published in Kéln. The newspaper was founded
by a group of the bourgeoisie which opposed
Prussian absolutism. With Marx as one of its
editors, the paper acquired an increasingly rev-
olutionary-democratic character. It heatedly dis-
cussed freedom of trade, the condition of the
peasantry and other urgent problems, thus com-
ing into conflict with the government which
eventually closed it down. It was the elaboration
of these themes that, according to Marx, provided
the first occasions for occupying himself with
economic questions.!

Marx and Engels formed their militant part-
nership in Paris in 1844. Still earlier they entered
into correspondence with each other. In Septem-
ber 1844, they commenced work on the book The
Holy Family, or Critique of Critical Critique?
in which they laid down the principles under-
lying their revolutionary-materialist views. Back
in 1843, Engels published his Notes for Critique
of Political Economy, which Marx subsequently
referred to as brilliant notes. Lenin wrote that
“contact with Engels was undoubtedly a factor
in Marx’s decision to study political economy,

In the period from 1844 to 1847, during which
he lived in Paris, Brussels, London and Manches-
ter, Marx studied economic literature, reading
the works of some 70 authors, including Smith,
Ricardo, Mill, Thompson, Senior, Owen, Ques-
nay, and made abstracts of books on economic
history.

He summed up the first results of his economic
studies in The Poverty of Philosophy, a polem-
ical work directed against the anarchist Proud-
hon (1847). The same year Marx delivered a se-
ries of lectures on “Hired Labour and Capital”
in a German workers’ society in Brussels. In
1849, these lectures were published in the form
of editorials in the Neue Rheinische Zeitung.

Marx interrupted his systematic study of polit-
ical economy in connection with the revolution-
ary developments of 1848-1849. Following the
1848 revolution in France, revolutions occurred
in other West European countries. Marx and
Engels returned to Germany and headed the
Neue Rheinische Zeitung, the finest and unsur-
passed organ of the revolutionary proletariat, as
Lenin called it.

In the revolutionary movement Marx and
Engels represented the position of the most pro-
gressive class—the proletariat—and were the
heart and soul of all the revolutionary-democratic
aspirations in Rhenish Prussia. They upheld the
interests of the people, their freedom. But reac-
tion won in this unequal battle, and the news-
paper was closed down.

In London Marx resumed his study of political
economy, devoting much time to it, particularly
in the period between 1850 and 1857. In these
years he filled dozens of thick notebooks with
remarks and excerpts—the result of his intensive
work in the library of the British Museum.

Marx studied different problems of political
economy (money circulation, banks, crises, land
rent, etc.) and collected a wealth of information
concerning the history of this science. The eco-
nomic crisis of 1857 prompted him to systematise
the material he had studied. He closely followed
the crisis, registered its development in different
countries from newspaper reports and asked En-
gels to send him the information he needed from
Manchester.

On December 8, 1857, Marx wrote to Engels
that he “worked like mad sitting up nights, sum-
ming up his economic studies, so as to have a
clear idea of at least the principal problems be-
fore the deluge descends”. He was in a hurry to
provide the fighting proletariat with a powerful
theoretical weapon before the “deluge”, i.e., be-
fore the revolutionary outburst.

From October 1857 to June 1858 Marx worked
on a manuscript of about 50 signatures; he was
the first in history to thoroughly elaborate the
theory of value and the theory of surplus-value.
This was his second great discovery after evolving
the materialist understanding of history. This

At first Marx planned to publish his economic
studies in separate instalments under the heading
On the Critique of Political Economy. But later
he repeatedly changed his mind, searching for
a more appropriate form of publishing his work.

In 1858, he decided to issue it in several sepa-
rate books: 1. On Capital. 2. On Property in
Land. 3. On Hired Labour. 4. On the State.
5. On International Trade. 6. On the World
Market. Besides, Marx planned to publish two
separate books containing a critical essay on the
history of political economy and an outline of
the history of the development of economic cate-
gories and relations.

In 1859, the Duncker Publishing House in Ber-
lin issued, as the first instalment of this work,
the book On the Critique of Political Economy,
which was an important stage in the formation
and further development of Marx’s economic
teaching and in the critique of bourgeois political
economy.

While working on Capital, Marx encountered
many obstacles. In 1860 he had to interrupt his
economic research in connection with the cam-
paign raised against him by Karl Vogt.

Vogt, a German scientist, joined the revolution
in 1848-1849 as a petty-bourgeois democrat. His
booklet My Case Against the “Allgemeine Zeitung”
in which he juggled facts and resorted to fabri-
cations, was an attempt to discredit Marx and his
associates and to present their activity in a false
light. The German bourgeois press caught up
Vogt’s slanderous attacks. Marx regarded Vogt’s
campaign as an attempt to deal a decisive blow
to the emerging proletarian party and to de-
grade it morally in the eyes of society.

Marx lost almost a year of his valuable time
compiling a pamphlet entitled Herr Vogt. It was
written when the proletariat’s revolutionary
struggle was on the upsurge following the eco-
nomic crisis of 1857-1858, and when Marx and
Engels intensified their efforts to muster, con-
solidate and educate revolutionary cadres. By
repelling Vogt’s campaign of slander Marx in-
tended to bring the great and pure cause of the
revolutionary party to the knowledge of the
masses and prevent its adversaries from be-
smirching it.

In his reply to Vogt Marx not only unmasked
his false assertions but also gave a truthful

In the middle of 1861, Marx resumed work on
Capital. But he did not work hastily. His scientific
conscientiousness was exceptional indeed. Before
arriving at a conclusion he would go through
mountains of books, he systematically checked his
conclusions and rewrote those parts of his work
which did not satisfy him. “Whatever shortcom-
ings my works may have,” Marx wrote to Engels
on July 31, 1865, “they have that artistic entirety
which can be achieved only by my method of not
sending them to print until they lie before me
as a whole.”

Franz Mehring, a German historian and au-
thority on world literature, considered the author
of Capital one of the best German writers of the
19th century. Wilhelm Liebknecht considered
that Marx was a brilliant master of German
prose.!

that no one except myself, not even you, will be

Engels advised Marx first to prepare Volume I
for publication and then go on with the rest of
the work. Accordingly, Marx started to prepare
a clean copy of Volume I, simultaneously im-
proving its style, or, as he put it, “licking the baby
clean after such difficult delivery”. But the real-
isation of even this reduced plan was a terrible
strain. Overwork undermined his health. He suf-
fered terribly from prolonged furunculosis, rheu-
matism, liver trouble and insomnia, which imped-
ed his work.

The first signs of overwork and undernourish-
ment made themselves felt in 1863. He endured
a particularly serious spell of illness in the begin-
ning of 1866. This is what he wrote at the time:
“...1 was constantly hovering at the edge of the
grave. Hence I had to make use of every moment
when I was able to work to complete my book, to
which I have sacrificed health, happiness, and
family.”!

In addition to ill health, Marx was constantly
beset with financial difficulties. He received very
little for his books. Neither could he count on
having his articles printed in the press. For sever-
al years he was a correspondent of the New York
Daily Tribune, and the money he received for
the articles he wrote for the paper was his main
source of income. But that too came to an end in

The publication of the French edition of
Capital was launched in 1872, shortly after the
appearance of the Russian translation of Vol-
ume I, and completed three years later.

This edition was intended to make Marx’s
economic theory accessible to workers in France
and other Latin countries. In this way Marxist
political economy became widespread where it
was needed most to fight against Proudhonism
and other varieties of petty-bourgeois socialism
which impeded the development of the working-
class movement.

At the time the French workers played a prom-
inent role not only in the history of their coun-
try, but also in the international revolutionary
movement.

Engels characterised the French proletariat as
“the fighting army of progress”.1 In 1871, for
the first time in world history, the French work-
