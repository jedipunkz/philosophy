By Parvus
Introduction

Socialism is based on the working class. The interests of the working class, however,
encompass the entire world. The proletariat wants and should become the bearer of civilization.
Therefore, in its idealistic striving, it has long since stepped beyond the confines of the wage
relationship and has turned its interest to the most diverse problems of economics, politics, and
culture. Our journal knows of no issue of public life that could remain outside the scope of its
socialist perspective. We cannot devote equal space to all problems because their social
significance varies, but we will have to deal with most of what comes to the forefront of public
attention: with the eight-hour day and rising food prices as well as with Turkish finances,
Russian peasant conditions, the question of the economic unification of Europe, the American
financial market, the nationalization of the German coal mines, the national development of the
Serbs or the Czechs, the situation of the Russian Jews, ocean trade, China, and Japan, with India
and Persia, with the policies of the big banks and with the efforts of anti-alcoholics, with literary
movements, art movements, and with accident prevention, industrial hygiene, etc. etc. We have
no particular subject, since we live in a time that is transforming everything. First and foremost,
we will, of course, devote our attention to the economic and cultural upheaval brought about by
the World War. Guided by the socialist perspective, this journal aims to be independent in its
judgment. Socialist knowledge has become broad and shallow; it has lost its depth and
revolutionary momentum. People have learned to adapt to ignorance and lack of understanding
in order to become popular more quickly. A unique socialist tradition has developed, which, like
every tradition, had a conservative effect. We want to keep ourselves free from all of this; we
also do not want to allow our judgment to be influenced by friendly and comradely
considerations, or even by pity for the weak and oppressed. We do not want to flatter the poor.

We will speak the whole, naked truth openly and freely regardless of who this benefits or harms.

For knowledge is the highest thing the individual can contribute to the progress of humanity. We
want to be read, but we do not seek the favor of the public. We prefer honest and brave
opponents to faint-hearted friends. We do not fear the clash of opinions; rather, we seek it out: for
only in the battle of minds do views become clear. But under all circumstances, we are for unity
in the action of the proletariat. Whoever still seeks to disturb the many experiences of the
proletarian class struggle can no longer do so out of / immaculate ignorance, but is committing a
crime against the working class. Anyone who really has something to say is welcome as a
collaborator. We don't look at names, we don't recognize authorities. Anyone who has facts or
ideas to present that can serve to clarify the situation in general or in individual countries, in

individual areas, should receive an unbiased evaluation.

The Beginnings: The Influence of the Great French Revolution.

It was the great French Revolution and the world wars that sprang from it that first shook
Germany politically. The emancipation of the peasants, elementary schools, universal
conscription, and an intellectual movement that ventured to tackle the principles of world order
were the consequences. History was later falsified by focusing on Napoleon. Napoleon was made
into a fetish, like the German Emperor now. But even if Napoleon's personality played an
incomparably greater role in the wars and politics of that time than the German Emperor does
today, the emphasis lay not in him, but in the political forces that the bourgeoisie had unleashed
in France. In Napoleon himself, people saw only a military genius, his tyranny, and his dynastic
interests, and overlooked the great state-building for which he had fought. It was the new
bourgeois political order of France that all of Europe wanted to adapt. Therefore, Europe would
have advanced by at least half a century if Napoleon had succeeded in maintaining his empire for
even just another twenty years The setback, as is well known, came from the Muscovite Empire,
which was still insufficiently developed materially and ideologically for the bourgeois state
order. Napoleon's army perished due to Russia's roadlessness, its inadequate means of
subsistence, and the primitive construction that made it possible to burn down all of Moscow.
Now the development of Europe was held back by reactionary force, just as it had previously
been driven forward by revolutionary force. It is good to remember this. Russian hegemony in
Europe lasted until after 1848, and that was the time of the political stagnation. Only the Crimean
War broke the disastrous Russian influence. Only political childish people and historica
anoramuses can imagine that the internal policy of a state can be separated from its foreign
policy, and that the political regime of such a large empire as Russia concerns only the state itself

and not its neighbors. Ever since Russia had become a modern military state, all of Europe

suffered from its political backwardness. Russian Tsarism not only held back Russia's
development, but was and remains a tremendous obstacle to European development The clash
with the Napoleonic armies brought to the full awareness of the entire German people the curse
of German petty states, of ossified The derman bouge isle embarked on its struggle on its
struggle for freedom. But the German idea of freedom was at the same time the idea of empire
the striving for national unification, for political centralization. By contrast, by this time the
general bourgeois ideal had already lost much of the luster with which it had een surrounded in
the first years of the great French Revolution. The French Revolution itself had unfolded, in
rapid succession, the class struggles that were disintegrating bourgeois society. With the
overthrow of Napoleon, the military power of the French revolutionary armies was broken, and
the prestige of the European monarchies was restored The minds in Germany were all the more
inclined to take a critical look at the French situation because the socia structure of the German
lands was not yet as permeated oy capitalism as in France, and revolutionary France initially
asserted itself against Germany as a hostilé power that robbed it of its political independence.
The workers' struggles in England and the development of socialist ideas in France provided
ample material to nourish this critical spirit All that remained was to allow the criticism to pass

through the depths of German philosophy, and the system was complete.
The Communist Manifesto and the German Revolution.

This happened in 1847 through the Communist Manifesto by Karl Marx and Fr. Engels.
The historical experience, the social struggles on which the Communist Manifesto is based,
relate entirely to France and England. Germany merely provided the philosophical method by
means of which the inner workings of bourgeois society built on industrialism, of so-called
capitalism, and its social consequences, were revealed. Thus, German philosophy overcame
capitalism ideally at a time when it had not yet been materially realized in Germany. From this
contradiction, an issue had to develop between the intellectual leaders of the German revolution
and the German bourgeoisie, the political bearer of the movement. While German revolutionary
thought went far beyond the ideas of the great French Revolution, the German bourgeoisie

lagged far behind the French bourgeoisie in its revolutionary behavior. Various factors played a

role. First, the German bourgeoisie felt less strong and was further weakened by political
fragmentation. For while in France the bourgeois revolution took place within the centralized
state, in Germany this state had to be created through the revolution. Second, the German
bourgeoisie had governments before it that already knew how to benefit from the experiences of
the French Revolution. Moreover, it had a proletariat behind it that was openly preparing to stab
it in the back. The German Revolution collapsed, and from then on the German proletariat
became increasingly politically detached from the Bourgeoisie. The radical spirits of the

revolution denounce the political betrayal of the bourgeoisie to the workers.” Nevertheless, there

external bond that united them separated them from bourgeois society, and brought them into
sharp opposition to government power, Since all other forms of public activity were severely
restricted, German Social Democracy shifted its attention primarily to parliamentary action.
