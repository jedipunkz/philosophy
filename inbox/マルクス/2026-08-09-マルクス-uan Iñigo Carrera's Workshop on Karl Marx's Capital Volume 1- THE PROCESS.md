etc.). Thick lines mark the end of each workshop meeting. Any proposal for improvement is
welcome and will be - and is being - considered. The complete workshop finished transcribing in
the early hours of a Wednesday, June 26, so: see you next Wednesday.

Workshop Introduction

[...| We are going to try to start by going to the core of the question of the
meaning of the workshop with one of the questions that you had in the survey,
which I asked with one intention, and universally you read it with another
intention. Something that we are going to refer to later again regarding whether
the question is to interpret, how to interpret what one supposes to be clear, and for
the others it is clear that they assumed that what was clear was the opposite of
what I had thought. The question is: what are they here for? Why are you
interested in the workshop? The idea of my question was that there should be only
one answer from the list of possible answers. And in a moment of weakness, I said:
I'm going to put "Main answer" and "Secondary answer" (with the idea that there
were going to be only 2 answers), and that enabled the system to fill all the rows.
And you interpreted by filling all the rows. I am going to take some of the answers
that appear to see how far we are going to be able to answer what appears, what
link it has with the development of the workshop.

So, I look at the responses—or at least some of them—since I had posted a very
long list. One was "Deepening Marx's thought." That answer immediately raises a
question in me: why do you want to delve deeper into Marx's thoughts? Where
does the interest come from, what is the purpose? Another answer was "The
difference between Marxist dialectics and Hegelian dialectics." And this answer
leads me to the same question as before: and why go deeper into the difference
between these two dialects? Is it a purely intellectual concern? Do I want it for
something? Do I want to enrich my subjectivity by being clear about this? Or is
there a reason behind that interest? I take another possible answer: "The debates
within Marxism." And I have the same question I had before: is it curiosity? Do I
have anything at stake with regard to the debates in Marxism? Why the need to
delve into that? And also, a need that implies - it is proposed that way - a work
simply with Volume 1 (which was raised in the ema// we sent you, for two years)?
And if I am willing to dedicate two hours a week to this—but over the course of
two years—is it simply a need that boils down to wanting to know more about this
individual, or about the debates currently taking place, or do I have something at
stake that goes beyond that?

To make a long story short, in general, the axles had that character. And... I want
to see the answer that was just in the chat. Jorge's answer is: "To know in depth
and dialogue with those who know Marx's work", but why? What is at stake in that
interest? There was one of the answers that had a different content. He was
referring not to knowing but to "doing", but also to doing one's own personally.
And when one looks in general at the interest in something called the critique of
political economy (a subtitle of Marx's work), when the term political economy
appears, what immediately confronts us with this theme is a problem of political
action. From whom? For Marx it would be his. The question at stake is the question
of political action itself. It is a problem of knowledge. And knowledge - we will
dwell on this later - is the knowledge of one's own determinations, of one's own
subjectivity. And in this case, the axis of the question that is placed in the title itself,
is one's own condition as a political subject. I'm going to try to take it in this way.

There was a workshop in which the answers were verbal and there were many
responses of: "I want to go deeper" in this aspect, in another aspect... And there
was a participant who immediately raised this: I come to the workshop because I
see what is happening in my neighborhood, in people who are close to me, and I
want to transform that situation. I want to do something to transform that
situation. And so the starting question that one can find beyond what appears
under an "intellectual" concern is a question about what to do, and about a political
"what to do." When one is confronted with the text, what one is asking is why this
is going to contribute to the ability to organize my action. If one looks at it (and
then I am going to ask why one can confront Capital, the text, in this way)... I am
going to take it in a situation that immediately touches me personally, which I
suppose must be shared by many more of those present.

We here in Argentina, as teachers, are in an action of struggle for our salary (to
take it in simple terms). And I can face this situation and say: well, it's a problem of
struggle for wages. And by knowing that I already have the answer I need to know
what to do. Now, it may be that when I am confronted with this question I will say:
it is not enough for me to know this in order to be able to decide how I organize my
action. If I'm fighting for a salary, the first thing I need to know is what a salary is. If
I say I'm struggling, then what I'm saying is: if I have an antagonistic relationship
with someone because of my salary. I could also say: well, I already have what it
takes to act, that is my enemy and I already know what I have to do in the face of
that. Or for some reason that at this point I cannot explain, it may be that I say: it is
not enough for me to know that it is a salary problem and that I have an
antagonistic relationship with the subject who pays me. I have to ask myself why
this antagonistic relationship is characteristic. And suppose I answer that what is
at stake here is the distribution of income. And again it may be that with that
answer he says: I already know what I have to do. And for some reason—and I'm
going to project it to you—you are not satisfied with that answer, because if not,
then you would not be wondering about what Marx is saying about the movement
and the relationship that you have as members of society.

And then, one continues to wonder about his "what to do" as a political subject.
He looks at the distribution of income and says: in this society there are classes
differentiated by the form of income: the form of my income is a salary, I am a
wage earner, and the form of income of those with whom I am confronted is the
class of capitalists who pay wages. Then I can say again: I already have what I
needed to know to organize my action. And I may say: but I still don't know how to
organize my action. Because in order to organize it, if I have to confront the
capitalists, I need to know what capital is. If I don't know, how am I going to
confront the capitalists? I'm going to be unarmed in front of these guys. And so I
look at what capital is and say: it is a sum of money that produces a profit. And this
is enough for me. And then that answer asks me a question: what is money? How
can I know what income distribution is and face it as a conscious subject in the
struggle for its distribution if I cannot explain what money is? A more or less
immediate elementary answer that I can give myself is: money is something that is
used to exchange commodities. And so to know what money is, I need to know
what a commodity is. And in this process—which I am presenting very crudely—I
found that in order to answer myself about what to do, I have to answer myself

about what Marx's text presents to me in the first paragraph where he says
[paraphrasing]: in societies where the capitalist mode of production prevails,
wealth is presented as an immense accumulation of commodities, and the
commodity as its elementary form. Consequently, our analysis starts from the
commodity. This is how the text begins.

So, several observations regarding this. On that path we never stop asking
ourselves what to do, about our own subjectivity. And what we find is that within
our subjectivity is the commodity. It seems that we have passed from our condition
as human subjects to these things that are commodities. And yet, we were always
looking at ourselves in our condition as subjects. We have no explanation so far of
what commodities are. Precisely what Marx proposes, who has taken this path that
I present here (in some places it is insinuated, thus referred), what we arrive at in
this process is that starting point. And now we have to go further, we have to
answer what a commodity is, but not simply because we are curious to know it. It
is because when we answer ourselves about what a commodity is, we are going to
answer ourselves about what we ourselves are as human subjects. And more
specifically, what we are as political subjects. And that is what the process that we
propose to develop is about. It is not a problem of economics. When you look at
what starts with the commodity, it can look like an economics book. It is not: itis a
book that so far deals with our subjectivity.

But in addition, another question related to this arises [...]: why the hell instead
of staying calm and doing as many other people do, who quickly know what form
they have to give to their political action (it may be that they don't give a damn
what form they are going to give it and that they resolve that they do what comes
to their mind at that moment)...why do we ask ourselves this question? It might
seem that we are "cooler", more lively, more intelligent or restless people, and that
is why we ask ourselves these questions that others do not ask. And that response
would not only be sterile, but would destroy us as political subjects. What we have
to answer is not only what to do, but why the what to do that we are proposing has
a determination that makes it face political action in a way, let's call it, "peculiar"
within the general movement of the life of society. This is also the answer we have
to find in the development of asking ourselves about our own subjectivity, a
determined subjectivity that, as such, is faced with the need to ask ourselves about
its own determination beyond what is "normal." And that is what we are going to
put at stake in the progress that we propose to develop. This is the first part of
what we wanted to present to you at this kick-off meeting. Then we are going to
move forward on concrete ways that make up this objective that we are proposing.

This is a tool, but it's not a tool simply out of abstract curiosity. One is always
asking oneself what to do, for one's own subjectivity. Knowledge is always the
knowledge of what powers are held as a subject with respect to the powers that
the environment offers to that subjectivity, how to operate on that environment.
When you are getting to know what you are doing, you are organizing your action.
And I want to emphasize one question: it is not the action of other subjects, it is not
simply or abstractly the action of the proletariat. The question is what am I as a
subject, who recognizes myself as a member of the proletariat. It is not an abstract

problem of what the proletariat is, but I have to answer myself what I do as an
individual organ of the proletariat.

[...| I might add: it is not a philosophy book. Because the problem of action began
with the question of what do I do, not with an abstract question of "What am I?" I
have to act all the time, not just in this, so I always have to answer what I am as the
subject of that action. From someone who precisely stated in contrast to action
that "Philosophers have done nothing but interpret the world in different ways,
but what is at stake is to change it" 2(and we look at knowledge not as something
external to action, but as the moment in which action is organizing itself). And who
also proposed that philosophy had ended with Hegel, the contrast that appears
there is between an interpretation of the world and the action of transforming it.
What we are not going to do is interpret the world by interpreting Marx in some
way. We are going to face what Marx says and we are going to ask ourselves if in
our own social bond, what Marx says we recognize or not. And consequently, we
are going to permanently subject what Marx unfolds to criticism, we are going to
require that we ourselves can reproduce that process. And in this sense I want to
contrast it with many statements that appear as: it is a problem of what Marx
meant. But the only one who could answer that is Marx himself, and what we are
not going to do is that Marx's text literally says something and it is said that this
cannot be what he meant. Because it clashes with Marx's entire development. If
Marx wrote something and we do not find that same something when we confront
capital (our social relation, not abstract, but concrete), we are not going to pretend
that Marx really meant what we want him to say. If we find something different, we
will say that this is what we find.

I strongly emphasize this because we are not going to interpret the text: we are
going to face the text as a tool that allows our process of knowledge of our
determinations (that is what the book is about, it is not about other subjects who
are out there), what the text is going to allow us is that instead of discovering
gunpowder every time, we can make our process of knowledge individually
original, as a process of recognition from the social point of view. It is a knowledge
that has an objective existence, which we can appropriate. of thinking about the
initial question all our lives, we will then have the potentiality of someone who
wondered about this and said: this is what I find. What we are going to do is say:
Marx found that, let's see if we find the same thing or not. And if we don't find the
same thing, we'll think about it very well, because the individual who did all this
work... We are not going to lightly say that we are going somewhere else and it is
over, we are going to look at it very carefully, and if we continue to find something
that was different we are going to say that this is what I need to organize my action,
if I do not find the other, it is alien to me, it does not answer the question of what to
do.

There is a discussion linked to this (I think it was more or less general in Latin
America, but in Argentina it had a lot of presence in the decades of the 60's and
70') about what the text of Capital is for. And it seemed that, well, it's about
England in the mid-nineteenth century, and that has nothing to do with Argentina

2Reference to number XI of Marx's Theses on Feuerbach (1845), published by Engels as an
appendix to his work Ludwig Feuerbach and the End of Classical German Philosophy (1888).

in the twentieth century. And this continues to be repeated today and worse,
because 50 more years have passed. In addition, this approach appeared
reinforced... I'm going to take a very simple example. Marx's development in
Capital shows how the development of machinery imposes a permanent
technological renewal and the capitals that do not maintain this movement are
defeated in competition. When one looked at Argentina at that time, one finds this
situation: the capitals that operate in Argentina in the industrial sector do so witha
productivity of labor that is a fifth of the productivity of the sector in the United
States. But they are the same capitals that are in the US or in European countries,
and here they operate with this productivity. So the answer was that this does not
serve to explain the situation in Argentina, because it not only referred to
situations from the mid-nineteenth century but also that what I said was going to
happen, is the opposite of what is happening here. Faced with this situation, one
can take different paths.

One that is incoherent and from my point of view wrong, which is to say: "That
doesn't matter to me", and now I explain what is happening in Argentina by
stopping at the immediate appearances that here is this, so-and-so came... And it
was over. And there are two answers that from my point of view are coherent, but
one is wrong, which is to say: well, then I start from scratch and ask myself again
what this process of social life is, and I do not use this knowledge that is objectified
in the text. And the answer that for me is coherent and correct is: well, I'm going to
do this process that I proposed before, I'm going to move forward on my path as a
process of original knowledge and recognition from the social point of view. And
when I find something that the text tells me that it is going there and I see that it is
going here, I am not going to say that I throw it away, but that I have to develop a
process of original knowledge from the social point of view to answer why the
concrete form that this general determination has has these specific forms that
appear as the negation of the general determination, because it is carried out
under these concrete forms. In this sense, we can say that we have two advantages
over Marx.

The first is that we have Marx's text and we can use it as a tool. What Marx's life
took and he could not finish, we will be able to do much more quickly, and
consequently we will be able to go further and ask ourselves about concrete forms
of our political action that go beyond what Marx could. The second is that we are

Chapter I. The commodity

1. The two factors of the commodity: use value and value (substance
of value, magnitude of value)

In our last meeting, we explored the question of what to do, of our own
subjectivity, and we outlined, very broadly, that in this process we confront the
commodity. What we're going to do now is look at it as it appears in the text,
without explicitly stating the prior analytical process, because it's not included in
the text.

Let's begin with the first sentence Marx uses in Capital, which is this (according
to different versions): “In societies where the capitalist mode of production
prevails, wealth appears as an ‘immense accumulation of commodities,’ and the
commodity as its elementary form.” This starting point is not a statement of
concepts or principles from which he will proceed; it is a description of a concrete
reality, presented as an immediate assertion (it has no further development
beyond this). Regarding the relationship between commodities and capital, the
only thing one can say is that within the capitalist mode of production,
commodities exist and appear as the expression of social wealth. Consequently,
one can recognize in this Marx's statement: I am not starting from concepts, but
from the simplest concrete reality of contemporary society, which is the
commodity.

First, we have this starting point. If we look at it, we'll find several things to
observe. Just as I said before that the commodity simply exists within the capitalist
mode of production, it's also not explicitly stated that the commodity is the
product of something: it's simply something present within this society as an
expression of wealth. We'll proceed step by step because we're not going to
interpret what the text says; what we're going to try to do is reproduce for
ourselves what Marx is confronting us with in the text. If we skip one of the steps,
we'll then find that we transform this attempt to reproduce the determinations of
our social being with our thinking into a representation, and representations can
only be interpreted, and everyone can interpret them as they please. But
interpretation is knowledge that stops at an appearance. And we're going to try not
to stop at any appearance, to ensure that each issue we face accounts for its
determinations, its necessity. And then I'm going to bring up something that came
a little later.

When we take this starting point, it's an immediate concrete reality presented to
us, and the only thing we can do in the face of it is what Marx says: let's analyze the
commodity. In that process of analysis, in the very expression that it all begins with

(Necessity, determination, and the question of “determinism” ].

To address this, since it's not a matter of stating principles, let's consider
ourselves as the objects of the question. And if we look at ourselves, the first thing
we'll see is that we are an actual existence. This existence has a concrete form, but
at the same time, it carries within it a multiplicity of potentialities to be realized,
potentialities that arise from, that are inherent in, its actual existence. These
potentialities are the concrete condition of actual existence and, therefore, of what
one is. So, when I say "necessity," it's because what one ís carries within it this
multiplicity of potentialities to be realized, and this is its necessity; it is determined
as the bearer of these potentialities.

Often, what emerges when we consider a potentiality to be realized is the image
of a simple potentiality, one that has immediate realization and does not exist in
the form of possibility. It has only one way of being realized, and it is not carried
alongside opposing potentialities. Consequently, if we look at it strictly, it doesn't
even have a temporal dimension because, being simple, it is realized immediately
upon itself. Now, I could take this as an expression of simple potentiality but
abstracted from its form of realization: we need to breathe. If we were to stop
breathing, we would cease to reproduce ourselves as the actual existence that
carries these potentialities. We would become an actual existence whose
potentiality is to begin to rot, to decompose, because it would be a body that no
longer has the potential to reproduce itself; it would cease to be a living existence.
We have the simple necessity to breathe. This potentiality to breathe does not exist
simultaneously with an equally potent potentiality not to breathe. We can hold our
breath for a brief period, but to affirm ourselves in the realization of our actual
existence, we need to breathe.

What other form does necessity take? One possesses potentialities that, when
realized, are contradictory: I cannot breathe and not breathe at the same time. I
have the temporary potentiality not to breathe, but I cannot realize both
simultaneously. The fact that I will realize my actual existence by realizing my
potentialities—I can realize one or I can realize the other—means that the
realization of these two potentialities cannot exist in the same concrete form. At
each moment of my existence, this potentiality exists in the form of possibility.
Hegel refers to this: it is a necessity that is simultaneously not a necessity. And the
general form of the potentialities of all forms of existence (and of the living subject
in particular), the form in which one carries one's determinations, one's necessity,
is in the form of possibility: they are potentialities that can be realized in one form
or another.

When I say that something is determined, | mean that the actual existence that
carries that potential to be realized, when that existence reaches its end, realizes
that potential, takes the concrete form that corresponds to that potential as
realized. Determination is not an external relation between two things; it is an
actual existence that realizes its potentialities (reaches its end) and takes a new
concrete form, which has been determined by the previous actual existence. That
form is an actual existence that is the form in which the previous existence has
transcended itself and taken on this new form. In the case of living subjects, the

corpse that is the object of the life process of other life forms, but not of ourselves.
