Preamble

The ghost of Communism is haunting Europe and all the major powers
have joined forces to drive it out. The Pope, the Tsar of the Russian
Empire, The Foreign Minister of the Austro-Hungarian Empire, French
Politicians, and German police spies all ﬁght together in an eﬀort to
drive out the ghost.
Every party in Europe that opposes the existing governments has been
called Communist. And every progressive has thrown the term
Communism at their more radical opponents as an insult.
As the result of this, we can say two things:
1) Everyone in Europe recognises that the Communist movement is
powerful; and
2) It's time for Communists to come out of the shadows and face their
opponents openly. We should publish our aims and beliefs for all to
see.
That's why Communists of many countries have met in London to write
this manifesto, which we'll publish in English, French, German, Italian,
Flemish and Danish.
 

Chapter 1: Capitalists and Proletarians

There has been class struggle in every society that has existed so far.
Slaves and their owners struggled against each other. The Roman
nobles and the Roman lower classes struggled against each other.
Medieval lords and their serfs struggled against each other. And
master craftsmen and their apprentices struggled against each other.
In other words, oppressor and oppressed have fought a constant battle
against each other. Sometimes the struggle was quiet and undramatic.
At other times, the class struggle has broken out into open warfare.
When that happened, society was either transformed by the class that
won the ﬁght, or else everyone suﬀered miserably from the damage
caused by the ﬁghting.
In earlier eras of history, there was a complicated social hierarchy
almost everywhere. In ancient Rome there were patricians, knights,
plebeians, and slaves. In the Middle Ages, there were major lords,
lords that served them, guild-masters, journeymen, apprentices, and
serfs. There was also a hierarchy of seniority inside almost all of these
classes.
Class struggle still exists in the modern, capitalist society that has
replaced feudalism. The only thing that's changed is the classes that
exist, how people are oppressed, and the way that the classes struggle
against each other.
Our era, the era of capitalism, is diﬀerent in one key way though. It has
made the class struggle simpler by splitting most of society into only
two classes. The two great classes that face each other today are the
capitalists (who own the means of production and proﬁt from exploiting
the labour of others,) and the proletarians, (the wage working class
who get no income from owning capital).
In the Middle Ages, some serfs became chartered burghers (citizens of
a town who weren't serfs, and who were born inside marriage.) The
Capitalist class started to develop from some of these townsfolk.
Europeans discovering the Americas and learning that they could sail
around the Cape of South Africa, towards Asia, opened up new
opportunities for the rising Capitalist class. They were able to trade
with East India and China, colonise America, and trade with all the
European colonies. These new opportunities for trade rapidly
increased the importance of trade, and let the Capitalist class grow
quickly inside their dying, feudal societies.
Under the feudal system, commodities (goods made for trade) were
made by craftsmen who belonged to guilds that regulated the price of
goods. But that system of production wasn't good enough to satisfy all
the trade that the new markets demanded. As a result, the guilds were

pushed aside by a new class of manufacturers. Instead of diﬀerent
parts of goods being manufactured by diﬀerent guilds, the whole
process was brought inside single workshops.
Even as this process of manufacturing workshops replaced the guild
system, the market for exports kept growing. These early
manufacturing operations couldn't survive either. Soon, the invention of
the steam engine transformed the manufacturing process. After that,
the industrial middle class was replaced by industrial millionaires, who
employed armies of workers to manufacture goods using large
machines. (Editor's note: because of inﬂation, a millionaire in the time
that Marx and Engels wrote this was closer to being worth £100 million
in today's money. The industrial millionaires are the people we'd think
of as large capitalists.)
Starting with the discovery of the Americas, modern industry has
created a global market. This market has led to developments in trade,
navigation, and land based travel technology. That led to the further
development of manufacturing industries. The more that industry and
trade have grown, navigation has improved, and railways have been
extended, the more the Capitalist class has grown and increased its
wealth. As that has happened, it has pushed aside all the old classes
of the Middle Ages.
We see then how the modern Capitalist class is the result of a long
series of development, during which the methods of producing and
distributing goods changed dramatically.
Each step in the development of the Capitalist class was matched by
an advance in its political power. Here it was an oppressed class under
the feudal nobility, then an armed, self governing medieval commune.
Here an independent republic, as happened in Germany and Spain.
There a taxable third class in society, as happened in France, (the ﬁrst
two classes being the nobility and the peasants.) Once the period of
manufacturing was underway, it became a force that the monarchy
could pit against the nobility in both the semi-feudal and absolute
monarchies. In fact, the growing Capitalist class became the key
power that the monarchy rested on. Finally, since it opened the world
market and established modern industrial manufacturing, the Capitalist
class has conquered exclusive political power for itself by establishing
liberal democracies. The executive branch of the modern, liberal
democracy is nothing more than a committee for managing the joint
interests of the Capitalist class.
The Capitalist class has historically played a most revolutionary role.
Whenever the Capitalist class has got the upper hand, it has put an
end to the old, romanticised patriarchal and feudal relations. It has torn
apart the old hierarchies which said that some people were naturally
above or below others, and has replaced the old social hierarchy with

relationships based on cold, hard cash and naked self interest. It has
got rid of romantic ideas about chivalry and religious idealism. It has
created a world where personal worth is based on how much
economic value you can produce. In place of a world where
exploitation was hidden behind religious and other moral sounding
justiﬁcations, the Capitalist class has created a world of open,
shameless, and brutal exploitation, under the name of Free Trade.
The Capitalist class has stripped once highly regarded professions of
the awed respect that people once had for them. The doctor, lawyer,
priest, poet, and scientist have been converted into wage workers.
The Capitalist class has torn away the sentimental view of family
relationships and has reduced the family to relationships based on
money.
Reactionaries greatly admire the vigorous and heroic deeds of the
Middle Ages, but the Capitalist class has shown how lazy and relaxed
most life in the Middle Ages truly was. While people in the Middle Ages
sometimes did extraordinary things, the Capitalist system has created
wonders that far surpass the creation of the Great Pyramids, the
Roman water systems, or the Gothic Cathedrals. It has gone on quests
that are far more impressive than any medieval crusade.
The Capitalist class can't exist without constantly updating the tools
and machinery used in production, and because of that it constantly
changes the relationships that people have with each other as they
produce, exchange, and distribute wealth. (The technical term for
these relationships as a whole is the Relations of Production. That
term is used in the original text). In all former Modes of Production, the
existence of classes depended on the technology for producing
remaining the same. That created a sense of certainty that we don't
have in Capitalist society. In Capitalist society, the ways in which
people produce are constantly updated and, because of that, the way
that people live is always changing. Ever-lasting uncertainty is what
makes this era diﬀerent to all former ones.
The long lasting, ﬁxed relationships between classes that existed
under previous Modes of Production allowed people to build up deeply
held beliefs about why things were the way they were. Today, the
relationships that people have with each other as they produce,
exchange, and distribute wealth become outdated before people have
a chance to form deeply held beliefs about them. With no new beliefs
that twist people's view of the relationships between classes, people
are forced to look at the real life conditions that they face. And they're
forced to look at the real nature of the relationships that they have with
other people in the economy.
The need to constantly expand the market for its goods forces the
Capitalist class to expand into all corners of the earth. It has to settle

everywhere and form connections everywhere.
By turning the whole world into a market for its goods, the Capitalist
class has given the world a shared culture of production and
consumption. Upsetting reactionaries greatly, the Capitalist class has
destroyed all the old national industries. All the old national industries
have already been destroyed or are being destroyed with each passing
day. They are pushed aside by new industries that don't only work
using raw materials from their homelands, but materials from the
remotest parts of the world. And the products of the industries aren't
only consumed at home, but all around the world as well. Instead of
being happy with only the products produced in their country as they
used to be, people now demand products from all over the globe.
Instead of nations being self-suﬃcient, now they're all dependent on
each other. Even the knowledge and ideas of each country are shared
globally. It's impossible for people to be narrow minded and only view
things from the perspective of their own country, like they used to.
By rapidly improving the tools and machinery used in production, and
by greatly extending the travel infrastructure, the Capitalist class draws
even the most backward nations into the modern world. No nation can
resist the cheap prices of manufactured products, and the Capitalists
use that to get even the most reluctant countries to join the global
market. They force these nations to become Capitalist themselves or
risk getting wiped out. In that way, the Capitalists remake the world
into a Capitalist one.
The Capitalist class has put the countryside under the power of the
towns. It has created huge cities, whose population is much greater
than the countryside. In that way, it has made large sections of the
population better educated and more sophisticated than they would
have been if they'd been isolated in villages. Just as the Capitalist
class has made the country dependent on the towns, it's made the
backward nations dependent on the advanced nations. It’s made the
nations of peasants in the East dependent on the nations of Capitalists
in the West.
The Capitalist class keeps getting rid of the scattered Means of
Production. It has gathered the population and the Means of
Production into small areas instead, and has centralised ownership of
the economy into the hands of only a few people. The result of this had
to be the centralisation of political power. Independent or only loosely
connected provinces, with separate interests, laws, governments, and
tax systems, became lumped together into one nation, with one
government, one code of laws, one national class-interest, one border,
and one set of import taxes.
In its rule of barely a hundred years, the Capitalist class has created
more gigantic industries than all previous generations put together.

They've tamed the forces of nature, built machinery, and used
chemistry to improve manufacturing and farming. They've created
steam ships, railways, and electric telegraphs, cleared whole
continents for use in commercial farming through colonisation and
forest clearance, built canals and overseen the gathering of huge
populations into cities. People in past centuries could never have
imagined such industries and populations of workers.
We see that the Capitalist class built up its means of production and
exchange while still existing within feudal society. At a certain stage of
their development, the legal structure of feudal society got in the way
of the new, capitalist means of producing and exchanging things. The
feudal relations of farming and manufacturing didn't match the needs
of the developing economy anymore, and held back its growth. When
society reached that stage, the feudal order was overthrown.
The new order that the Capitalist class established was based on free
competition, and had a political system which supported that. This
system is one where the Capitalist class controls society.
A similar movement is happening before our eyes. Capitalist society
has built such a gigantic system of production, exchange, and property
rights that it's like they've summoned a monster that they can't control.
For decades, the history of manufacturing and trade has been one of
the modern productive forces rebelling against the system of property
rights that forms the basis of Capitalist rule. (The term productive
forces is the Means of Production, that is the tools and raw materials
used in production, together with the labour force.) As evidence, we
can look at the frequent economic crises that put the entire existence
of Capitalist society on trial, each time more threateningly.
In these crises, not only do a large percentage of products on the
market go to waste (because there's nobody to buy them), a large part
of the productive forces are destroyed too (as businesses go bankrupt
or downsize.) The kinds of crises caused by the Capitalist system
would seem absurd under earlier economic systems. Suddenly,
society is plunged into an economic crisis that sends businesses
bankrupt and sees workers lose their jobs. It's like society is being
plugged into a famine. And why? Because too much has been
produced. There are too many of the things necessary for life. There is
too much available for trade.
The tools, raw materials, and labour power no longer serve to increase
Capitalist private property. Instead, they've become too powerful for
this economic system and are being held back by it. As soon as they
overcome the limits of capitalist production, they bring disorder into the
whole of Capitalist society and threaten Capitalist property. The
conditions of Capitalist society are too restrictive to contain the wealth
created by it. And how does the Capitalist class get over these crises?

Partly by forcing the destruction of parts of the productive forces, partly
by conquering new markets, and partly by exploiting existing markets
more thoroughly. In other words, they deal with the crises by paving
the way for larger and more destructive crises, and by eliminating
things which prevent crises.
The weapons that the Capitalist class used to cut down feudalism now
turned on the Capitalist class itself.
But not only has the Capitalist class forged the weapons that bring
death to itself; it has also called into existence the people who are to
wield those weapons — the modern working class — the Proletarians.
To the extent that capitalism is developed, the proletariat develops in
proportion to it. The proletariat is the modern working class: a class of
labourers, who live only so long as they ﬁnd work, and who ﬁnd work
only so long as their labour increases capital. These labourers, who
must sell themselves daily, are a commodity, like every other product
on the market. Because of that they are exposed to all the changing
conditions caused by competition, to all the ﬂuctuations of the market.
Due to the use of machinery and the division of labour into small tasks,
work has lost much of its individuality and its appeal to the labourer.
The person becomes an extension of the machine and it is only the
most simple, most monotonous, and most easily acquired knack that is
required of them. Since there's no craft to learn, the cost of producing
the worker is only the cost of keeping them alive and raising their
replacement. The price of a commodity is only that of producing it, so
the less enjoyable the work becomes, the more the wage paid for it
goes down. Actually, the more machinery and the division of labour
into smaller tasks is used in production, the more the burden on the
worker increases, either because they're forced to work for longer or to
work faster.
Modern Industry has converted the little workshop of the patriarchal
master into the great factory of the industrial capitalist. Masses of
labourers, crowded into the factory, are organised like soldiers. As
privates of the industrial army they are placed under the command of a
perfect hierarchy of oﬃcers and sergeants. Not only are they slaves of
the Capitalist class, and of the capitalist state; they are daily and hourly
enslaved by the machine, by the supervisor, and, above all, by the
individual capitalist themself. The more openly this dictator of capital
proclaims proﬁt to be its aim, the more hateful and easier to resent it is.
The less the skill and strength needed in labour, in other words, the
more modern industry is developed, the more women replace men in
the workforce. Diﬀerences of age and sex have no longer any useful
meaning for the working class. All are instruments of labour, more or
less expensive to use, according to their age and sex. (In the time that
Marx was writing, it was common to openly pay women less than men

and to hire children for lower wages still.)
As soon as the worker has ﬁnished being exploited in the factory,
they're set upon by other sections of the Capitalist class: the landlord,
the shopkeeper, and pawn broker etc.
The lower section of the middle class — the small tradespeople,
shopkeepers, and retired tradespeople generally, the handicraftsmen
and peasants — all these sink gradually into the proletariat. Partly it's
because the small amount of capital they have isn't enough to compete
with modern large-scale industry. Partly it's because their specialised
skills have been made worthless by the new methods of production. In
this way, people from all classes of society become proletarian. (At the
time of capitalism developing, the Capitalist class was known as the
middle class, since it was viewed as being between the poor labourers
and the aristocracy.)
The proletariat goes through several stages of development. At its
birth, it begins its struggle with the Capitalist class. To start with, this
struggle is between individual workers and their employers, then by the
workers of a single workplace, then by the workers of an entire trade,
in one place, against the Capitalists who directly exploit them. They
direct their attacks not against the capitalist conditions of production,
but against the instruments of production themselves; they destroy
imported goods that compete with their labour, they smash to pieces
machinery, they set factories on ﬁre, they seek to regain by force the
lost status of the workman of the Middle Ages.
At this stage, the labourers still form a confused mass scattered over
the whole country, and broken up by their mutual competition. To
begin with, when the labourers come together for political ends, it's
always because the Capitalists have organised it. They organise their
workers against the remnants of absolute monarchy, the landowners,
the non-industrial Capitalists, and the petty bourgeois (the small
capitalists who work for themselves and have at most a few
employees.) In this way, the proletariat doesn't ﬁght its enemy,
Capitalism, but ﬁghts the enemy of the Capitalists. The whole historical
movement of this phase is driven by the Capitalists and every victory
won is a victory for their class.
But with the development of industry, the proletariat not only increases
in number; it becomes concentrated in greater masses, its strength
grows, and it feels that strength more. The conditions of life within the
proletariat become equal as the use of machinery takes away the skill
diﬀerence between the workers, and nearly everywhere wages fall to
the same level. The growing competition among the Capitalists, and
the economic crises that result from it, make the wages of the workers
ever more unstable. The improvements of machinery, which get better
at ever faster rates, makes the livelihood of the proletariat more and

more unstable. The conﬂicts between individual workers and individual
Capitalists become more and more like the collisions between two
classes. The workers begin to form trade unions against the
Capitalists; they club together in order to keep up the rate of wages;
they create permanent organisations in order to make preparations
beforehand for these occasional revolts. Here and there, the contest
breaks out into riots.
Now and then the workers are victorious, but only for a time. The real
reward of their battles isn't the immediate result, but the constantly
growing union of the workers. This union is helped by the improved
methods of transportation that are created by modern industry,
meaning that workers in diﬀerent places can stay in contact with each
other. This contact was needed to centralise the many local struggles,
which are all of the same type, into one national struggle between
classes. Thanks to the terrible highways that they had to rely on, the
burghers (free citizens of towns) took centuries to form into a uniﬁed
class, but thanks to the railways, proletarians can do it in only a few
years.
This organisation of the proletarians into a class, and, because of that
into a political party, is continually being taken oﬀ track by the
competition between the workers themselves. But the union between
them returns, even stronger than before. By taking advantage of
diﬀerent interests amongst the Capitalist class, the proletariat
manages to get legislation changed to help its interests. This is how
the ten-hours’ bill was passed in England. (The ten-hours' bill,
otherwise known as The Factory Act 1847, limited the working hours of
women and children aged 13-18 in most textile mills to 10 hours a day,
ﬁve days a week, and eight hours on Saturday.)
The collisions between the classes of the old society help to advance
the cause of the proletariat in many ways. The Capitalist class ﬁnds
itself in constant battle. At ﬁrst with the aristocracy; later on, with those
portions of its own class whose interests are getting in the way of
developing industry. At all times it's in competition with the Capitalist
class of foreign countries. In all these battles, it needs to ask the
proletariat for help, and drags it into the political arena. In this way, the
Capitalist class itself gives the proletariat elements of political and
general education. In other words, it gives the proletariat the weapons
for ﬁghting the Capitalist class itself.
As we have already seen, entire sections of the ruling class are
lowered to the proletariat by the development of industry, or at least
see their privileged positions threatened. These people supply the
proletariat with fresh insights and help them advance.
Finally, at times when the class struggle is reaching a breaking point,
the process of other classes being thrown down into the lower classes

becomes so violent and obvious that a small section of the ruling class
joins the revolutionary class, the class that holds the future in its
hands. Just as in an earlier period when a section of the nobility went
over to the Capitalist class, so now a portion of the Capitalist class
goes over to the proletariat, and in particular, a portion of the Capitalist
ideologists, who have raised themselves to the level of understanding
the historical movement of change as a whole.
Of all the classes that stand face to face with the Capitalist class today,
the proletariat alone is a really revolutionary class. The other classes
decay and ﬁnally disappear in the face of modern industry; the
proletariat is the special and essential result of modern industry.
The lower middle class, the small manufacturer, the shopkeeper, the
artisan, the peasant, all these ﬁght against the Capitalist class, to hold
onto their existence as parts of the middle class. They are not
revolutionary, but conservative. More than that, they are reactionary,
since they try to roll back the wheel of history. If by chance they are
revolutionary, they only become so because they face the immediate
threat of becoming proletarian. They decide to ﬁght for their future
interests as proletarians instead of their current interests as parts of
the middle class.
The “dangerous class”, [lumpenproletariat] the social scum, that
passively rotting mass thrown oﬀ by the lowest layers of the old
society, may, here and there, be swept into the movement by a
proletarian revolution. But its conditions of life usually make it more
likely to be part of a bribed tool of reactionary (anti revolutionary)
intrigue.
(The lumpenproletariat, as Marx thought of it, includes small-time
criminals, beggars, prostitutes, the chronically unemployed, those who
have become unemployable, and similar. Some people today feel that
the term is insulting because it classiﬁes marginalised people as lower
than those able to engage in regular wage work. It seems that Marx's
purpose in grouping these sections of the proletariat together was
because they had diﬀerent conditions of life from the regularly
employed proletariat, and potentially had diﬀerent motivations because
of that. Marx abandoned the term by 1850. During the period of his
career that Marxism properly emerged as a mature school of thought,
the term lumpenproletariat had been replaced with the term “reserve
army of labour”, to show that the group formed a backup labour force.)
Society has already been largely brought into the proletariat, to face its
conditions. The proletarian doesn't own property. The relations of their
family no longer have anything in common with the relations of families
in the Capitalist class. In England, in France, in America, in Germany,
everywhere the proletarian is a slave to modern industry and capital,
and they no longer have a distinct national character. To the

proletarian, law, morality, and religion are just things of the Capitalist
class, which act like traps to enforce Capitalist interests.
Every class that has taken control of society before has imposed its
present economic system on the whole of society. The proletarians
can't take control of the productive forces of society without destroying
their own condition as proletarians — that of propertyless workers who
are dependent on working for a capitalist — and without destroying all
production based on individual private property. They're not trying to
secure their position in a rising economic order, but to destroy what
came before.
All previous historical movements were movements of minorities, or in
the interest of minorities. The proletarian movement is the self-
conscious, independent movement of the immense majority, in the
interest of the immense majority. The proletariat, the lowest section of
our present society, cannot raise itself up without throwing the whole of
society, which rests on the capitalist economic base, up into the air.
Even if the whole capitalist system is its enemy, in practice, the
struggle of the proletariat against the Capitalist class is at ﬁrst a
national struggle. The proletariat of each country must ﬁrst of all settle
matters with its own Capitalist class.
In looking at the most general phases of the development of the
proletariat, we traced the more or less hidden civil war raging within
existing society, up to the point where that war breaks out into open
revolution, and where the violent overthrow of the Capitalist class lays
the foundation for the rule of the proletariat.
Up until now, every form of society has been based on the opposition
of oppressing and oppressed classes. But in order to oppress a class,
you have to make sure that the right conditions are in place for it to be
your slave. In the period of serfdom, the serf raised themself to
membership in the commune, just as under feudal absolutism, the
small capitalist managed to develop into the large capitalist. But
instead of rising with the process of modern industry, the modern
labourer sinks deeper and deeper below their previous level of
existence. They become a pauper, and pauperism develops more
rapidly than the population and wealth. Here it becomes clear that the
Capitalist class is no longer ﬁt to be the ruling class in society, and to
impose its conditions of existence upon society as an overriding law. It
is unﬁt to rule because it isn't able to keep its slaves alive within their
slavery, because it cannot help letting its slaves sink into such a state
that it has to feed them instead of being fed by them. Society can no
longer live under this Capitalist class, in other words, its existence is
no longer compatible with society.
The essential conditions for the existence and for the rule of the
Capitalist class is the formation and growth of capital; the condition for

capital is wage-labour. Wage-labour rests exclusively on competition
between the labourers. The need for proﬁt forces the Capitalist class to
develop industry, and in doing so it brings the workers out of their
isolation and into joint workplaces, where they become a revolutionary
group. Because of this, the development of Modern Industry destroys
the conditions that kept the wage labourer enslaved to the Capitalist
class. Therefore, what the Capitalist class produces, above all, are its
own grave-diggers. Its fall and the victory of the proletariat are equally
inevitable.
 

Chapter 2: Proletarians and Communists

In what relation do the Communists stand to the proletarians as a
whole?
The Communists do not form a separate party opposed to the other
working-class parties.
They have no interests separate and apart from those of the proletariat
as a whole.
They do not set up any sectarian principles of their own, by which to
shape and mould the proletarian movement.
You can tell the Communists apart from the other working-class parties
by this only: 1) In the national struggles of the proletarians of the
diﬀerent countries, they point out and bring to the front the common
interests of the entire proletariat, independently of all nationality. 2) In
the various stages of development which the working class has to go
through in its struggle against the Capitalist class, Communists always
and everywhere represent the interests of the movement as a whole.
The Communists are the most advanced and resolute section of the
working-class parties of every country, that section which pushes
forward all others. They also have the advantage over the great mass
of the proletariat of clearly understanding the line of march, the
conditions, and the ultimate general results of the proletarian
movement.
The aim of the Communists is the same as that of all other proletarian
parties: the formation of the proletariat into a class, the overthrow of
the Capitalist supremacy, and the seizing of political power by the
proletariat.
The theoretical conclusions of the Communists are in no way based on
ideas or principles that have been invented by this or that would-be
reformer. They merely express, in general terms, actual relations of the
existing class struggle, and the historical movement going on under
our very eyes.
The abolition of existing property relations is not at all a unique feature
of communism. Property relations have continually changed
throughout history as conditions have changed. The French
Revolution, for example, abolished feudal property in favour of
capitalist property.
The distinguishing feature of Communism is not the abolition of all
property, but the abolition of capitalist property. But modern capitalist
private property is the ﬁnal and most complete system of producing
and securing products that is based on class oppression, on the
exploitation of the many by the few.
In this sense, the theory of the Communists may be summed up in the

single sentence: elimination of private property.
People have complained that we Communists want to abolish the right
of personally acquiring property from the fruit of a person's own labour.
That property, they say, is the basis of all personal freedom, activity
and independence.
Hard-won, self-acquired, self-earned property! Do they mean the
property of petty artisan and of the small peasant, a form of property
that came before the Capitalist form? There is no need to abolish that;
the development of industry has already mostly destroyed it, and is still
destroying it daily.
Or do you mean the modern capitalist private property?
But does wage-labour create any property for the labourer? Not a bit. It
creates capital, that kind of property which exploits wage-labour, and
which cannot increase unless it gets a new supply of wage-labour to
exploit. Property, in its present form, is based on the antagonism of
capital and wage labour. Let us examine both sides of this antagonism.
To be a capitalist, is to have not only a purely personal, but a social
status in production. Capital is a collective product, and can only be set
in motion by the united action of the whole society. Capital is therefore
not only personal; it is a social power.
When capital is converted into the common property of all members of
society, people don't lose their personal property. It's only capitalist
private property that's converted into property owned socially by all of
society. It loses its class character.
Let us now take wage-labour.
The average price of wage labour is the cost of keeping the labourer
alive and functioning. So what the wage labourer gets from their labour
is merely enough to prolong and reproduce their existence. We don't
mean to get rid of the labourer’s ability to earn a living through their
work, and we don't want to give them the ability to gather wealth so
they can exploit the labour of other people. All we want to do is get rid
of the miserable nature of labour that exists under capitalism, where
the labourer lives only to increase capital and is allowed to live only
insofar as it helps the interest of the ruling class.
In capitalist society, living labour is but a means to increase
accumulated labour. In Communist society, accumulated labour is only
a way to widen, to enrich, and to promote the existence of the
labourer. (In Marxism, the term “accumulated labour” refers to labour
that has already been used to create things useful to society. It is
accumulated in those objects, which couldn't exist without the labour
having been spent on them. Living labour is just what it sounds like:
the active labour of the living worker. So Marx and Engels are saying
here that the only purpose of labour under communism is to improve
the lives of the working class, which is now the whole of society.)

In capitalist society, the present is controlled by the past, (because the
capital produced in the past demands to be used in the present to
create even more capital.) In Communist society, the past increases
the freedom of choice in the present, (because without the laws of
capitalism driving the economy, people can choose to do a wider
range of things with their accumulated wealth.)
In capitalist society, capital is independent and has individuality, while
the living person is dependent on it and has no individuality.
And the capitalist calls ending this state of things taking away
individuality and freedom! Rightly so. The end of capitalist individuality,
of capitalist independence, and of capitalist freedom is our aim.
Under present capitalist conditions, freedom means free trade, the
freedom to buy and sell.
But if selling and buying disappears, free selling and buying also
disappears. This talk about free selling and buying, and all the other
“brave words” of our capitalists about freedom in general, only have
any meaning when contrasting things with the restricted buying and
selling of the Middle Ages. They have no meaning when placed
against the Communistic elimination of buying and selling, of the
capitalist conditions of production, and of the Capitalist class itself.
You are horriﬁed at our intention to do away with private property. But
in your existing society, private property has already been done away
with for nine-tenths of the population. Its existence for the few is wholly
due to its non-existence in the hands of those nine-tenths. So you
criticise us for intending to do away with a form of property that
requires the immense majority of society to have no property.
In one word, you disapprove of us for intending to do away with your
property. Precisely so; that's just what we intend to do.
From the moment when labour can no longer be converted into capital,
money, or rent, into a social power capable of being monopolised, from
the moment when individual property can no longer be transformed
into Capitalist property, into capital, from that moment, you say,
individuality vanishes.
Then you must confess that by “individual” you mean no other person
than the capitalist, than the middle-class owner of property. This
person must, indeed, be swept out of the way and made impossible.
Communism deprives no person of the power to acquire the products
of society; all that it does is to deny them the power to exploit the
labour of others through using what they've acquired.
People have objected when private property is abolished, all work will
end, and universal laziness will take over.
According to this, capitalist society ought long ago to have gone to the
dogs through sheer idleness; because those of its members who work,
acquire nothing, and those who acquire anything do not work. The

whole of this objection is just another way of saying that there can no
longer be any wage-labour when there is no longer any capital.
All objections that have been made against the Communistic mode of
producing and distributing material products, have also been made
against the Communistic mode of producing and distributing
intellectual products. In the same way that, to the capitalist, the
disappearance of class property is the disappearance of production
itself, the disappearance of class culture is identical to them with the
disappearance of all culture itself.
That culture that the capitalist complains about losing is, for the
enormous majority, mere training to act as a machine.
Don't debate with us if you want to apply your capitalist standards of
freedom, culture, and law to our intended abolition of capitalist
property. Your very ideas are just the outgrowth of the conditions of life
created by your capitalist production and capitalist property, just as
your legal philosophy is only the will of your class made into a law for
all to obey. That will is shaped by your existence as capitalists.
Like all previous ruling classes, you present the social forms of the
current economic system and property relations as eternal laws. While
you can see that ancient property relations and feudal property
relations had to end, you cannot admit it about your capitalist property
relations.
Abolition of the family! Even the most radical ﬂare up at this infamous
proposal of the Communists.
What foundation is the present family, the capitalist family, based on?
On capital, on private gain. In its completely developed form this family
exists only among the Capitalist class. But this state of things ensured
the practical absence of the family among the proletarians, and in
public prostitution. (The capitalist family is based on the inheritance of
private property and the maintenance of class rule. Marx and Engels
are saying here that only the families of the Capitalist class are based
on maintaining wealth and power, because the vast majority of society
has no property to pass onto their children. Because of this, members
of the proletarian family have to be continually sold for a wage.)
The capitalist family will vanish as a matter of course when the
proletarian family vanishes, and both will vanish with the vanishing of
capital.
Do you charge us with wanting to stop the exploitation of children by
their parents? To this crime we plead guilty.
You say that we destroy the most sacred of relations when we replace
home education with social education.
But isn't education in capitalist society already inﬂuenced by the social
conditions of the society it exists in? By school curriculums, existing
social attitudes, and the like? The Communists have not given

education a political character, informed by wider society; but we do
seek to change the character of that education, and to rescue
education from the inﬂuence of the ruling class.
The more modern industry progresses and turns families proletarian,
so that they have to sell their children's labour power like any other
commodity, the more disgusting the capitalist claptrap about the
sacred relations of the family becomes.
But you Communists would introduce the common ownership of
women, screams the capitalist in chorus.
The capitalist sees his wife as a mere piece of property used in
production. He hears that the instruments of production are to be
exploited in common, and naturally comes to no other conclusion than
that women are to be exploited by all.
It doesn't even occur to him that the real point we aim at is to do away
with the status of women as mere instruments of production.
For the rest, nothing is more ridiculous than the virtuous indignation of
our capitalist at the common exploitation of women, which they pretend
is to be openly established by the Communists. The Communists have
no need to introduce it, as it has existed for almost as long as recorded
history.
Our capitalist, not content with having wives and daughters of their
proletarians at their disposal, not to speak of common prostitutes, take
the greatest pleasure in seducing each other’s wives.
Capitalist marriage is, in reality, a system of wives in common and so
all the Communists might possibly be accused of is the desire to
introduce openly what is already happening unoﬃcially. As for the rest,
it is obvious that ending the present system of production must also
end the communal use of women which comes from that system, i.e.,
of prostitution both public and private.
The Communists are further criticised for desiring to abolish countries
and nationality.
The working people have no country. We cannot take from them what
they have not got. Since the proletariat must ﬁrst rise to be the leading
class, the dominant political power of their nation, must make
themselves the nation, that far it is itself national, but not in the
capitalist sense of the word.
National diﬀerences and active hostility between peoples are vanishing
more and more because of the development of capitalism, free trade,
the world market, and the similar conditions of life that it gives to
people everywhere.
The supremacy of the proletariat will cause that hostility to vanish even
faster. United action, of the leading developed countries at least, is one
of the ﬁrst conditions for winning the freedom of the proletariat.
The more we put an end to the exploitation of one individual by

another, we will also put an end to the exploitation of one nation by
another. In proportion as the hostile opposition between classes within
the nation vanishes, the hostility of one nation to another will come to
an end.
The charges against Communism made from a religious, a
philosophical and from an ideological standpoint in general don't
deserve serious examination.
Does it require deep intuition to understand that people's ideas, their
views, their consciousness, changes with every change in the
conditions of their material existence, with every change in their social
relations and social life?
History proves that the production and distribution of ideas changes its
character in proportion as material production is changed. The ruling
ideas of each age have always been the ideas of its ruling class.
When people speak of the ideas that revolutionise society, they're only
expressing the fact that within the old society the elements of a new
one have been created, and that the burying of the old ideas keeps
pace with the burying of the old conditions of existence.
When the ancient world was in its last days, the ancient religions were
overcome by Christianity. When Christian ideas were replaced in the
18th century to rationalist ideas, feudal society fought its death battle
with the then revolutionary Capitalist class. The ideas of religious
liberty and freedom of conscience merely gave expression to the
inﬂuence of free competition within the domain of knowledge.
“Undoubtedly,” it will be said, “religious, moral, philosophical, and legal
ideas have been changed in the course of historical development. But
religion, morality, philosophy, political science, and law, constantly
survived this change.”
“There are, besides, eternal truths, such as Freedom, Justice, etc., that
are common to all states of society. But Communism abolishes eternal
truths, it abolishes all religion, and all morality, instead of constituting
them on a new basis; it therefore acts in opposition to all past historical
experience.”
What does this accusation reduce itself to? The history of all past
society has been the history of class struggle, struggle that has taken
diﬀerent forms in diﬀerent eras.
But whatever form they may have taken, one fact is common to all past
ages: the exploitation of one part of society by the other. No wonder,
then, that the social consciousness of past ages, despite all the variety
it displays, keeps within certain common forms, or general ideas,
which cannot completely vanish until the total disappearance of
classes and class struggle.
The Communist revolution is the most radical break with traditional
property relations; no wonder that its development involved the most

radical break with traditional ideas.
But let us have done with the capitalist objections to Communism.
We have seen above, that the ﬁrst step in the revolution by the working
class is to raise the proletariat to the position of ruling class to win the
battle of democracy.
The proletariat will use its political rule to take all capital from the
Capitalist class one piece at a time, and centralise ownership of all
economic instruments of production into the hands of the state; in
other words, in the hands of the proletariat organised as the ruling
class. Then it will increase the productive forces as rapidly as possible.
To begin with, of course, we cannot do this without discarding the
rights of property, and the normal conditions of capitalist production.
That can make our actions seem ineﬃcient and economically
unworkable in the long term. But once started, our actions will force us
to discard the old social order more and more, until we revolutionise
the whole mode of production.
These measures will, of course, be diﬀerent in diﬀerent countries.
Nevertheless, in most advanced countries, the following will pretty
generally apply.
1. Taking state ownership of all land and using rents generated from it
for public purposes.
2. A heavy progressive or graduated income tax.
3. Abolishing all rights of inheritance.
4. Conﬁscating the property of all emigrants and rebels.
5. Centralisation of credit in the hands of the state, by means of a
national bank with State capital and an exclusive monopoly.
6. Centralisation of the means of communication and transport in the
hands of the State.
7. Growing factories and increasing instruments of production owned
by the State; the bringing of waste lands into cultivation, and the
improvement of the soil generally in line with a common plan.
8. Enforcing that all who are able to work do so. Establishing groups of
unemployed people who are available to labour where necessary,
especially for agriculture.
9. Combining agriculture with manufacturing industries; gradually
abolishing all the distinction between town and country by a fairer
distribution of the population over the country.
10. Free education for all children in public schools. Abolition of
children’s factory labour in its present form. Combining education with
industrial production.
When class distinctions have disappeared and all production has been
concentrated in the hands of the whole nation, the public power will
lose its political character. Political power is merely the organised
power of one class for oppressing another. If its struggle against the

Capitalist class forces the proletariat to organise itself as a class, and if
it makes itself the ruling class through a revolution and sweeps away
the old conditions of production by force, then it will have swept away
the conditions that make class struggle possible, and will have
destroyed classes generally. In doing so it will have abolished its own
supremacy as a class.
In place of the old capitalist society, with its classes and hostile
opposition of class interests, we shall have an association, in which the
free development of each is the condition for the free development of
all.

Chapter 3: Socialist and Communist Literature

Reactionary Socialism

Feudal Socialism
Thanks to their historical position in society, the aristocrats of France
and England took to writing pamphlets against modern capitalist
society. In the French Revolution of July 1830, and in the English
reform agitation that aimed to weaken the position of the aristocracy,
these two countries’ aristocracies again lost to the Capitalist class that
they hated. After that, a serious political struggle was altogether out of
the question. A literary battle alone remained possible. But even in the
realm of writing, the old cries from the Stuart restoration of the
monarchy had become impossible.
In order to gain sympathy for its views, the aristocracy had to pretend
to lose sight of its own interests and to formulate their argument
against the Capitalist class from the view of helping the exploited
working class alone. So, the aristocracy took their revenge by creating
harsh satires of their new masters and whispering in their ears scary
predictions of coming disaster.
In this way arose feudal Socialism: half an expression of grief, half
ridicule; half an echo of the past, half dire warning about the future; at
times, by its bitter, witty and cutting criticism, striking the Capitalist
class to the very heart’s core; but always ridiculous in its eﬀect,
because of its total inability to understand the march of modern history.
The aristocracy, in order to rally the people to them, waved the
proletarian begging-bag in front as its banner. But the people, just as
often as it joined them, saw on their hindquarters the old feudal coats
of arms, and deserted them with loud and disrespectful laughter.
One section of the French Legitimists and “Young England” are an
example of just this.
In pointing out that their method of exploiting the workers was diﬀerent
to that of the Capitalist class, the feudalists forget that they exploited
under circumstances and conditions that were quite diﬀerent than
those that exist today. In showing that, under their rule, the modern
proletariat never existed, they forget that the modern Capitalist class is
the natural outgrowth of their own form of society.
As for the rest, they do so little to conceal the reactionary character of
their criticism that their chief accusation against the Capitalist class is
this: that under the capitalist regime, a class is being developed which
is destined to completely uproot the old order of society.
What they scold the Capitalist class with is not so much that it creates

a proletariat as that it creates a revolutionary proletariat.
In political practice, then, they join in all coercive measures against the
working class; and in ordinary life, despite their pompous phrases, they
stoop to pick up the rewards created by industry, and to barter truth,
love, and honour, for trade in wool, beetroot, sugar, and potato spirits.
Just as the parson has always gone hand in hand with the landlord, so
has Clerical Socialism with Feudal Socialism.
Nothing is easier than to give Christian asceticism a Socialist tinge.
Has not Christianity protested against private property, against
marriage, against the State? Has it not preached that charity and
poverty, celibacy and mortiﬁcation of the ﬂesh, monastic life and
Mother Church should take the place of them? Christian Socialism is
but the holy water with which the priest makes the heartburn of the
aristocrat religious.
Petit Bourgeois Socialism
The feudal aristocracy was not the only class that was ruined by the
Capitalist class, not the only class whose conditions of existence
suﬀered a physical decline and perished in the atmosphere of modern
Capitalist society. The medieval burgesses (free citizens of towns) and
the small peasant proprietors were the starting point of the modern
Capitalist class. In those countries which are only a little developed,
industrially and commercially, these two classes still sit passively side
by side with the rising Capitalist class.
In countries where modern society has become fully developed, a new
class of petit bourgeois (small capitalist) has been formed, ﬂuctuating
between the proletariat and Capitalist class, and continually renewing
itself as an additional part of Capitalist society. The individual members
of this class, however, are being constantly hurled down into the
proletariat by the action of competition. And, as modern industry
develops, they even see the moment approaching when they will
completely disappear as an independent section of modern society, to
be replaced in manufactures, agriculture and commerce, by
supervisors, bailiﬀs and shopkeepers.
In countries like France, where the peasants make up far more than
half of the population, it was natural that writers who sided with the
proletariat against the Capitalist class should use, in their criticism of
the capitalist regime, the standard of the peasant and petit bourgeois.
And from the standpoint of these intermediate classes, it was natural
that they should take up battle for the working class. Like that, petit-
bourgeois Socialism arose. Sismondi was the head of this school, not
only in France but also in England.
This school of Socialism was insightful in its assessment of the
contradictions present in modern production. It laid bare the
hypocritical apologies of economists. It proved undeniably the

disastrous eﬀects of machinery and division of labour; that capital
concentrates in the hands of a few; and that capitalist production leads
to frequent crises that are brought on by overproduction. It pointed out
the inevitable ruin of the petit bourgeois and peasant, the miserable
conditions it creates for the proletariat, and the chaotic and unstable
nature of an economy based on capitalist competition. It laid bare the
huge inequalities in the distribution of wealth, the industrial war of
extermination between nations, and the breaking apart of old moral
bonds, of the old family relations, and of the old nationalities.
In its goals, however, this form of Socialism aims to either restore the
old means of production and of exchange, and with them the old
property relations, and the old society, or to place the modern means
of production and of exchange within the framework of the old property
relations, which can't handle them without tearing apart. In either case,
it is both reactionary and Utopian.
Its last words are: corporate guilds for manufacture; patriarchal
relations in agriculture.
Ultimately, when stubborn historical facts had destroyed all heady self-
deception, this form of Socialism ended in despair.
German or “True” Socialism
The Socialist and Communist literature of France, a literature that
originated under the pressure of a Capitalist class in power, and that
was the expressions of the struggle against this power, was introduced
into Germany at a time when the Capitalist class, in that country, had
just begun its contest with feudal absolutism.
German philosophers, would-be philosophers, and educated men,
eagerly seized on this literature, only forgetting that when these
writings immigrated from France into Germany, French social
conditions had not immigrated along with them. Under German social
conditions, this French literature lost all its immediate practical value
and became something of purely literary value. So, the demands of the
French Revolution and its ideals were adopted by the eighteenth
century German, who treated these ideas as universally valid, and not
springing from the speciﬁc conditions of the French Capitalist class
during that time.
The work of the German scholars consisted solely in bringing the new
French ideas into harmony with their own ancient philosophical values,
or rather, in taking on the French ideas without letting go of their own
philosophical point of view.
This adoption of ideas took place in the same way in which a foreign
language is appropriated, namely, by translation.
It is well known how the monks wrote silly lives of Catholic Saints over
the manuscripts on which the classical works of ancient heathendom
had been written. The German scholars reversed this process with the

unholy French literature. They wrote their philosophical nonsense
beneath the French original. For instance, beneath the French criticism
of the economic functions of money, they wrote “Alienation of
Humanity”, and beneath the French criticism of the capitalist state they
wrote “Dethronement of the Category of the General”, and so forth.
The introduction of these philosophical phrases at the back of the
French historical criticisms, they called “Philosophy of Action”, “True
Socialism”, “German Science of Socialism”, “Philosophical Foundation
of Socialism”, and so on.
The French Socialist and Communist literature was thus robbed of all
value. And, since the Germans had taken all expression of class
struggle out of the literature, they felt aware of having overcome
“French one-sidedness” and not of presenting needs of action, but of
uncovering eternal truth. They weren't presenting the interests of the
proletariat, but the interests of Human Nature, of Man in general, who
belongs to no class, has no reality, who exists only in the fantasy realm
of the philosopher.
This German socialism, which took its schoolboy task so seriously and
solemnly, and sold its poor ideas in such a deceptive fashion,
gradually turned from naivety to become consciously reactionary.
The ﬁght of the Germans, and especially of the Prussian Capitalist
class, against feudal aristocracy and absolute monarchy, in other
words, the liberal movement, became more committed to actually
overthrowing the old order.
Through this, the long-wished for opportunity was oﬀered to “True”
German Socialism of confronting the political movement with the
Socialist demands, of hurling the traditional denouncements against
liberalism, against representative government, against capitalist
competition, capitalist freedom of the press, capitalist legislation,
capitalist liberty and equality, and of preaching to the masses that they
had nothing to gain, and everything to lose, by this capitalist
movement. German Socialists forgot that the French criticism, whose
silly echo it was, presupposed the existence of modern capitalist
society, with its economic conditions and political constitution — the
very things that the Germans were still struggling to achieve.
To the absolute governments, with their following of parsons,
professors, country squires, and oﬃcials, this criticism served as a
welcome weapon to scare the Capitalist class with.
At the same time, these governments placated the workers with
reforms after having beaten down their movements with brutality and
bullets.
While this “True” Socialism thus served the government as a weapon
for ﬁghting the German Capitalist class, it, at the same time, directly
represented a reactionary interest, the interest of German Philistines.

In Germany, the petit bourgeois class, a relic of the sixteenth century,
and since then constantly cropping up again under the various forms,
is the real social basis of the existing state of things.
To preserve this class is to preserve the existing state of things in
Germany. The industrial and political supremacy of the Capitalist class
threatens it with certain destruction — on the one hand, from the
concentration of capital; on the other, from the rise of a revolutionary
proletariat. “True” Socialism appeared to kill these two birds with one
stone. It spread like an epidemic.
The German Socialists sold their sorry “eternal truths” with ﬁne words
and high sounding ideas and speculations, to make them attractive to
the public.
And on its part German Socialism recognised, more and more, its own
calling as the loud and conﬁdent representative of the petit bourgeois
Philistine.
It proclaimed the German nation to be the model nation, and the
German petty Philistine to be the typical person. It gave a hidden,
higher, Socialistic interpretation to every selﬁsh and reactionary trait,
the exact contrary of its real character. It went to the extreme length of
directly opposing the “brutally destructive” tendency of Communism,
and of proclaiming its supreme and impartial contempt of all class
struggles. With very few exceptions, all the so-called Socialist and
Communist publications that now (in 1847) circulate in Germany
belong to the domain of this foul and lifeless literature.

Conservative or Bourgeois Socialism

A part of the Capitalist class wants to address social complaints in
order to secure the continued existence of capitalist society.
This is includes the economists*, philanthropists, humanitarians,
improvers of the condition of the working class, organisers of charity,
members of societies for the prevention of cruelty to animals, sobriety
fanatics, and reformers of every kind imaginable. This form of
socialism has even been worked out into complete systems. (*This is
referring to economists in the sense that we use the word today, not
people who follow the political thought of economism, which is
discussed in other marxist works.)
Proudhon's Philosophy of Poverty is one form of this.
The Socialistic capitalist wants all the advantages of modern social
conditions without the struggles and dangers that result from it. They
desire the existing state of society, minus the elements that are
revolutionary and pull it apart. They wish for a Capitalist class without a
proletariat. The Capitalist class naturally understands the world in
which it rules to be the best one; and Capitalistic Socialism develops

this comfortable idea into various more or less complete systems. In
requiring the proletariat to carry out such a system, which marches
them into a social utopia, it requires that the proletariat remains within
the limits of existing society, and that it should cast aside all its hateful
ideas concerning the Capitalist class.
A second, and more practical, but less systematic, form of this
Socialism sought to cast shade on every revolutionary movement in
the eyes of the working class, by showing that no mere political reform,
but only a change in the material conditions of existence, in economic
relations, could improve their situation. While seeking changes in the
material conditions of existence, this form of Socialism doesn't
understand the need to abolish capitalist relations of production — an
abolition that can be aﬀected only by a revolution. Instead it advocates
for administrative reforms, based on the continued existence of
capitalist relations of production. These reforms in no way aﬀect the
relations between capital and labour, but at the best lessen the cost,
and simplify the administrative work, of capitalist government.
Bourgeois Socialism reveals its true, toothless nature when it becomes
a mere ﬁgure of speech.
Free trade: for the beneﬁt of the working class. Protective duties: for
the beneﬁt of the working class. Prison Reform: for the beneﬁt of the
working class. This is the last word and the only seriously meant word
of bourgeois socialism.
It is summed up in the phrase: the capitalist is a capitalist — for the
beneﬁt of the working class.

Critical-Utopian Socialism and Communism

We're not referring here to that literature which, in every great modern
revolution, has always given voice to the demands of the proletariat,
such as the writings of Babeuf.
The ﬁrst direct attempts of the proletariat to reach its own goals, made
in times of universal upheaval, when feudal society was being
overthrown, predictably failed, because of the undeveloped state of the
proletariat as well as the unsuitable economic conditions for its
liberation. These conditions that had yet to be produced, could only be
produced by the coming Capitalist epoch. The revolutionary literature
that accompanied these ﬁrst movements of the proletariat of course
had a reactionary character. It preached universal frugality and social
levelling in its crudest form.
The Socialist and Communist systems of Saint-Simon, Fourier, Owen,
and others, sprung into existence during the struggle between the
proletariat and Capitalist class in the early, undeveloped period which
we described above.

The founders of these systems see the hostile opposition between
classes as well as the action of the existing classes falling apart in their
current form of society. But the proletariat, as yet in its infancy, looks to
them like a class without any historical initiative or any independent
political movement.
Since the development of class hostilities develop at the same pace as
the development of industry, the economic situation, as they ﬁnd it,
doesn't seem to oﬀer any chance for the proletariat to liberate itself yet.
These Socialists therefore search after a new social science, after new
social laws, that are able to create these conditions.
Instead of historical development leading to change, these Socialists
thought they would invent the new society themselves. Instead of
conditions developing that would allow the workers to liberate
themselves, these Socialists would do it with their wild plans. The
gradual, spontaneous development of the proletariat into an organised
class would be replaced with a society invented by these creators. In
their eyes, the future would be shaped by them propagandising and
putting their plans for society into eﬀect.
In forming their plans, they care chieﬂy for the interests of the working
class, since it is the most suﬀering class. Only from the point of view of
being the most suﬀering class does the proletariat exist for them.
The undeveloped state of the class struggle, as well as their own
surroundings, causes Socialists of this kind to consider themselves far
superior to all hostile opposition between classes. They want to
improve the condition of every member of society, even that of the
most favoured. So, they habitually appeal to society at large, without
the distinction of class; no, they even appeal primarily to the ruling
class. For how can people, once they understand their system, fail to
see that it's the best possible plan to create the best possible society?
So, these Socialists reject all political, and especially all revolutionary
action. They want to reach their goals, which are necessarily doomed
to failure, by peaceful means and by the force of their example.
Such fantasy-like pictures of future society, painted at a time when the
proletariat is still in a very undeveloped state and has only a wild view
of its own position, matches with the ﬁrst instinctive yearnings of that
class for a general reordering of society.
But these Socialist and Communist publications also contain some
critiques. They attack every principle of existing society. Because of
this, they are full of the most valuable materials for the education of the
working class. The practical measures proposed in them — such as
the elimination of the distinction between town and country, of the
family, of private individuals doing business for proﬁt, and of the wage
system, the announcement of social harmony, the conversion of the
function of the state into a mere manager of production — all these

proposals point solely to the disappearance of hostile class oppositions
which were, at that time, only just cropping up. These publications
recognised these problems only in their earliest indistinct and
undeﬁned forms. Because of that, the solutions they put forward are of
a purely Utopian character.
The signiﬁcance of Critical-Utopian Socialism and Communism lost
signiﬁcance as the historical development of society continued. In
proportion as the modern class struggle develops and takes deﬁnite
shape, these fantasy ﬁlled attacks on it lose all practical value and all
theoretical justiﬁcation. So, although the creators of these socialist
systems were in many ways revolutionary, their disciples have formed
mere reactionary sects. They hold onto all the original views of their
masters, ignoring the progressive historical development of the
proletariat. They constantly try to deaden the class struggle and to
reconcile the opposition between classes. They still dream of
experimental realisation of their social Utopias, of founding isolated
colonies, and to complete all these castles in the sky, they have to
appeal to the feelings and purses of the Capitalist class. By degrees,
they sink into the category of the reactionary or conservative Socialists
depicted above, diﬀering from these only by more systematic
obsession with their own ideas, and by their fanatical and superstitious
belief in the miraculous eﬀects of their social science.
They, therefore, violently oppose all political action on the part of the
working class; such action, according to them, can only result from
blind unbelief in their program.
The Owenites in England, and the Fourierists in France, respectively,
oppose the Chartists and the Réformistes.
 

Chapter 4: Position of the Communists in Relation
to the Various Existing Opposition Parties

Chapter 2 has made the relations of the Communists to the existing
working-class parties clear, such as the Chartists in England and the
Agrarian Reformers in America.
The Communists ﬁght for the immediate aims, for imposing the
momentary interests of the working class; but they also represent and
take care of the future of their movement. In France, the Communists
ally with the Social-Democrats against the conservative and radical
Capitalist class, but insist on the right to take up a critical position in
regard to phrases and dreams traditionally handed down from the
great Revolution.
In Switzerland, they support the Radicals, without losing sight of the
fact that this party consists of hostile elements, partly of Democratic
Socialists, in the French sense, and partly of radical Capitalists. (The
Democratic Socialists “in the French sense” are roughly equivalent to
modern Democratic Socialists. They were reformers who wanted
socialist economic reforms.)
In Poland, they support the party that insists on an agrarian revolution
as the prime condition for national emancipation, that party which
fomented the insurrection of Cracow in 1846.
In Germany, they ﬁght alongside the Capitalist class whenever it acts
in a revolutionary way against the absolute monarchy, the feudal
squirearchy (class of landowners), and the petit bourgeoisie.
But they never cease, for a single instant, to instill into the working
class the clearest possible recognition of the opposing class interests
between Capitalist class and proletariat. That way the German workers
can immediately use the social and political conditions that the
Capitalist class has to introduce as weapons against them when the
battle against the reactionary classes is won. The workers can begin
the battle against the Capitalist class straight away.
The Communists turn their attention primarily to Germany, because
that country is on the eve of a Capitalist (i.e a liberal) revolution that is
sure to be carried out under more advanced conditions of social
development and with a much more developed proletariat than that of
England was in the seventeenth, or France in the eighteenth century.
Also because the Capitalist revolution in Germany will be immediately
followed by a proletarian revolution.
In short, the Communists everywhere support every revolutionary
movement against the existing social and political order of things.
They bring the question of property ownership to the foreground in all
of these movements, no matter what its degree of development at the

time.
Finally, they work everywhere for the union and agreement of the
democratic parties of all countries.
The Communists refuse to conceal their views and aims. They openly
declare that their ends can only be achieved by the forcible overthrow
of all existing social conditions. Let the ruling classes tremble at a
Communistic revolution. The proletarians have nothing to lose but their
chains. They have a world to win.
