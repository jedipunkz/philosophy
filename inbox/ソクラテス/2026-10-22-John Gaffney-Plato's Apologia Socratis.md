There follows, in parallel columns, the original Ancient Greek
of Plato’s ’AzroAoyia Xwxparous (Apologia Socratis, The Apology of
Socrates) and a translation into English. The word azroAoyia is the
Ancient Greek technical term for the defense presented at trial by
the defendant, and Plato’s Apology is a vivid account of Socrates’s
defense at his trial in 399Bc. Plato knew Socrates well and was
present at the trial, and we are among those who believe his ac-
count to be historically accurate, at least in the same sense that
the speeches in Thucydides are historically accurate. Certainly it
is true to the character of Socrates. One of Plato’s earliest di-
alogues, it provides our clearest and most compelling picture of
Socrates the man, of his extraordinary character, integrity, intel-
lect, and devotion to moral philosophy and right living. We were
fortunate to have first read it in the original Greek in Roger Bag-
nall’s introductory course at Columbia University and have reread
it countless times in the nearly fifty years since.* Plato’s Apology
has been our guide through life — ¢iAocodia Biouv KuBepv7jtys. We
hope that you will find it equally compelling and meaningful.

*We have no pretensions to scholarship, nor to any expertise beyond that
of a devoted amateur fortunate enough to have had the opportunity to teach
various levels of Greek over the years, often including Plato’s Apology. The
flaws and failings in this work are of course entirely our own and in spite of

The Greek and English texts are formatted side by side, para-

The translator’s task is never an easy one; translation is not an
exact science. We strive to be true to the meaning and spirit of the
Greek while conforming to it as closely as possible. We embarked
on the endeavor hoping for the sake of the parallel presentation to
be nearly uniformly literal, but, having had to abandon that naive
effort in despair after just the first two Stephanus pages, have more
often than we would like found it necessary to deviate to a lesser
or greater degree from that ideal in order to accurately convey the
import of the Greek and to be true to Socrates’s cogently plain
and straightforward manner of speaking. We have, however, still
sought to avoid the gratuitous deviations from literal that seem
common among translations: great works should be translated
with humility; to attempt to improve on them is arrogance.

We were motivated to write this translation by the feeling that
all the translations with which we were familiar failed to rise to the
original. And while the result of our efforts is yet another transla-
tion that fails to rise to the original, we hope that the venture will
be justified and rendered worthwhile by the footnotes, which are
intended to give the reader a much fuller understanding of what
the Greek actually says than the usual one-size-fits-all translations
that opaquely provide only the single phrasing on which the trans-
lator settled.

This ends the defense presented by Socrates, following which the
jury convicts him 280 to 220. There now follows the antitimesis:
when the sentence for a case was not prescribed by law, both the
prosecution and the defense would propose sentences, and the jury
would decide which to impose. This part of Socrates’s defense often
seems arrogant and flippant on a first reading of the Apology, and
certainly it struck the jurors as insulting — the vote to condemn
was considerably more lopsided than the vote to convict. But to
have proposed any sort of punishment would have been implicitly
acknowledging the validity and legitimacy of the proceedings and
charges brought by his accusers, when, as he himself explains, he
was actually doing a great public service in his mission on behalf of
the oracle, and in forgoing a livelihood for the benefit of the public
he merited public support. He was, of course, as fully aware that
his proposal would result in his being condemned as he was that he
would be convicted. But while audacious and unyielding, Socrates
was being neither arrogant nor flippant; his honesty, integrity, and
devotion to what was right would not have allowed him to propose
otherwise. Socrates’s reasoning behind his conduct of his defense
and the antitimesis, and the reasonableness and rightness of that
conduct, will become much more clear in his discussion of the
“divine sign” (76 tod §e068 oypetiov) and his explanation of events
at the end of the dialogue (starting at 38c and particularly from
39e).

A note on participles.

Whereas English has only two participles, Greek has, depend-
ing on how one counts degenerate morphologies, ten or twelve, and
makes correspondingly extensive use of them, very frequently with
particles indicating the logical relation of the participial phrase to
the main clause. Often these participial phrases need to be ren-
dered in English as subordinate clauses, with the logical relation
conveyed by the subordinating conjunction. These situations will
occur frequently enough that they will generally be explicitly noted
only when the English rendering differs in some critical way from
the Greek.

A note on the particle ydp.

Occurring 128 times in the text, yap (for) very frequently has
a causal force equivalent to since or because, but from this, as the
reader may glean from the 57 pages that Denniston devotes to the
particle yap, derive a great many senses and uses of varied shades.
Very common among these is the epexegetic yap (Denn., ydp II
& III), which, as perhaps best explained by Smythe (§2808), “has
the force of now, namely, that is, for example .... It usually intro-
duces, as an explanation, the details of that which was promised
in an incomplete or general statement; sometimes, without any
such statement, it introduces a new fact.” In spite of Smythe’s
admonition that it is usually not to be translated, we have more
often than not been unable to resist the temptation to render it
explicitly, usually as for, even at the price of some awkwardness in
English.

A note on deictic iotas.

An iota suffix, called the deictic iota, can be added to demon-
stratives in Greek to indicate that the person is present and is
being pointed out: for example, odtos = this one, this person, but
ovtooi = this one here, this person here, or simply this one or this
person, with the reader imagining the speaker pointing out the
person in question. Plato makes frequent use of the deictic iota
to indicate that a person was present at the trial. The deictic
iota is also sometimes used more figuratively to convey immediacy
and emphasis in the same way as italics in written and emphatic
intonation in spoken English.
