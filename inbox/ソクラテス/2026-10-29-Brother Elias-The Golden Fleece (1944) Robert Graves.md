INTRODUCTION

A BALLAD-CYCLE about Jason’s Argonauts and their voyage to recover
the Golden Fleece from King Ae&tes of Colchis seems to have been
current in Greece at a very early date, but every line of it has been lost,
unless perhaps some passages survive as interpolations in Homer’s Odyssey.
Homer and Hesiod, the two earliest Greek poets whose works are still
extant, both knew of the voyage, and Homer speaks of it as “on every-
one’s lips." Subsequent references occur in the fragmentary works of
Eumelus (eighth century B.C.) and of Mimnermus, Simonides, Heca-
taeus, Herodorus, and Acusilaüs (seventh and sixth century). In the fifth
century the Athenians Aeschylus, Sophocles, and Euripides all took inci-
dents from the cycle as dramatic themes; Herodotus refers to it several
times in his universal history; Pherecydes of Athens, in his mythological
work, which has survived only in short quotations, was perhaps the
first writer to record the story as a consecutive whole; and the Boeotian
poet Pindar in his Fourth Pythian Ode (462 B.c.) gives a brief summary
of it, the earlicst to survive complete.

Apollonius Rhodius in the third century B.C. wrote an Argonautic epic.
In the Royal Library at Alexandria he had access to the works of Pindar,
Pherecydes, and several obscure writers on the same subject, whom his
scholiast cites. I find him not only the most useful of the main authorities
but the most pleasant to read. The poet Theocritus, an Alexandrian
contemporary, supplements this epic with fuller accounts, in his Idylls, of
the boxing match between Amycus and Pollux, and of the loss of Hylas.
Apollodorus, the Athenian grammarian, in a dry but trustworthy sum-
mary of ancient legends, called the Library, written about 140 B.c.,
supplics important details not otherwise known; so do Hyginus the
fabulist, who was librarian to the Emperor Augustus, and his con-
temporary the poet Ovid, author of the Metamorphoses. Diodorus of
Sicily, the historian, a contemporary of Julius Caesar, makes shrewd
critical comments on the story as he knew it; so do Strabo, Dionysus,
and Pausanias, commonsense geographers who wrote under the early
Emperors.

The first surviving Argonautic epic in Latin was composed about
A.D. 93 by Valerius Flaccus, a Roman priest belonging to the College
which had charge of the Sibylline books and which was responsible to
the Emperor for the purity of religious ritual. He writes heavily and
insincerely, but is well informed, and it is a pity that he did not quite
finish the story. Obviously ancient tradition about the Argonauts and
the Fleece keeps cropping up throughout the centuries until as late as
the eleventh century A.D. when John Tzetzes, the temperamental Byzan-
tine, wrote his rambling Chiliads and, with the help of his brother Isaac,
a commentary on the Cassandra of Lycophron; and when Suidas com-
piled his Lexicon of Ancient Authors.

All these authorities disagree with one another on countless points.
Contradictions even occur between different works written by the same
author. Thus Sophocles, telling in his Colchides how Jason murdered
Apsyrtus, Medea’s brother, places the murder in Colchis; but in another
play, the Scythae, p. it somewhere in Scythia. Ovid places the same
murder at Tomi, his place of exile, which lies some distance south of the
Danube delta; Apollonius, on the island of Artemis off the northern
mouth of the Danube; Hyginus, on the island of Apsyrtus at the head
of the Adriatic gulf. Some writers make Apsyrtus a mere child whom
his sister Medea carried off as a hostage; some make him a grown man
sent in pursuit of her. Diodorus and the Roman orator Cicero call him
Aegialus, not Apsyrtus; Apollonius mentions that he was somctimes
known as Phaéthon; and his mother is variously named Ipsia, Idyia,
Asterodeia, Hecate, Neaera, and Eurylyte.

The nominal rolls of the Argo’s crew given by Apollonius, Hyginus,
Apollodorus, Valerius Flaccus, and Tzetzes are irreconcilable, and still
more names occur in other authors besides these. This diversity of detail
is typical of the whole corpus of Argonautic legend and justifies me in
choosing for my own account whatever version of any incident makes
the best sense, and even occasionally in improvising where a gap cannot
be bridged by existent materials. For example, I have rejected the
improbable account of Herodotus that Jason, before making the voyage
from Iolcos, circumnavigated the Peloponnese in order to consult the
oracle at Delphi. Obviously the oracle of Dodona, not Delphi, was his
objective. He could have gone to Delphi by land much more safely
and speedily. And tbis voyage, which Herodotus has muddled up with
the homeward voyage of the Argo, was doubtless the occasion of Jason's
adventure with the sea-monster, for which there is no cvidence in
literature, but plenty on red-figured vases.

William Morris published his Life and Death of Jason in 1867, but
English verse-epics are out of fashion and Morris's is now seldom read.
Most English-speaking people have therefore come across the story
only in the nursery prose of Charles Kingsley or Nathaniel Hawthorne.
Those who have read Classics at school are (to judge from my own casc)
little better off, since the Argonautica of Apollonius does not figure in
the ordinary Classical curriculum. They probably know something,
from the Medea of Euripides, about Jason’s life at Corinth after the
voyage was over, and have gathered a few further details of the voyage
itself from Pindar’s decorative ode, but the voyage still has for them the
same timeless, ungeographical, inconsequent charm as the nursery-rhyme
voyages of the Owl and the Pussy-Cat, the Jumblies, and the Three
Wise Men of Gotham. A reading of the Life and Death of Jason will do
little to break the charm. Morris was a pre-Raphaclite romantic; that
is to say, the more mysterious and even nonsensical he found a legend,
the more poetic he considered it. Rejecting the obvious tradition that
the Argo returned to Greece from her Black Sea voyage by the way that
she came, namely the Bosphorus, he wilfully follows the most fantastic

account available—the one given in the anonymous Argonautica Orphica,
written about the year 350 A.D.—which sends her up an unidentifiable
Russian river, with the occasional aid of rollers, to the Gulf of Finland
and so home by way of the Baltic, the Channel, Ireland, and the Straits
of Gibraltar. Morris also shows his lack of Classical conscience by
omitting the one incident of the voyage on which all authors agree—
the merry repopulation of Lemnos—I suppose for fear of offending
against Victorian good taste.

The Greeks, on the whole a level-headed people, regarded the voyage
as an historical event which took place about two generations before
the fall of Troy—to which they assigned the reasonable date of 1184 s.c.
The voyage was a good deal nearer in time for Homer and Hesiod than
Columbus's discovery of America is for us; and though in the fifth
century B.C. Thucydides found the story too intractable for inclusion
in the First Book of his history, no Greek, so far as I know, ever ventured
to deny that the Argo was a real ship which sailed from Thessalian Iolcos
to Aea on the Colchian river Phasis—that is to say, from Volo in eastern
Greece to Kutais on the Georgian river Rion—and back again. Classical
experts, it must be admitted, are still dubious about the historicity of the
voyage: they will seldom allow more than that "the story probably
arose out of accounts of commercial expeditions made to the shores of
the Black Sea by wealthy Minyans." But then it is only recently that
archaeological evidence has forced them to take the Minotaur and the
Dictean Cave legends seriously, and to admit the historicity of the
siege of Troy. Since many Homeric characters have fathers connected
with the story of the Argo—for instance, Queen Hypsipyle’s son by
Jason, King Euneus of Lemnos, provided the Greeks with wine during
the siege of Troy, and Peleus the father of Achilles was a prominent
Argonaut—there seems no good reason to doubt that the voyage did
take place, however distorted the extant accounts of it may be, and at
about the time that the Greeks claimed that it did. Eratosthenes, the
Alexandrian scientist who calculated the breadth of a geographical degree
and from it deduced, with remarkable precision, the circumference of
the globe, fixed on 1225 s.c. as the date of Jason’s voyage; but it is not
known exactly how he arrived at either result.

If the voyage was genuine, how is one to account for the supernatural

incidents that attach to the legend ?

Here are several interconnected answers :

(A) The archaic Greeks, like the American Indians, recorded current
events pictograpbically both in art and in poctry, and when by
lapse of time the original meaning of a pictographic composition
was forgotten, a new meaning was invented that satisfied con-
temporary curiosity. A poetic pictograph was called a “myth”
by the Greeks, but the word had no connotation of untruth, as
the adjective “mythical” has in modern English.

(B) The original Argonautic ballad-cycle was never perhaps committed

II

to writing. Some of the travelling bards who carried it about
Greece in their heads would forget some parts of it, some would
forget others, and what was lost would be made good either with
invented material or with incidents patched in from some alto-
gether different story.

(C) Parts of the original ballad-cycle became offensive to the religious
and political susceptibilities of later generations and had therefore
to be altered or omitted.

(D) Satiric or humorous material and accounts of dreams or visions
surviving from the original version may have become incorpor-
ated in later versions as ordinary factual narrative.

(E) From perhaps the fifth century 5.c. onwards, Orphic enthusiasts
used the tradition that Orpheus was an Argonaut as an excuse for
making the voyage allegorical of the Orphic Mysteries.

Before enlarging upon these answers, I must insist that the Greeks were
not romantic in the Victorian sense: they did not wilfully invent non-
sensicalities, though they might preserve a fantastic legend just as it was
handed down, in the confidence that it would make sense if properly
understood. For instance, though many of the poets rejected the
commonsense Homeric account (followed by the sixth-century critic
Herodorus) that the Argo returned through the Bosphorus, and offered
instead queer alternative routes by way of the Danube, Rion, Dniester,
or Don rivers, this was not because they were in love with the mysterious.
My conclusion is, that they rejected it because of a strong pre-Homeric
tradition that the Fleece had not come back to Grcece through the
Bosphorus, but by way of the Danube, the Save, and the Adriatic Sea.
But they found that the Save does not flow into the Adriatic Sea; so
they wrote Po for Save; and when later they found that the lower
Danube was not navigable for more than four hundred miles from the
Delta, because of the Iron Gates, and that the Po does not connect with
the upper Danube, someone suggested that the Argo had sailed up the
Rion; and when it was discovered that the Rion does not flow into the
Caspian Sea and that the Caspian Sea does not connect with the Indian
Ocean, someone else suggested that the Dniester must have been meant ;
and when this was found an equally impossible route, someonc else
suggested. the Don.

The poets, I think, were being stupid. It never occurred to them that
Jason. and Medea with the Fleece might have gone a different way home
from the Argonauts and the Argo—a way that the Argo could not have
taken; must have gone, indeed, because their Sie an murder of
Apsyrtus dace them from re-embarking in the Argo until they
were purified. Since we know that the only person who could purify
them was fair-haired Circe, the dead man's aunt, the Danube is thc
natural route for them to have taken; the amber trade had provided it
with posting facilities and it led to the island of Aeaea, off Istria, at the
head of the Adriatic Sea, where Circe lived. This was the route by

which, after their arrival in South Russia, the straw-wrapped gifts of
"the Hyperboreans" (the people of Britain :) came to Apollo at Delos.
What these gifts were is not known, but they are likely to have included
amber to be used in the worship of Apollo (see Apollonius Rhodius,
Book IV, 611) and were perhaps rewarded with obsidian from Melos.
I place Circe’s island off Istria rather than off the western coast of Italy
(where Odysseus is said to have been entertained by her many years
later), not only because the Istrian island of Apsyrtus figures in the
Argonautic legend, but because of the tradition that Aeaea had once
belonged to Chryses, father of Minyas the Ionian: the Ionians probably
came down from the Danube into Greece by way of Istria—they can
hardly have come by way of Italy. The island is now named Lussin.

(A) Today, the pictographic method survives in art, but is rarely
found in literature. It is still used in Classical or pseudo-Classical designs
—for example, in Town Hall frescoes showing Agriculture and Industry
shaking hands in the presence of a buxom matron with a turreted head-
dress—but the literary language that corresponds with this art-style is
long out of date. A more vigorous use of the pictograph is the news-
paper cartoon; and the only instance that I can recall of a newspaper
cartoon translated into verse, Nicholas Vachel Lindsay’s poem Bryan,
Bryan, Bryan, deserves the attention of all students of Greek mythology.
In recalling his adolescent emotions during William Jennings Bryan’s
campaign for the U.S.A. Presidency in the year 1896, Lindsay mixes
factual statement about himself and his best girl with pictographic
language borrowed from Democratic cartoonists :

In a coat like a deacon

In a black Stetson hat

He scourged the Elephant plutocrats
With barbed wire from the Platte . . .
... plutocrats ...

With dollar signs upon their coats
And spats on their feet.

This is a simple myth to decipher. It is clear that McKinley, Hanna,
and the Republican bosses were scourged only verbally by Bryan, and
that the Elephant is the cartoonists’ symbol for the Republican party,
and that the barbed wire stands for the voting interests of the cattle-
raising South and West—the Platte is in Bryan's Nebraska. It is clear,
too, that the plutocrats seldom wore spats (which symbolized an affec-
tation of English culture) and that the dollar signs on their coats were
imaginatively put there by their opponents as a taunt that moncy was
closer to their hearts than anything else.

The Greek poets used exactly this sort of language, and if Lindsay
had been a sixth-century Greek, the poem would have appeared in the
mythological records of Hyginus and Apollodorus more or less as
follows :

“Lindsaeos of Ochian Parthenie, the dithyrambist, tells of a fabulous

The original meaning of myths and other pictographs is soon forgotten.
For instance, English-speaking parents still repeat to their children the
nursery rhyme about the Lion and the Unicorn who fought for the
Crown; and, though most of them are aware that the Lion and Unicorn
are supporters of the Royal Arms of England and Scotland, there can
be very few who do not dismiss the rhyme as an idle fancy suggested
by the heraldic device. Yet it is almost certainly a pictographic record
of the war in 1650 between England, heraldically represented by the
Lion, and Scotland, heraldically represented by the Unicorn. They
were fighting for the Crown, because the Scots had ambitiously crowned
Charles II as King not only of Scotland, but of England, though England
had proclaimed herself a Commonwealth. (An alternative explanation
may be that the rhyme perpetuates a forgotten quarrel at Edinburgh
between some Lyon King-at-Arms and his Unicorn Pursuivant.)

Diodorus interprets the familiar but mysterious pictograph of the
flying Ram, on whose back Helle and Phrixus rode to Colchis, as a fast
ship with a ram figurehead, and suggests that Helle’s falling down into
the Hellespont merely means that the current made her seasick and
that, as she leant over the gunwale, she tumbled overboard. He is likely
enough to have been right about the ram, though his interpretations of
other parts of the legend are not nearly so plausible. He has obviously
gone astray when he says that the serpent which guarded the Fleece was
an officer whose name happened to be Captain Serpent. So has Dionysius
Scytobrachion when he suggests that the Golden Fleece was really the
skin of young Mr. Ram, Phrixus’s page, which was cured and gilded
by the Colchians after they had killed him. So has Strabo, when he
says the Argonauts were sailing in search of gold and that the Fleece was
the alluvial gold of Colchis which the Colchians collected in fleeces laid
in their tubs at the washings. So has Suidas, when he says that it was a
sheep-skin book containing the secret of alchemy, which the Colchians
had inherited from their Egyptian ancestors. So has Charax of Per-
gamon, who wrote in the first century A.D., when he says that it was a
parchment roll giving the secret of illuminating on gold. Yet all these
Greeks believed the voyage to have taken place historically, and its
wildest symbols to be interpretable in a simple factual way: they did
not resort, as the German scholars of the last century did, to dismissing
the whole story as a solar myth. i

We might, however, have had to fall back on Strabo's fairly plausible
explanation—there is still alluvial gold in the Rion and the fleece method
of gold-washing is a practical one—had not the secret of the story been
let out by Herodotus, Apollodorus, Pausanias, and the anonymous First
Vatican mythographer between them. This mythographer, though
writing in the fifth century a.p., evidently had access to a very early
source of the legend: he is the only authority who mentions the ban of
the Trojans on the Argo’s passage to the Black Sea,! an important element
in the plot. He records that the Fleece was the one “in which Zeus used
to ascend into Heaven,” and thus links it, more securely than does
Pausanias, to the story of the Ram God, Laphystian Zeus, and to the
attempted sacrifice by Athamas the Minyan of his son Phrixus as a means
of persuading Zeus to send rain when the crops had failed. The purple
colour of the Fleece, mentioned by Simonides, emphasizes the connexion :
white clouds do not bring rain, purple ones do ; so with white and purple
fleeces magically used. It appears that Phrixus to avoid being sacrificed
to the Ram God stole the purple Fleece (with the golden fringe :), the
sacred and necessary instrument of the rain-making ritual, and escaped
with it to Colchis, out of reach of pursuit. The loss of the Fleece natur-
ally gave the Minyans a sense of bad luck; and a generation later the
Argonauts, who were all Minyans, either by birth or adoption, sailed
off to bring it back.

(B) It is now thought that one or two even of the most circumstantial
incidents in Homer's Ilíad are interpolations: for example, certain single
combats on the plain before the city of Troy may belong to other ballad-
cycles. pees both Hector, allegedly a Trojan, and Patroclus the
Opian whom he killed, were worshipped as heroes in Boeotia before
Agamemnon’s expedition sailed for Troy; the combat between them
may therefore represent a conflict between the clans of which they were
the founders, rather than between themselves. Thus it is probable that
the Argonautic cycle, which was not stabilized as a written narrative
until long after the Homeric poems, contains even more interpolations.
Many of these are obvious because anachronistic. For instance, Theseus,
who is mentioned as an Argonaut by Apollodorus and others, belongs
to a much earlier period; he is connected with the story of Daedalus,
Icarus, and the sack of Cnossos which occurred about 1500 s.c. And
the Argonauts in 1225 B.C. cannot have offered libations of wine to the
Olympian Gods, as many of the poets record. The Gods were conser-
vative and refused all but "sober sacrifices" until many centuries later.

of the story, but I had to be careful to reject nothing too summarily. I
was at first not sure whether to include Hercules among the Argonauts.

(C) It is not difficult to see why the original meaning of the Golden
Fleece became a mystery to Greeks of the Classical age, granted that the
seizure of the Fleece was an episode in a religious conflict between. the
supporters of the matriarchal Moon Goddess of the “Pelasgians”’ and those

openly of this conflict, which had not yet ended with Zeus’s apparently
complete victory. There are traces of it even in the poems of Homer,
despite their careful editing in sixth-century Athens and third-century
Alexandria: for the bickering between Zeus and Hera is far more than
a satire on the domestic differences in Greek families—it is a conflict

so powerful and the Moon Goddess so reduced, that such kings and
heroes as Salmoneus, Sisyphus, and Tantalus, who had hitherto been
mentioned with respect and enjoyed heroic honours, were regarded as
criminals suffering perpetual punishment in Hell. There has been an
accidental or wilful misunderstanding of all pictographs connected with
the Moon Goddess. For instance, her ritual marriage as Pasiphaë (“She
who Shines for all") with Minos the Sun God, to whom the Bull was
sacred, became obscenely interpreted by Classical writers as a perverted

By Alexandrian times the Greeks had come to dislike making public
confession of practices or beliefs which they had outgrown or of old
scandals which might become precedents for new scandals. They had
hushed up the well-established tales of human sacrifice. Iphigeneia, they
said, was not really sacrificed at Aulis by her father Agamemnon, but
was carried off on a cloud to the Crimea, while a she-bear, or some other
animal, was sacrificed in her place. Helen, they said, did not really run
off to Troy with Paris—his bride was a false Helen, or deceitful wraith,
and the real Helen went to Egypt and there lived virtuously until Mene-
laus came for her again. To say otherwise would have been an insult to
Castor and Pollux, her demi-god brothers. Thus also Pindar, in telling the
story of the seizure of the Fleece from Colchis, breaks off short, with the
excuse that time presses, to avoid giving the horrid details of Jason’s
murder of Apsyrtus : for the ancestor of the man to whom he addressed the
ode was the Argonaut Euphemus of Taenaron, an accessory to the crime.

The Romans were not so squeamish as the Greeks, and the butcher-
shop descriptions of crime and battle that occur in the Argonautica of
Valerius Flaccus have been a useful reminder to me of the true savagery
of the story. But they were on the whole a dull, literal-minded lot, and
pictographs puzzled and sometimes annoyed them. Horace is very
scathing on the subject of Centaurs in one of his Epistles. He asks, who
ever saw a half-human horse: He did not realize that Centaurs, Sile-
nians, Satyrs, and the like were merely Pelasgians, pictographically
identified as belonging to the Horse, Goat, or other totemic fraternities.

Greek priests of the Olympian Gods could always find a ready explan-

"navel-shrine," which continued to be used of the white tomb at Delphi
long after Apollo’s people had killed the oracular python in which the
soul of the tomb’s dead occupant was housed, they explained by declaring
that the shrine lay at the exact centre of Greece, as the navel lies at the
centre of the belly, or the navel-boss at the centre of a shield. This
passed. muster, though Delphi was not in the exact centre of Greece and
two or three other navel-shrines were known of in other parts of the
country. Mycenaean hero-tombs were built of stone in a bee-hive
shape which recalls their African origin, and the truth seems to have
been that they were called navel-shrines because they contained not only
the jawbone and genitals of the hero, but also his navel-string, which
was a visible reminder that he was born of woman, and thus a servant
of the Moon Goddess.

The tides, currents, prevalent winds, landmarks, and anchorages men-
tioned in this book have been checked with ancient and modern
geographies and peripluses, and with the Black Sea and Mediterranean
Pilots. The voyage, as I have logged it, may be challenged in many
details, but at least it was one physically possible for a square-rigged
war-galley to make, with luck and good management, in the required
period of seven months. I reject the tradition recorded by Apollodorus
that it took only four months, unless he is reckoning the time to the
Argo’s first return to home waters and regards the African adventure as
a separate voyage; and the statement of Theocritus that she delayed her
sailing until the rise of the Pleiads. I also reject the account that she had
fifty oars, though vessels as large appear on early Cretan seals; thirty
was plenty for the task assigned to the Argonauts.

The alliance that I postulate between the Colchians and Trojans is
suggested partly by geographical likelihood—Colchis was the gateway

peoples—partly by Herodotus's statement that the carrying-off of Helen

The form into which I have cast the story of the Argonauts is the
historical novel, and a historical novelist should always make it clear
at what vantage-point of time he is standing. Here it would have been
unwise to tell the story as from the thirteenth century B.c.: this would
have meant writing in poetic pictographs. To tell it as from the present
day would not only have meant recording conversation in an unsuitably
modern style, but would have prevented me from believing whole-
heartedly in the story. The only solution was to take up my position
in an age which still believed, but which had achieved the required
critical detachment and a plain but dignified prose style.

I have therefore allowed the phrases “to this day” and "today" to
occur here and there in the book. Historians will gather from the
concluding page that "today" means not later than the 146 s.c. when

remained on show in the temple of Poseidon on the Isthmus ever since
Jason had dedicated her there (with continuous repairs, century after
century), finally disappeared— probably pulled apart by the drunken

ANCAEUS AT THE ORANGE-GROVE

ANCAEUS the Lelegian, of Flowery Samos, was marooned one summer
evening on the sandy southern shore of Majorca, the largest of the
Islands of the Hesperides, or, as some call them, the Islands of the Slingers,

The islanders, astonished by his appearance, refrained from putting him

sandals, short travel-stained tunic, and heavy seaman’s cloak, to the
Chief Priestess and Governess of Majorca, who lived in the cave of the
Draché, the most distant from Greece of the many entrances to the
Underworld.

Since she happened to be preoccupied with some work of divination,
the Chief Priestess sent Ancaeus across the island to be judged and disposed

been initiated into the rites of the Goat fraternity. Three times they
came to places where three ways met, and each time made a wide circuit

enclosures are sacred.
