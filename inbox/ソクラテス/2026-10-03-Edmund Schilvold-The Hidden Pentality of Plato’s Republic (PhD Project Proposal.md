Project abstract

Was Plato the “totalitarian” he has sometimes been made out to be -a
utopian obsessed with the realization of an anti-democratic, theocratic
city state - or was he actually one of the finest and most profound
thinkers of all of human history? Or should he be placed somewhere

between these two extremes?

That is one way to phrase the fundamental question I propose exploring -
and in order to properly explore it, I believe it will be absolutely
necessary to delve deeply into what I perceive to be the “pentality” or

fivefold nature of Plato's Republic, anciently styled the Politeia.

In other words, I should like to embark on an extensive inquiry into why
and how Plato's famous or infamous “Republic” may in fact be viewed as
having no less than five (and certainly several) different levels of

meaning.

Hence, what I have in mind is not a “basic” allegorical reading of the
Politeia, which has, admittedly, been partially carried out before, but a
sophisticated study of multiple distinct and partially “hidden” messages -

and their interconnectedness.

Phrased differently: Is that which is arguably Plato's most important

work being woefully misconstrued by the ordinary interpretations of it?

Edmund Schilvold
February 2024

Project description

Introduction

Research question: Is there a “Pentality” of Levels of Meaning in
Plato's “Republic”, and, if so, what are its nature and its

implications?

As already indicated in the abstract, the proposed project would consist
in a careful, text-focused scrutiny of the several levels of meaning | have
become convinced are present in Plato's longest and most important
dialogue, as well as in an evaluation of Plato's Politeia as a whole, geared
towards determining, in a conclusive manner, precisely how we, in our
day and age, ought to view this exceedingly important and still influential

ancient literary work.

The five levels are, very briefly put, if we consider the five types of
“government” analyzed by Plato, the following: (1) The nature and
operation of a certain faculty or power of the incarnated human being,

posited by Plato himself, such as the suprarational, contemplative power

of Spiritual Sight, styled by Plato “the Eye of the Soul”, (2) the nature
and morphology of a given inner, psychological state, which is not
necessarily permanent, such as “Timocracy” (“Will to Power”-domination)
or “Oligarchy”, (3) the rather obvious, literal level, i.e. the external and,
in a modern sense, truly political kind of ordering signified by the
government in question, (4) the nature, the duties and the tendencies of
a certain social category or class or aggregate, whose existence is viewed
as natural and unavoidable, as it is generated by whatever inclination
dominates the psyche of the individual (and by the correspondence
between the needs of the psyche and those of society at large), and (5)
the character and trajectory of a given eon or age - which, in the ancient
world, was a metaphysically anchored concept - such as one of the five
ages of Hesiod, or one of the four ages forming part of the reckoning

embedded into Indian Dharma or “Hinduism” to this very day.

I would like to emphasize that these five levels, and the multiple ways in
which each of them may be elucidated, are not at all ideas this
contemporary mind of mine is arbitrarily reading into the ancient Greek
text without justification, but concepts which are either explicitly
mentioned by Plato himself, in the work in question, or strongly hinted at,

via one of the interlocutors.

If the prospect of having to square the number five into 25 potential
hermeneutical possibilities in order to fully comprehend an almost two
and a half thousand years old work seems a little surprising, I would
point out that Socrates is made to say, in Politeia 544c-544e, that just as
there are five major kinds of external governments, so there are five
states of the Soul, or five “political” arrangements within (paraphrase).
Moreover, in 368d-369b, Socrates proclaims that the City will be
fashioned for the sake of more easily grasping just what the individual,
psychological State of Justice is (paraphrase) - and these are but two of

numerous such statements and clues in Plato's dialogues.

There are many residual questions and complications, however. For
example, it is not entirely clear if there are actually five “political states”,
and not in fact six (and the question of whether Monarchy is distinct from
Aristocracy or not is indeed asked inside of the “Republic” itself), nor
how all the aspects of the hypothetical City might be reconciled with
allegorical and metaphysical interpretations, nor how all the potential
correspondences between the various governments and faculties and

psychological constellations ought to be mapped out.

Moreover, as far as I can tell, no one has attempted the writing of sucha
comprehensive and penetrating study of Plato's “Republic” as the one I
am now proposing in modern times. Yes, there is a multitude of scattered
books and papers and websites mentioning allegorical and “alchemical”
possibilities, but there is no systematic, conclusive and holistic synthesis
of all the “fives” out there. In fact, it seems that the last or last surviving
treatise to consider several of the hermeneutical vistas I have here set
forth is Proclus' rarely read commentary. Concurrently, the Platonic
corpus has a crucial position as regards both “Western”/European and
Middle-Eastern culture in general and the three so-called Abrahamic
religions in particular, and even forms an essential “companion” to the
reading of modern philosophers like Kant and Nietzsche.

It is high time that we begin to properly understand it, and stop

misconstruing it. That is why the proposed thesis needs to be written.

State of the Art

As I imagine it, the proposed thesis would form a natural and important
continuation of my master's thesis, which, although I wrote it in c. four
months, was actually based on c. half a year of intensive research, as well
as about a decade of taking a strong interest in both Platonic philosophy

and Hellenic and Greco-Roman culture and history in general.

Furthermore, although it is now almost five (!) years since I completed
my thesis on the concept of Nous, I have been anything but idle, and
have spent more than a thousand hours on the reading of the parts of the
Platonic corpus I had not yet read in 2020, examining various excerpts in
various translations, and in the original Greek, perusing various ancient
and modern commentaries - such as Proclus’ seminal magnum opus, “On
the Theology of Plato” and Rafael Ferber’s and Gregor Damschen’s
inquiries into the ontological status of Plato's Idea of the Good - and so
on and so forth.

Although this research of mine has not yet manifested itself in peer-
reviewed academic papers, I have written a 24-page essay on Plato's
conception of the Supreme Deity, called “The Nature of Plato’s Good
Revealed: Platonic Theology and Its Relation to Christianity and
Judaism”, and I have also participated in several online discussions with

other academics and people interested in Plato.

The contemporary researcher who comes the closest to articulating my
five-faceted view of the Politeia is probably an American psychologist and
statistician named John Uebersax, who has published a series of
insightful articles on his two websites, “Christian Platonism” and

“Satyagraha”.

There are others, however. One Dr. Edward P. Butler wrote a journal
article in 2014, “Esoteric City: Theological Hermeneutics in Plato’s
Republic”, which constitutes an intriguing attempt to make the kind of
ground-breaking and ingenious interpretations of specific aspects of
Plato's City which | have the humble ambition of making.

In the third place, | would call attention to an article by the American
Classics professor Gregory Nagy, “Socrates in Plato’s Phaedo: The
meaning of theoria”, which is the single best piece on the “Sacred
Journey”-part of that dialogue | have encountered, and which begins to do

with the Greek term theoria exactly what | hope to do with various terms
in the Politeia, viz. carry out a multi-level “exegesis” of various terms
central to the extended parable of the City.

The problem with these and other such investigations is of course that
they are rather short, that they form a very disjointed and unsatisfying
whole, and that they do not even approach the weaving together of the
“fives” | that |am hoping to one day accomplish, let alone the cubing of
the fives into one of Plato's symbols of the “Royal” and “Heavenly” state
of mind (Politiea 528b, 587b-587e).

There is a multitude of other papers and journal articles and anthology
contributions and the like which might be pulled into and made use of ina
project like the one | am proposing, of course. Vishwa Adluri’s 2006 article,
“Initiation into the Mysteries: The Experience of the Irrational in Plato”,
argues that Plato was profoundly influenced by the venerable institution
that was the Eleusinian Mysteries, and that he was not in an antagonistic
relationship with them, and the lectures by Prof. William Ralph Inge (1899)
go even further into the subject of Plato and Eleusis. Additionally, we have
the great work by the late Prof. Konrad Gaiser (1998) of the Tubingen
“Plato-School” (Platon-Schule) on the “Unwritten Doctrine” probably
taught by Plato at his Academy.

The simple fact is, though, that none of these contributions to the debate
come close to studying, in a systematic and synthesizing manner, the
dazzlingly beautiful and sophisticated conception | view as underlying
Plato's Politeia, not to mention the bringing out or rendering of this
wonderful and largely veiled complexity of thought, so frequently
overlooked by modern students and introductions to Platonic philosophy,
into a form easily accessible and comprehensible to the modern
philosophical reader. That is another reason why this thesis needs to come
into being.

As for societal relevance, | would remind the reader of the famous

statement made by Alfred North Whitehead, even though it is somewhat
hyperbolic: “The safest general characterization of the European
philosophical tradition is that it consists of a series of footnotes to Plato.”
(Whitehead, 1978, p. 39) | would also, moreover, draw attention to what
the researcher Paul Tyson says when discussing the problems with the
prevailing worldview of our time: “Metaphysics is the endeavor to
understand what reality really is (...).” (Tyson, 2014, p. 16) What could be
more important? But Plato's schema is also the “metaphysics” of the Self
(or so | would claim).

Relevant research environments would be Roots of Europe/Linguistics
(since there is a need to ascertain what the spectra of meaning of various
terms in Plato's Attic Greek were) and Biblical Studies/Hebrew
Bible/Hellenic philosophy (not only because Platonic philosophy is an
expression of Hellenic culture, but because Plato may have influenced the
Hebrew Bible to a far greater degree than is usually recognized). Other
potential matches for the project are Political Theory, Center for
Subjectivity Research and Center for the Study of Paradoxes.

Theoretical framework

This is obviously a project which, if accepted, will be dealing mainly with
ancient texts, and with the reading and interpretation of such texts. I am
of the conviction that it is fully possible for a modern human being such
as myself to understand such texts, even though their origin is far
removed from us in time, and to extract meanings and messages from
them which are fully in accord with the original intention of the author,
since it is an observable and scientific fact that human nature, and the
structure of the human mind, are fundamentally the same as they were in
Plato's day and age, for example. We should not exaggerate the
differences between ourselves and our time and those of Athens 2,400

years ago, as many of the most striking disparities are superficial and

10

external ones, such as the presence of various technological
marvels, which have no bearing whatsoever on the basic nature of
the human being, or on the field of metaphysics, or on any of the
Everlasting Questions arising out of the tensions and challenges

inherent in the Human Condition.

Nevertheless, we must of course exercise great caution when attempting
to ascertain what the messages embedded in a text from another time
and another culture might be, so as to avoid, whether consciously or
unconsciously, the imposition of our own views and assumptions on the

piece of communication in question.

I believe I have a distinct advantage when it comes to the interpretation
of texts like those of Plato, not only since I have been immersing myself
in ancient literature and history since 2009 or thereabouts, but since I
am myself a writer and an author, who has been working intensively with
the tool or instrument that is language - initially only Norwegian, but
later also English - for some twenty astonishing years. Hence, I have
developed an insight into how an author works, or might work,
and a sensitivity to words and phrases, regardless of language,

which can only come with years and years of practical experience.

Moreover, it has been abundantly clear to me for a very long time now
that words and phrases and languages, contrary to what is sometimes
maintained nowadays, are not at all the primary “fabric” of which
thoughts are made, and that thoughts, when they initially come into
being and develop, transcend all verbal words and all structured human
languages. The foremost struggle an author faces, particularly when
attempting to compose truly original and excellent work, is always that of
“translating” the non-verbal and ineffable and unspeakable into the
containers and vehicles of meaning called words, and this is holds true
whether the “object” one is laboring to capture is some worldly event or

an altered state of consciousness. Moreover, this process is always

11

experienced to be reductive. No amount of words, however
carefully selected and put together, can ever do justice to the
luminous richness of one's inner life, or to the seamless

complexity of the original experience.

In spite of such insights and such skills, we cannot, of course, take it for
granted that we are in a position to comprehend the meanings of the
words and sentences constituting an ancient work unaided - even if we
are able to read the text in the original language, so that we do
not have to rely on the imperfect image and derivative that a
translation always is. This is where the discipline of linguistics
becomes a virtually indispensable handmaiden, since only a careful
examination of the etymology and spectrum of meaning of a given term,
as recorded in sophisticated dictionaries, as well as of the usage of the
term in question, enables us to dispense with our default assumptions,
and to gauge what an author like Plato may have had in mind when he

chose to employ it.

In other words, properly reading a text like that of the Politeia
entails not only the use of a critical faculty, but also openness,

reverence, visualization, contemplation and synthesis.

I believe I adhered to this framework for reading when I examined Plato's
use of and statements on the Greek term idea a few years ago, and that I
made some extraordinary findings - even though the impetus underlying
my research came from the American teacher, Platonist and Buddhist
who almost single-handedly converted me to True Philosophy, the late Dr.
Pierre Grimes (1924-2024).

Another discipline likely to prove highly useful to an endeavor such as
this is Jungian Psychology. The reason why I say psychology is, as might
have been anticipated, that several of the five levels outlined in the above

are clearly psychological, and the reason why I say Jungian is that the

12

psychological theory developed by C. G. Jung is eminently suitable as a
framework for considering Plato's dialogues, and as a source of
commentary and learned disagreement - not only because Jungian
psychology is fundamentally theistic, and has an inclusive and welcoming
relationship to religion and belief, but also since Jungian Psychology may
be viewed as a 20" century outgrowth of Platonic philosophy - and even
Jung himself admitted the indebtedness of parts of his system to Plato,

and to alchemical works arising out of Platonic philosophy.

Finally, a term which may be translated as “the psychotherapy” actually
occurs in the Politeia, in 585d (nj Sepaneia thc woxc; “the
therapy/care/healing of the soul”). This does not necessarily mean that
Plato had developed a practice resembling those now classified as
psychotherapy, of course, but it does give an indication of how concerned
Plato was with the inner, mental life of the human being, and with the
improvement and transformation of that life, the goal of which was
Justice (Wholeness) and Inner Kingship (a state in which the desires have
been brought under rational control, and the power of Spiritual Sight has

been activated, and is continually guiding Reason and the Will).

Methods

In the first place, a project like the one presented, which is really a
project belonging to the philosophy of religion, as well as to psychology
and philology, must, according to my convictions, return “ad fontes”, to
the sources of the concepts that are to be investigated, i.e. the texts in
which they were first articulated or the archaeological remains first
depicting their symbols, and then use those materials as the fountains of

“data” to be analyzed.

This is so because all translations of those sources into another language

are mere likenesses of the originals (as Plato would say), based on

13

innumerable choices made by individuals who were not directly in touch
with the original ideas, and whose language may not be able to

accommodate even their original expressions.

In the second place, a number of different translations of the originals in
question must be consulted (if possible), and especially those of the
“literal” kind, for it is of course exceedingly valuable to learn something

of what great scholars in the language in question thought that the texts

signify.

In the third place, it is necessary to create a searchable database in
which both particularly interesting passages, as well as translations and
preliminary observations may be stored - for without such a database,
the amount of data being collected for processing will quickly become
overwhelming. This storage facility could take the form of something as
rudimentary as a word processing document, or something more
advanced - what is crucially important is to have all the data in a digital

and therefore searchable form.

In the fourth place, there has to be a stage, or rather stages, of
processing the data thus collected. Essential phrases, like those
containing statements mentioning one of the “fives”, have to be studied
in a word-for-word manner, including both the words of the original
language and those of at least a couple of different translations. Other
stages of processing should be made to consist in discussions with the
supervisor, or with other researchers, and in careful studies of the
etymologies of certain words deemed to be of great importance (using
sophisticated digital tools like the LSJ online dictionary, for instance). The
latter stage is a way of overcoming our modern conditioning and our
default assumptions, and of discovering the original or ancient range of

meaning associated with a given word.

In addition to all of this, it would of course be appropriate to see what

14

has been said by the minds of later times of the passages singled out for
analysis. The danger inherent in this phase of processing is that it relies
on images of images of images of images (in the cases where the

commentaries rely on translations).

In the fifth place, the actual writing of the thesis itself would only
commence once the above has been at least partly accomplished, in
order to avoid spending precious time and energy on the creation of
chapters which later research makes it necessary to greatly revise or

discard.

As for ethical considerations, I would say that it takes time and hard
work to gain something like a permission, in a moral sense, to so much as
begin to judge the works of an author who was likely wiser than oneself,

and who is unable to mount a defense of his or her ideas.

Expected results

A fascinating exposition and synthesis of all the “fives” - complex yet
lucid. The rediscovery and reappraisal of one of the foundations of
European and Middle-Eastern philosophy, religion (c.f. Tyson, 2014, p.
91-96), psychology and anthropology. Plato rehabilitated/exonerated
from the charge of totalitarianism/utopianism? “Platonic Yoga” - a viable
practice for the human being of our day and age? The relevance of Plato
to systems like those of Christianity, Buddhism and Islam. A useful point
of departure for inter-religious dialogue? The single most important
surviving ancient Greek work on the Self reinstated? Plato incarnated

into a new age.
