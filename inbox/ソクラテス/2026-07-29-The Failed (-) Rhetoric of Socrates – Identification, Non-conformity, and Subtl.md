## Abstract

This paper examines the rhetoric used by Socrates in Plato's Apology through the lens of Social Identity Theory and Burke’s concept of identification. Considering rhetoric as the art of persuasion through the orator’s invocation of shared group identity with the audience, the analysis explores the extent to which Socrates aligned himself with widely accepted conventional norms, values, and beliefs to establish rapport with the dicasts. Remaining steadfast to truth, his principles, and his divine mission, Socrates emerged as a nonconformist to the majority of the contemporary audience. Nonetheless, he managed to establish himself as the prototype philosopher, advocating for an elevated identification based on virtue with his followers and successors across all generations.

Social Identity Theory and Burke’s Identification
In a tripartite dialogue between a speaker, his adversary, and the audience, rhetoric can be defined as the art of identification and division, in an attempt to
“discover the means of persuasion available in a given case”. 8 Kenneth Burke, in his seminal study A Rhetoric of Motives, approached rhetoric as the enterprise of establishing rapport between the speaker and the audience. 9 Once ‘identification’
takes place, usually by reference to real or imagined shared values, norms, and beliefs, human beings act together, have common sensations, concepts, images, ideas, and attitudes that make them ‘consubstantial’ and, therefore, receptive to persuasion. 10
Rhetoric, thus defined, belongs in the psychological realm of Social Identity
Theory, which acknowledges and analyses the “individual’s knowledge that he belongs to certain social groups together with some emotional and value significance to him of this group’s membership”. 11 Within this psychological framework, individuals categorize themselves and others into separate social groups, fostering a sense of belonging and self-esteem based on this group membership. As a result, they often align their actions with and show a preference for the values, beliefs, and norms of their own group, while simultaneously showing prejudice against other groups. The psychological construction of an in-group / out-group domain leads individuals to show preference toward members of their group, with whom they share a purportedly common identity, while exhibiting bias against outsiders perceived as not conforming to the group's values. To persuade, then, a skilful rhetorician aims to artfully construct a shared identity with the audience, while underscoring the opponent’s division and non-adherence to the norms of the group.

Socrates’ Division with the Audience: The Trial of an Ethical Outsider
Socrates’ preexisting division with the audience, primarily attributed to the deep-seated prejudice fostered by previous accusers (Pl. Apol. 18c; 19c) and the animosity provoked by his questioning of prominent individuals regarding their understanding of ethical matters (Pl. Apol. 21c-23a), was recognized throughout the
Apology and triggered a subtle criticism of the way Athenian courts operated.
Socrates recognised that numerous Athenian dicasts were inclined to vote against him, not because of the current legal charges but due to longstanding bias formed against a fabricated version of ‘Socrates’ as ethical outsider, constructed through the falsehoods propagated by his old accusers. This distorted image bore no resemblance to the genuine Socrates, who bore no responsibility for these misrepresentations (Pl. Apol. 19c), but was enough to condemn him. Thus, being aware of the effect of the established division with the audience, Socrates was doubtful that he would be able to remove this prejudice in the very short time of a trial when it has grown so great for so long a time (Pl. Apol. 19a; 24a; 37a-b).
Yet, Socrates’ division with – at least – part of the audience stemmed from deeper underlying reasons.12 While acknowledging the significant impact of the caricature created by comedians, particularly Aristophanes in The Clouds (Pl. Apol.
18d; 19c), on the perceptions of Athenian dicasts, it is evident that Socrates did little to hide his opposition to the democracy and the damaging rule of the masses, although this did not automatically make him a supporter of oligarchy (Pl. Apol.

In any case, Socrates, distrusting the wisdom of the masses in decision-making and although knowing the risk of opposing them, deliberately refrained from flattering the audience or feigning conformity with their norms and ideas. 22
Consequently, he was not willing to sacrifice his personal identity just to align with their values in the hope of persuading them. In that respect, Socrates’ rhetoric was destined to fail, or, in other words, Socrates employed a failed rhetoric.
The Apology frequently emphasised the contrasting hierarchy of principles and beliefs, showcasing the Athenians' pursuit of material wealth and other petty things against Socrates' commitment to intellectual and philosophical ideals. 23
Socrates contended that throughout his life remained loyal to his mission to the god, as assigned by the oracle of Delphi (Pl. Apol. 21a-23d), which proclaimed that there was no one wiser than him. This dedication to divine service, he argued, stirred prejudice and animosity toward him, as it compelled him to persist in examining individuals whom the majority deemed wise but whom he ultimately discovered to be ignorant. Yet, although perceiving that he was “hated, and grieving and fearing” (Pl. Apol. 21e), Socrates nevertheless considered the god's business of the highest importance, “going about and searching and investigating at the god's behest anyone, whether citizen or foreigner, whom I [he] think is wise; and when he does not seem so to me, I give aid to the god and show that he is not wise” (Pl. Apol. 23b; cf. 28e, 33c, 37e). This commitment to his divine mission represented true piety for Socrates, and he had no plan to stray from it, disregarding the power and authority of the majority which wrongly interpreted his conduct as eccentric, if not impious. 24 His firm adherence to his personal identity and the unconditional execution of his divine mission posed challenges for his identification with the audience, especially within the confines of conventional morality and practices.

Certainly, Socrates, who never treated anyone unjustly, including himself, and preferred to be wronged rather than harm another, provoked the audience even after being condemned as guilty by proposing a ‘penalty’ suitable for a benefactor of the city: to receive honorary meals in the prytaneum. He deemed this outcome more fitting for him than even for someone winning a race at the Olympic games, as the latter merely made the Athenians appear happy, whereas Socrates aimed to genuinely contribute to their happiness (Pl. Apol. 36d). Naturally, the dicasts were unable to grasp his progressive and advanced reasoning; despite
Socrates’ friends persuading him to propose a reasonable financial penalty, they ultimately voted for his execution.
Remaining true to his understanding of the role of a judge, Socrates reserved this title solely for those who voted for his acquittal (Pl. Apol. 40a), referring to the others simply as “men of Athens”.28In a direct criticism of the Athenian administration of justice, Socrates implies – by reference to those who condemned him – that dicasts could be easily swayed by rhetorical and performative tricks aimed at contrived, superficial identification, eliciting compassion and pity from the jurors.
The fact that he decided not to adopt the tactics that the dicasts would normally expect (or desire) from defendants, reveals his commitment to making an apology befitting his character and principles. Ironically – yet not untypically for Socrates – his condemnation to death on charges of impiety elevated him to the status of a martyr and the archetype philosopher for future generations; and, indeed, in Plato’s Apology Socrates emerged as the accuser of his dicasts, denouncing the authoritarian and censorial tendencies of social conformity as employed by the Athenian popular courts which, in his case, suppressed freedom of thought, conscience and expression. Despite respecting the verdict and famously drinking the hemlock, Socrates’ stance delegitimised the decision-making authority of his fellow citizens (who did not deserve to be named
‘dicasts’) and the conformity-imposing power of the many. Establishing a new prototype for nobler values and beliefs, Socrates warned the Athenians:
“For if you think that by putting men to death you will prevent anyone from reproaching you because you do not act as you should, you are mistaken. That mode of escape is neither possible at all nor honourable, but the easiest and most honourable escape is not by suppressing others, but by making yourselves as good as possible. (Pl. Apol. 39d)”

Socrates, remaining true to his principles, may have failed to achieve his identification with the majority of his contemporaries in the famous trial of 399 BC, yet he succeeded in his alignment with virtue, adherence to the law, and identification with the future generations of intellectuals. In this regard, Socrates’ seemingly failed rhetoric in the court became a triumph for his legacy and enduring influence across all generations.
