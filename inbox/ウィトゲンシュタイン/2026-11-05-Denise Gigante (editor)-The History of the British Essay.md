THE BRITISH ESSAY

From ancient inﬂuences on the essay to the Irish essay as per-
formance, from British imperial propaganda to African postcolo-
nial resistance, from political pamphlets to the rise of literary
professionalism, and from gastronomy to ecocriticism, The
Cambridge History of the British Essay offers the ﬁrst authoritative
single-volume history of the essay’s development within British
literary tradition. Its ﬁfty contributors come from widely diverse
backgrounds and areas of expertise to show how, as the form
morphs to serve new contexts and concerns, it remains a vital
genre of literary ‘attempt’ in such ﬁelds as journalism, academic
and cultural critique, life writing, and digital essayism.

D G is the Sadie Dernham Patek Professor of
Humanities at Stanford University and editor of The Great Age of
the English Essay (). Her essays on the essay include ‘On
Coffee Houses, Smoking and the English Essay Tradition’
(); ‘Pedantry and Party Politics: Essays in the Public Sphere’
(); and ‘Sometimes a Stick is Just a Stick: The Essay as
(Organic) Form’ ().

J C is a writer and independent scholar based in Berlin
and Dijon. He has published research on the essay in The

The essay may present itself in the shape of verse under the title of an
essay, or it may pass by other names in the form of a de facto essay. It may be
plucked from a longer text and collected in a book of essays, or it may appear
serially in the more ephemeral form of a periodical. It may adopt a tone that

be strident in the face of injustice, or soporiﬁc (at times) in high seriousness.

unsociable like a tortoise tucked away under an old motley cover. Whatever

history that it is and resist the very habits of classiﬁcation that deﬁne its

Ancient Inﬂuences on the Essay

 

The essay, as Francis Bacon observed, has a classical pedigree. ‘The word is
late, but the thing is ancient’, he says. ‘For Seneca’s epistles to Lucilius, if one
mark them well, are but essays – that is, dispersed meditations, though
conveyed in the form of epistles.’ Seneca’s letters to his friend Lucilius –
often referred to as the ‘Moral Essays’ – are an obvious point of reference for
the early essay. These  letters have the broad aim of inculcating an
understanding and appreciation of Stoic doctrine. The mid-seventeenth
century was the high watermark of Neostoicism, and while many early
English essayists espoused Stoic as well as other ancient principles, it was
from a stylistic and formal, rather than philosophical, perspective that they
did so. In his letters to Lucilius, Seneca ranged over a wide variety of topics:
there are letters on crowds, on animals, on old age, and on drunkenness, all
of which strike a note of sensible and easy conversation. The philosopher, he
claims, should aim not for ascetic extremism, but for ‘common sense,
humanity, and intercourse and society’. Such work as this set the tone for
Joseph Addison and Richard Steele.
Although there is no equivalent term for ‘essay’ in Greek or Latin, ancient
literature was instrumental to the development of the British essay in the

Classical Models: Seneca, Plutarch, Cicero

In a verse epistle to his friend Owen Feltham, written in the s, the poet
Thomas Randolph offers an overview of the Senecan style:

I mean the style, being pure and strong and round,
Not long but pithy: being short breath’d, but sound.
Such as the grave, acute, wise Seneca sings,
That best of tutor to the worst of kings.
Not long and empty; lofty but not proud;
Subtle but sweet, high but without a cloud.
Well settled full of nerves, in brief ’tis such
That in a little hath comprised much,
Like th’ Iliads in a Nutshell.

Roger L’Estrange describes the Senecan style similarly in the preface to his
abridgement of Seneca’s Epistles: ‘His Excellency Consists rather in a
Rhapsody of Divine, and Extraordinary Hints, and Notions, than in any
Regulated Method of Discourse.’ The inﬂuence on Bacon is clear enough,
both in terms of the wide-ranging and gradually evolving form of the work as
a whole (‘dispersed meditations’) and in terms of this aphoristic approach to
communicating ideas.
The fact that Seneca’s advice to Lucilius is conveyed in letters means that it
unfolds haphazardly over time. Thomas Lodge’s  translation drops the
greeting and sign-off from each letter, making the work more closely
resemble an essay collection, but the epistolary form still shines through.
In his eighty-ﬁrst epistle Seneca offers a theory of prose which stresses the
importance of concision and compactness, such that such aphoristic writing
takes on the qualities of poetry: ‘Those things are heard more negligently,
and persuade less powerfully, as long as they are delivered in prose and
ordinary discourse, but when as they are shut up in numbers and good
sense, be enclosed in certain feet and cadences, that very sentence is darted
and delivered as it were an arrow from a strong arm.’ Such compressed
diction as Seneca espouses was fundamental to the English prose essay from
its origins.

The similarity of the Senecan and Baconian styles can be seen by compar-
ing two brief passages:

Seneca: Attalus the philosopher was wont to say, that it was a far more
pleasant thing to make a friend, than to have a friend; as it is more agreeable
to a painter to paint, than to have ﬁnished his picture . . . . The youth of our
children is more fruitful unto us, but their infancy more sweet.

Bacon: The joys of parents are secret; and so are their griefs, and fears: they
cannot utter the one; nor they will not utter the other. Children sweeten
labours; but they make misfortunes more bitter: They increase the cares of
life; but they mitigate the remembrance of death.

What Bacon has in common with Seneca is his tightness of expression and
staccato delivery. Seneca’s ﬁnal line might easily have found a place in
Bacon’s essay. Yet if one draws out and looks at the essays in their entirety,
the same sentences serve a very different function. Seneca’s observation
about children is put to the service of a broader message: we will never feel
a want of friendship if we take pleasure in the present rather than the future.
Bacon is certainly sympathetic to that idea, but that is not the subject of his
passage. Rather, his sentences lead into a set of quickﬁre observations on the
nature of parenting, which takes in biblical wisdom, classical references,
natural history, sociological observation, accounts of cultural difference,
and practical advice. As he wrote in The Advancement of Learning (),
while praising the ‘excellent virtues’ of the aphoristic style, ‘Aphorisms,
except they should be ridiculous, cannot be made but of the pith and heart
of sciences: for discourse of illustration is cut off, recitals of examples are cut
off: discourse of connexion, and order is cut off; descriptions of practice, are
cut off’, leaving one with the broader message.

as Moral Essays) is a misnomer. Derived from the Greek Ἠθικά (Ethika), it

The Melliﬁcation and Digestion of the Ancients

In The Battel of the Books (), Jonathan Swift describes an encounter
between a spider and a bee that serves as a model for different approaches
to literary production. The bee stands for the Senecan method. His habit, he
tells the spider, is to ‘visit, indeed, all the ﬂowers and blossoms of the ﬁeld
and the garden, but whatever I collect from thence, enriches myself, without
the least injury to their beauty, their smell, or their taste’. The bee’s relation
to the ﬂowers corresponds to the reading practices advocated by Swift and
other self-styled ‘Ancients’. He operates ‘by universal range, with long
search, much study, true judgment, and distinction of things’, ultimately
bringing home ‘honey and wax’ – that is to say, sweetness and light. The
more modern spider, on the other hand, generates his material from within,
possessing ‘a genuine store of dirt and poison in [his] breast’.

Swift’s bee, ﬁttingly enough, has a long literary pedigree. Bacon had, in his
Instauratio, identiﬁed three categories of thinkers, which he likened to
spiders, ants, and bees:

The empirics, in the manner of the ant, only store up and use things; the
rationalists, in the manner of spiders, spin webs from their own entrails; but
the bee takes the middle path: it collects its material from the ﬂowers of ﬁeld
and garden, but its special gift is to convert and digest it. The true job of
philosophy is not much different.

Swift would no doubt have been familiar with Bacon’s analogy, and even
more so with Sir William Temple’s reuse of it in ‘Of Poetry’, having served
as Temple’s private secretary and the editor of his works. Temple compares
poets to bees, who ‘must range through ﬁelds as well as gardens, choose such
ﬂowers as they please . . . They must work upon their cells, with admirable
art, extract their honey with inﬁnite labour.’

result of an individual author’s reading, and yet an integral part of the text,
having been appropriated for and indeed by it’. We might call this the
process of melliﬁcation.
Swift’s bee is clearly a Senecan one, and an essayistic mode of assimilating
classical literature is opposed to a modern approach in which ancient material
is disregarded in favour of self-generated ‘Dirt and Poison’. In A Tale of a Tub,
Swift assumes the role of a ‘most devoted Servant of all Modern Forms’,
holding ancient writings in disdain because they do not address modern
concerns. He grudgingly concedes that Homer is ‘not without some
Abilities’, but he lambasts him satirically for ‘his gross Ignorance in the
Common Laws of this Realm, and in the Doctrine as well as Discipline of the

such as political wagering, spleen, and tea. The narrator of the Tale is not
interested in the kind of universality that can enable communication across
the centuries. He considers it impossible for an author to speak to the present
age without speaking of it from a condition of historical embeddedness.

Ancient Transmission

The essay was not only a form that was assimilated from the ancient world,
as discussed above, but also – and to a much greater extent – a major route
through which ancient ideas and writings were transmitted to modern
readers. This function is particularly striking in the early eighteenth-century
periodical essay and becomes more prominent after the Restoration.
Abraham Cowley, in Several Discourses, by Way of Essays in Verse and Prose
(), follows Seneca (and Montaigne) in their emphasis on retirement and
self-awareness. His essays are also studded with brief quotations, often
massaged or truncated in order to ﬁt the essayist’s larger aims. As Paul
Davis writes of Cowley’s allusive practice, ‘There is a thin line between being
on the same wavelength and tuning out difference, feeling that you are
forever taking the words right out of a friend’s mouth and putting words into
that friend’s mouth.’ As Johnson put it, Cowley beneﬁted from ‘an intellec-
tual digestion that concocted the pulp of learning, but refused the husks’.

Besides digesting a range of sources, Cowley also presented the reader with
much larger, undigested passages. It was his essay ‘On Agriculture’ that ﬁrst
introduced English readers to his celebrated translations of the sixth satire of
Horace’s second book (‘The Country Mouse’), itself digested from Aesop, and
to the praises of country life from the second book of Virgil’s Georgics.
Despite its larger scale, Cowley’s version of Horace’s second Epode is a classic
example of commonplace quotation which simultaneously resists and communi-
cates its original meaning. The poem is concerned with the delights of rural
retirement, but it ends with a sudden shift in perspective when the reader learns
that the whole account has been the daydream of a city-bound moneylender,
Alﬁus. Eliminating the ﬁnal twist allows Cowley to present a less complicated
version of Horace’s poem, no longer a reﬂection on the distance between human
aspirations and human behaviour but a straightforward paean to the countryside.
But the translation is more complicated than that. As Davis has shown, its main
section is shot through with the imagery of commerce and ﬁnance. To give one

Resolving the Early Essay

The early essayists themselves recognised this tension between ﬂow and
poise as key to the form of literary prose they were developing. In a famous
passage at the beginning of ‘Of Repenting’, Montaigne outlines the peculiar
relation of his writing to time and truth, in terms that anticipate John Hall’s
punctum ﬂuens: ‘I describe not the essence, but the passage; not a passage
from age to age, or as the people reckon, from seven years to seven, but from
day to day, from minute to minute. . . . I may perhaps gainsay my self, but
truth . . . I never gainsay [sc. contradict].’ Montaigne represents his writing as
a moving point of experience, like Hall’s punctum ﬂuens, inconsistent across
time but self-consistent in its testimony to the moment. He continues: ‘Were
my mind settled, I would not essay, but resolve my self.’ Essaying is the
opposite of resolution.

include the vow, the resolve, the discourse, the meditation, the observation,
and the counsel. Among these, resolves were among the most popular,

in terms which recall his characterisation of the ‘false’ essays of Montaigne
and others as ‘strong, and able to endure the sharpest trial’. But there he
cannot stop: ‘hardly have my thoughts made an end of this gallant discourse,
but in comes a wife, or a friend, at whose sight my Armour of defence is
broken, and I could weep with them, or be content to laugh at their trivial
sports’. Discourse ends, only to be set running again by circumstance.
As Felltham writes at the end of his ﬁrst book of Resolves, ‘Resolutions may
often change.’ The end of each essay invites a new beginning.

Another, and equally important, inﬂuence on the early essay was the long
tradition of miscellaneous writing that reached back to classical antiquity, and

bewildering range of topics. Book  alone ranges across mathematics, moral

The complexity, inconsistency, and evasiveness of the self, moreover, makes

The remainder of the essay combines a discussion of various authors with
a description of Montaigne’s own note-taking technique. He commends
Plutarch and Seneca, for example, for their axiomatic, sententious, and
easy-to-extract style, and also for their shortness: ‘Both have this excellent
commodity for my humour, that whosoever read them is not tied to plod
long upon them, whereof I am uncapable.’ Their terse style, of course,
lends itself to the commonplace approach, and both are amongst the most
widely cited authors in commonplace books from the period. However, the
style of notation that Montaigne proceeds to describe suggests that his own
way of reading was more idiosyncratic, more dynamic, and more clearly
ﬁxed on judgment. Many times, he admits, he alighted upon books in his
library, which he ‘supposed to be new, and never to have read’, only to ﬁnd
them ‘all bescribbled with my notes’. He, therefore, devised an annotative
practice that would make this kind of forgetfulness impossible: ‘I have a
while since, accustomed my self, to note at the end of my book (I mean such
as I purpose to read but once) the time I made an end to read it, and to set
down what censure or judgement I gave of it.’ The intention was that, at a
later date, those notes would then ‘represent unto my mind, the air and

Miscellanies and Commonplaces

As the seventeenth century progressed, the connections between essays,
commonplace books, and miscellanies that Cornwallis and Montaigne high-
lighted remained every bit as important. Essayists continued to rely on
commonplace and miscellany models for the varied citations and subjects
that were their stock-in-trade. The essayistic endeavours of Joshua Baildon, a
translator and miscellanist from Barn Elms in Surrey, and scion of a promin-
ent Yorkshire family, are a case in point. Baildon is today an almost entirely
forgotten literary ﬁgure. In the s and s, however, he had a brief
period of efﬂorescence, producing translations of Spanish, Italian, and French
books, and completing a long manuscript miscellany of his own. His most
notable work was The Rarities of the World (), an adaptation of the
Spanish writer and humanist Pedro Mexía’s La silva di varia lección (),
one of the most popular of all sixteenth-century miscellanies. As Baildon
explained in his preface, his adaptation was, in fact, based on a French
intermediary (‘a book, Petrus Messia, translated into French’), which his friend
and kinsman, Paul Holdenby, had brought back from France and had given
him ‘twenty years since’. This was almost certainly one of the many
editions of Claude Gruget’s translation of the Silva, a book ﬁrst published
at Lyon in , where it was issued under the title Les diverses leçons de Pierre
Messie. Gruget’s translation was then frequently reprinted and circulated
widely: we know, for example, that Montaigne encountered Mexía in
this version.

As well as adapting the Silva, Baildon was a miscellanist in his own right.

under topical headings in humanist pedagogical fashion. He then mixed these
citations to produce what is, in effect, a series of short aphoristic discourses in
the vein of Bacon’s early essays. Taking his cue from Montaigne, Baildon
collected these ‘ﬂowers’, as his title explains, not only to speak and argue

ﬁrst of these is Hall’s ﬁfty-ﬁfth meditation; the second is his ninety-eighth
meditation. In both instances, Baildon copied the text almost verbatim, but
then amended it to turn a ﬁrst-person meditation into second-person precepts
and instructions. His essay, in short, is made from commonplaces, but it is
also more than just a rehearsal of them.

The meaning of Osborne’s analogy depends on the reader understanding
the anamorphic technique and knowing how such paintings were made. Its
purpose was twofold. First, it captures the elusive and multi-perspectival
aspect of miscellanies and essays – the fact that, by dint of their ‘mixed nature’,
they are shifting, and sometimes also contradictory, textual forms. Second,
and just as importantly, it underscores the essential craft and assemblage of
the two genres: like pleated pictures, essays and miscellanies are also made
up of fragments; their citations and commonplaces, examples and extracts,
are the equivalents of the vertical strips or ‘parallel pieces’ that constituted the
raw ingredients of those paintings. Separated out, these textual fragments,
like the individual strips, have little meaning. But reassembled they can be
made to constitute a coherent whole, and also something surprising and new.
Osborne’s analogy thus articulates the central paradox of miscellanies: that
something as capacious and heterogeneous could still be recognised as a
form of order and a way of engaging with the worlds of both words and
things. Furthermore, in its combination of the abstruse, the novel, and the
mundane, the analogy also exactly beﬁts the essay in its seventeenth-century
form and encapsulates what made it, when in rhetorically skilful hands such
as Osborne’s, so compelling a vehicle for exploration and reﬂection.

combination of inﬂuences – the fact that essayists mixed the verbal resources

that did not mean that essays always ended up either as the schoolboy
formula that Ralph Johnson prescribed, or the windy, wordy derivative that
his near namesake decried.

Any examination of the epistemology of the eighteenth-century essay might
usefully begin by asking why the author of the founding work of systematic
empiricism titled it an ‘Essay’ in the ﬁrst place. As his ‘Epistle to the Reader’
makes clear, John Locke chose the term with care. The ‘Epistle’ recounts
how the initial impetus for writing the Essay Concerning Human Understanding
() arose from Locke’s frustration at the way in which many of the
debates he engaged in with his friends ended up heading down blind alleys:

Were it ﬁt to trouble thee with the History of this Essay, I should tell thee
that ﬁve or six Friends meeting at my Chamber, and discoursing on a Subject
very remote from this, found themselves quickly at a stand, by the
Difﬁculties that rose on every side. After we had a while puzzled our selves,
without coming nearer a Resolution of those Doubts which perplexed us, it
came into my Thoughts, that we took a wrong course; and that, before we
set our selves upon Enquiries of that Nature, it was necessary to examine
our own Abilities, and see, what Objects our Understandings were, or were
not ﬁtted to deal with.

The root of the problem, Locke determined, lay in the participants’ collective
failure properly to establish the parameters and preconditions of what could
possibly constitute an intelligible discussion of the topic at hand. To remedy
this deﬁciency, what was needed was an epistemological groundwork that
could gauge the capacity of the human mind to inquire into certain questions,
based upon a clear understanding of the human intellectual frame: its origins,
its structure, its capabilities, and its limitations. The Essay was to conduct a
foundational reassessment of method, to ‘examine our own Abilities, and see,
what Objects our Understandings were, or were not ﬁtted to deal with’.

What Locke’s ‘Epistle’ reveals is an epistemological ambivalence that lies
not just within the essay genre, but also within British empiricism itself. This
ambiguity manifests as a cleavage in Enlightenment thought between com-
municative and apodeictic models of reason, or between what Jürgen
Habermas identiﬁes as the spheres of ‘lifeworld’ and ‘system’ in the eight-
eenth century. According to Habermas, in the ‘constitutive’, ‘already inter-
preted’, ‘intersubjectively shared’ lifeworld, discourse is determined by the
pragmatic considerations of speech acts that collectively underpin communi-
cative rationality; in the regulative ‘system’, by contrast, the thinker engages
with a world determined by considerations of truth and falsity, in which
thought is made the object of reﬂection. And yet, rather than identifying
itself with either of these possibilities, the essay internalises the epistemo-
logical fold within empiricism.
Accordingly, from Locke onwards, the genre simultaneously follows two
divergent epistemological pathways. In one direction, in the works of Joseph
Addison, Richard Steele, David Hume, and Samuel Johnson, the familiar
essay becomes an analogue for an idea of ‘experience’ that is primarily
communicative and ‘open’. At the same time, in its more systematic or
‘closed’ mode, the essay honours the Essay’s commitment to sound method,
demonstrative reasoning, and argumentative conclusiveness. Through this
latter, systematic trajectory, the essay contributes to the expansion of abstract
knowledge and the specialisation of intellectual functions engendered by
instrumental rationality, leading to the ﬂourishing of the ‘academic’ essay
practised by a new generation of professional scholars, including natural and
moral philosophers. By accommodating both ‘open’ and ‘closed’ modes, the
eighteenth-century essay presents itself as Janus-faced, offering writers both a
vehicle for expressing the quickness of subjectivity in its communicative
engagements and a tool for robust scientiﬁc procedure and demonstrative
reasoning.

Systemic Disposition

Most of the great philosophical essays of Hume and Johnson appeared in the
middle of the eighteenth century. Towards the end of the century, however,
the ambassadorial middle ground between scholarly learning and public
discourse could no longer hold. As science diversiﬁed and deepened, and
the book trade burgeoned, the Montaignian essay, which continued to
prioritise (albeit with growing wistfulness) sociability, communicability,
and civic virtues, drifted further away from the Baconian essay, which
utilised the essay as a vehicle for an increasingly abstract form of rationality
pursued through technical and theorised experimental procedures. In this
respect, Richard Blackmore’s earlier doubts regarding the suitability of essays
or ‘small Discourses’ to convey ‘full and comprehensive Conceptions of
Things’ proved prescient:

There is that Connexion, Dependence, and beautiful Order of the Parts in a
perfect Body of Science, that it is impossible to contemplate the whole
Structure but in a lame and imperfect View, when the Members of it are
sever’d and disunited, as they are in small Discourses. If the relation of one
Proposition to another, and the coherent Train of Conclusions are not
discern’d by the Mind, there must follow a great Confusion and Obscurity
of Ideas; and at best those Men, for want of full and comprehensive
Conceptions of Things, will only perceive some scatter’d Branches of
Truth, and form but a dim and defective Sketch of any Art or System
of Knowledge.

Blackmore’s claim that the essay was able to communicate to its reader only
‘a great Confusion and Obscurity of Ideas’ and ‘a dim and defective Sketch of
any Art or System of Knowledge’, highlights the essay’s uneasy relationship

preacher to understand and then impart the sacred meanings of the divine
text through rhetorical craft. Whether early modern sermons took on the
form of a homily (a verse-by-verse exposition of a scriptural text) or, more
commonly, of the thematic sermon (a sermon which derives its theme from

and out of the received sense of a scriptural text of which they are mediators
rather than authors, and this self-effacing stance of anti-authorship is one of
the primary features which would seem to distinguish a sermon from a
subjective essay in which the speaker’s ‘I’ meanders through loosely associ-

tions that are no longer merely superﬁcial, since in both cases the author’s
mediation of their perceived authority to speak and convey ideas and
opinions is similarly dependent on rhetorical and dialectical techniques of
exposition and analysis.

Anger at Rhetoric

In one sense, anger is a dated problem for the intersection of feminist and
early modernist literary criticism. During the Elizabethan and Jacobean
periods, the querelle des femmes, or ‘woman debate’, as critics have called it,
invoked anger to characterise the source and the style of female authorship –
and, at times, to insist that these authors spoke on behalf of, or represented a
uniﬁed subject position conﬁdently identiﬁed as ‘Woman’. This was not
without its problems, both theoretical and historical. Surveying the intersec-

publication of Judith Butler’s Gender Trouble, Diane Purkiss noted that anger,
outrage, fervour, and passion ‘are recognizable key words in the rhetoric of
certain kinds of radical feminism which characteristically valorize the emo-
tions of rebellion’: ‘Early feminism becomes an enabling pretext for the
production of feminism now. Such reading strategies necessarily lead to an
assumption that texts signed by women were indeed the work of women;
the language of criticism reveals a desire for material girls, women as real as
ourselves.’ By Purkiss’s logic, the collapse of past into present made for bad
politics because it compressed the evolving continuum of gender into a single
and consistent subject position. It made for bad criticism because variously
sophisticated textual strategies – allusion, irony, satire – become occluded by
the political interest in shoring up the early modern ﬁgure of ‘Woman’.

The seventeenth- and eighteenth-century British essay was a ﬂuid and
malleable form – to use a polyvalent early modern term, it was profoundly
‘fugitive’. An essay could be deeply topical, ﬂeeting, and perishable, taking
up ephemeral and occasional matters, and it could easily travel across media
and from reader to reader in the coffeehouses of Enlightenment London. But
the same fugitive form could also accommodate weightier matters, as in the
philosophical essays of Francis Bacon. The early British essay, as Caroline
Levine might characterise it, was a ﬂexible form possessing multiple affor-
dances, those ‘potential uses or actions latent in materials and designs’. This
chapter offers an overview of how writers exploited the affordances of the
essay in pamphlets and newsbooks from the seventeenth century and in the
emergent periodicals of the early eighteenth century.
This chapter will focus on the rhetorical qualities of these essays, viewing
them, after William R. Jones, as an ‘activistic art’ that sought to shape public
perceptions and promote political intervention on the part of readers. It will
also attend to the material forms that authors employed, because the media
in which these essays circulated potentially posed ﬁnancial and legal risks, not
only to the essayists, but also to their printers and publishers. Our argument
unfolds in three parts. The ﬁrst section traces the origins of the English
newsbook within a media ecology shaped by the edicts of the Star Chamber,
an elite court composed of Privy Counsellors and common-law judges, and
by the regulations of the Stationers’ Company. As a case study in stylistic
innovation and rhetorical self-fashioning, we examine the essayistic writings
of Marchamont Nedham across a range of newsbooks and pamphlets.

The Press Licensing Act and the News

In the later seventeenth century, all works intended for print were regulated
through the  Press Licensing Act. This act limited London to twenty
master printers and four founders of type. It also mandated the registration of
titles with the Stationers’ Company and the pre-publication submission of
manuscripts to the government for licensing. Both essayists and pamphlet-
eers laboured under the censorial requirements of the licensing era, and both
developed evasions. Underground illicit presses quickly emerged, and as
many as half of all printed works from this period were published without
an ofﬁcial licence. Manuscript news sheets also remained an important
venue for essayistic information sharing, political commentary, and polem-
ical agitation. Letters from abroad became an important ‘bridge genre’ in the
later seventeenth century for the circulation of news and commentary,
whether in manuscript or print, and as Rachael Scarborough King has shown,
such ‘early newspapers were essentially series of epistles’.

During the licensing period, the government worked to control the spread
of news and worried that essayists and polemicists might foment dissent
using illicit periodical forms. Thanks to the Press Licensing Act, there was
only one ofﬁcial newspaper, The London Gazette, and a few newsletters.
Readers, who possessed ‘a nearly pathological interest in reading and hearing
news’, simply had few licensed print venues for information and opinion.
